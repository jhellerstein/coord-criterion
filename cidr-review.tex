\begin{abstract}
When does a distributed system intrinsically need coordination?  The
literature has lacked a formalism in which the question can be posed in
general.  CALM, CAP, and CRDTs
answer it for one class of programs, consistency models, or design patterns.  Their answers cannot
be compared or combined, and together they leave basic questions open, such as how coordination repairs inconsistency.  We put them on common
ground.  We provide a minimalist relational definition of a specification: a
table that includes, for each input, all outputs a system may produce.  Each
column carries its own partial order, saying when one input \inrel another and
when one output \outrel another.  A specification is \emph{consistent} when its
runs are confluent: runs whose inputs have a common \indesc can always catch up
to a common state.  Possibilities narrow as input grows or outputs are emitted.
An irrevocable \emph{exclusion} removes possibilities not subsumed by those
remaining; it requires coordination when another run could contradict it.

We lift CALM out of its original setting.  \emph{Functional CALM} generalizes
the CALM Theorem from Datalog queries to any function between ordered domains:
such a specification is consistent exactly when it is monotone, and then no
output requires exclusion.  \emph{Complete CALM} handles ambiguous specifications
that allow several outputs for the same input, where the test becomes
\emph{Lifted Monotonicity}: as input \ingrows, some output permitted at the
larger input \outrel whatever was emitted at the smaller one.  As a corollary,
\emph{Complete CAP} inherits the same criterion: the CAP trade-off arises
exactly when Lifted Monotonicity fails.
\emph{Proper Coordination} identifies which stage of a composition must coordinate,
and certifies that later stages need no coordination of their own.  Highly Available Transactions, Invariant
Confluence, and later refinements of CALM all map into the same framework.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Concurrent systems rely on coordination mechanisms ranging from mutexes and
atomic instructions to database locks and consensus protocols. These mechanisms prevent concurrent actions from producing undesirable
outcomes. They also bring costs: overhead, latency, and
availability in distributed settings. Avoiding unnecessary coordination
is therefore a central goal in distributed systems and databases. This paper
asks what coordination mechanisms fundamentally accomplish, and
when that effect is intrinsically required.

The CALM Conjecture
proposed a crisp, bidirectional answer: consistency can be achieved without
coordination exactly when the specification is
monotone~\cite{hellerstein2010declarative}. Ameloot et al.\ proved it in both
directions, but only for Datalog queries evaluated by relational
transducer networks~\cite{ameloot2013relational,hellerstein2020keeping}. A
closely related question appears in Brewer's CAP conjecture: which consistency
guarantees can remain available when participants cannot communicate? Gilbert and Lynch proved its impossibility result, but only for linearizable
registers~\cite{brewer2000towards,gilbert2002cap}. The apparent connection
between CALM and CAP has never been established in a common formalism. Both
results are invoked far more broadly: CALM as a general rule that monotone
programs need no coordination, CAP as a general constraint on distributed
systems. The folklore outgrew the proofs.

The need for a common criterion extends beyond CALM and CAP. CRDTs use
inflationary updates over join-semilattices for coordination-free
convergence~\cite{shapiro2011crdt}; HATs identify transactional guarantees
available under partition~\cite{bailis2013hat}; and invariant confluence asks
when independently reached database states merge safely~\cite{bailis2014coordination}. Yet these results speak different formal languages, leaving unclear whether
CALM encompasses CAP, whether CRDT join laws are necessary, why only some
transactional isolation levels remain available during partition, and whether
invariant confluence differs from CALM (\S\ref{sec:instances}).

Our answer begins with a simple observation. In systems, as in life,
possibilities narrow over time. Some are out of our hands: the world moves on,
and possibilities are lost. Others are left for us to decide, and the
consequences vary. Incidental decisions close no doors: each possibility
they remove is covered by a more informative possibility that remains. Fateful
decisions make some outcomes impossible: they exclude a possibility that
nothing remaining can cover. Which specifications can be implemented without
fateful decisions?

Our foundation is a \emph{possibility relation} $P\subseteq I\times O$, which
may be infinite, pairing inputs with permitted outputs and depicted as rows in
a table. It describes observable behavior without fixing a language,
protocol, or placement. A designer equips the input domain $I$ and output
domain $O$ with partial orders that declare the specification's semantics. On inputs, $i\InputOrd j$ means that $j$
\emph{\inrel} $i$ with more input from the environment. On outputs,
$u\OutputGe o$ means that $u$ subsumes all specification-relevant information
in $o$; equivalently, $o\OutputOrd u$, and $u$ \emph{\outrel} $o$. An \emph{exclusion} is a fateful system
decision that removes one or more rows $(i,o)$ for which no remaining row
$(i,u)$ has $o\OutputOrd u$: no remaining possibility subsumes an excluded
one.

\textcolor{newblue}{State-machine-style specifications can be projected into this form, eliding
operational details that are not externally observable (\S\ref{sec:scope}).} \textcolor{newblue}{Despite its simplicity, the model captures not only application invariants,
but also general-purpose consistency definitions such as linearizability,
causal consistency, and transactional isolation levels
(\S\ref{sec:complete-cap}, \S\ref{sec:hat-instance}, and \extended).}

A specification is \emph{consistent} when the executions it admits are
\emph{confluent}: states reached along different paths can be brought to
a common state.

Our results develop the criterion in two stages.

\paragraph{Learn and refine.}
Input growth records what a system may learn from the environment. Monotonicity under input growth requires outputs to refine as input grows. \emph{Functional
CALM} (\S\ref{sec:functional-calm-theorem}) covers specifications that permit
one output at each input. It proves that consistency is equivalent to
monotonicity: $i\InputOrd j$ implies $f(i)\OutputOrd f(j)$. If monotonicity
fails, some output requires a fateful decision: an exclusion that rules
out later input which could contradict it. The result covers relational query
languages and the usual CRDT state specification.

\paragraph{Choose and align.}
A second facet appears when the table contains several rows with the same
input and different outputs. Each row offers the system a possible output,
so different runs may choose differently, as in leader election or
transaction ordering. Monotonicity under output choice requires outputs
chosen by different runs to grow together. We call this Directed Outputs:
some larger permitted output refines them all, making those decisions
incidental.

Together, monotonicity under input growth and output choice form \emph{Lifted
Monotonicity}, our unified criterion on the possibility relation. \emph{Complete
CALM} proves that consistency is equivalent to Lifted Monotonicity
(\S\ref{sec:complete-calm-theorem}).

Distributed histories obey the same consistency criterion
(\S\ref{sec:distributed-execution}). An exclusion \emph{requires coordination}
if another run can contradict it, and \emph{distributed coordination} if that
run is at another participant. Complete CAP (\S\ref{sec:complete-cap}) says
Lifted Monotonicity exactly characterizes specifications that can keep every
permitted output available and confluent during a partition. The
Gilbert--Lynch theorem follows directly.

This paper makes five contributions:
\begin{enumerate}
\item
\emph{Ordered relational specifications.} A possibility relation over
        ordered input and output domains describes permitted behavior;
        exclusions remove possibilities.
\item
\emph{Consistency as confluence.} One definition covers general
        consistency models and application-specific requirements, and it is
        equivalent to distributed consistency.
\item
\emph{Functional CALM.} For functions between ordered domains,
        consistency is equivalent to monotonicity.
\item
\emph{Complete CALM and CAP.} In general, consistency is equivalent
        to Lifted Monotonicity, and Complete CAP gives the matching
        availability boundary under partition for any declared set of outputs
        that must stay available.
\item
\emph{Composition and Proper Coordination.} Specifications
        compose by equijoin, and we show that in a composition, upstream
        stages can free downstream stages from issuing exclusions (\S\ref{sec:composition}). \removednote{\textbf{unclassified}, 34 words, rank 46; \textit{The paper develops these contributions in order, then relates the criterion to earlier results () and ...}}
\end{enumerate}

We introduce the framework with a small election, illustrated in
Figure~\ref{fig:election-frames}. Each row of the specification table pairs
the ballots received so far with the permitted result; the column orders
say how inputs \ingrow and which later outputs \outrelbase earlier ones.

Three voters $v_1,v_2,v_3$ each cast at most one immutable ballot for $a$ or
$b$. The output is whichever candidate has two ballots, or $\bot$ while there
is no winner. An input is the ballots received so far, written as a
three-character string with a dot for a ballot not yet received. For brevity,
the figure shows only inputs \inrelpart $a\cdot\cdot$.

The two orders are where a designer makes decisions. The \emph{input order}
records accumulated ballots, one per voter: $ab\cdot$ \inrel $a\cdot\cdot$, while
$aa\cdot$ and $ab\cdot$ are incomparable. The \emph{output order} says which
announcements are safe to build on: $\bot$ claims nothing, so either winner
\outrel it, while $a$ and $b$ are incomparable. In general, the functional table is \emph{monotone}
when
\[
  i\InputOrd j\quad\Longrightarrow\quad f(i)\OutputOrd f(j).
\]
Here, for example, the input sequence $a\cdot\cdot\InputOrd ab\cdot\InputOrd aba$
monotonically produces the output sequence $\bot\OutputOrd\bot\OutputOrd a$.

\paragraph{Input and determination.}
Each panel captures the specification state: a pair of a \emph{current input}
and a \emph{remainder table} of rows
still possible from that input. \textcolor{newblue}{When $v_2$'s ballot for $b$ arrives, the input event
$\mathsf{input}(ab\cdot)$ produces panel~(b), causing the table to lose rows whose inputs no
longer \inrelbase the ballots received; such inputs are now illegal.} \textcolor{newblue}{The \emph{upper fiber} is the set of
outputs in the remaining rows at the current input and its \indescs: in panel~(b),
$\{\bot,a,b\}$.} \textcolor{newblue}{If $v_2$ instead votes for $a$ (panel~(c)), the upper fiber is $\{a\}$.} \textcolor{newblue}{The state
is \emph{determined}: input alone resolved the outcome, without exclusion.}

Panel~(d), by contrast, involves the \emph{system}: the implementation that
receives the ballots and announces the result
(\S\ref{sec:systems}). In this example, the system acts as a registrar, emitting $\bot$
and closing the polls at
$ab\cdot$ in the same output event. Its irrevocable \emph{exclusion}
removes the still-possible $aba$ and $abb$ rows but leaves the current input
unchanged, and so makes $\bot$ the final result. Emitting $\bot$ by itself
would need no exclusion, since every output still possible \outrel it;
closing the polls is a further decision by the system, not something the
specification requires.

\paragraph{Output and consistency.}
In a functional table, an output $o$ is a \emph{legal output} when every
output that could occur in future \outrel it---the entire upper fiber of the
current input. Hence $\bot$ is legal in panels~(a) and~(b), and $a$ in panel~(c). The atomic output event
$\mathsf{output}(o)$ empties any legal input whose output does not \outrelbase
$o$, then emits $o$, in one transition. Emitting $o$ is a promise:
from then on, only rows whose outputs \outrelbase $o$ remain. The emission
matters because the environment is powerful and unpredictable; it may use $o$
when choosing later input.

Figure~\ref{fig:nonmonotone-frame} considers a variant of our example that
changes only the output order: all outputs are incomparable, including $\bot$
for no winner (the \emph{equality order}, under which each element is related only
to itself). Then $\bot$ is incompatible with future winners, so emitting it
requires an exclusion. That change in the output order is Functional CALM in
miniature: the original order is monotone, so later outputs \outrelbase every
earlier one; under the equality order, some outputs can be emitted
only with an exclusion.

The obstruction is a property solely of the table and its orders. Rows $(ab\cdot,\bot)$ and
$(aba,a)$ have the common input \indesc $aba$, but under the equality
order no output at $aba$ \outrel both outputs $\bot$ and $a$. A run that emits
$\bot$ at $ab\cdot$ and one that receives $aba$ cannot be reconciled at $aba$.

Input and output events form a transition system, and the specification is
consistent when its runs are confluent. \removednote{\textbf{unclassified}, 15 words, rank 72; \textit{For functional tables this corresponds exactly to monotonicity ().}} \textcolor{newblue}{Section~\ref{sec:functional-calm-theorem} shows that for functional tables
this corresponds exactly to monotonicity.}

\subsection{The framework in brief}
\label{sec:framework-brief}
\label{sec:functional-specifications}
\label{sec:functional-events}
\label{sec:executions}

\textcolor{newblue}{We now state these notions precisely.}

\begin{definition}[Functional ordered specification, total]
\label{def:functional-specification}
\textcolor{newblue}{A \emph{functional ordered specification} is a tuple
$\Spec=(I,\InputOrd,O,\OutputOrd,P)$,
where $(I,\InputOrd)$ and $(O,\OutputOrd)$ are partially ordered input and
output types, and $P\subseteq I\times O$ is its \emph{possibility relation}:
a typed relation with schema $(\mathrm{in},\mathrm{out})$ in which every
input appears in at least one row ($P$ is \emph{total} on $I$) and at most one
(a functional dependency).} \textcolor{newblue}{The unique output paired with $i$ induces a
function $f:I\to O$.} \removednote{\textbf{unclassified}, 8 words, rank 88; \textit{with [math] We call [math] the input order .}}
\end{definition}

\textcolor{newblue}{Because an output may depend on everything a system has received, we model
inputs as \emph{cumulative}: $i\InputOrd j$ means that $j$ contains at least
the input information in $i$.} \textcolor{newblue}{We say that $j$ \emph{\inrel} $i$, or is an
\emph{\indesc} of $i$.} We call $\OutputOrd$ the \emph{output order}:
$o\OutputOrd u$ means that $u$ preserves every externally visible claim in $o$
and may add more, and we say that $u$ \emph{\outrel} $o$. \removednote{\textbf{formal definition/notation}, 24 words, rank 57; \textit{We use these two verbs only in these senses: inputs , and outputs are .}} \textcolor{newblue}{A \emph{common
\indesc} of inputs is an input that \inrel each of them, and a \emph{common
\outnoun} of outputs is an output that \outrel each of them.} Neither need be
unique or least: for example, two independent transactions $u$ and $v$, with schedules

\end{proposition}

\end{proof}

\subsection{The Functional CALM theorem}
\label{sec:functional-calm-theorem}

Consistency is defined by a specification's executions, monotonicity by its
table and orders alone. Functional CALM shows that these coincide. \textcolor{newblue}{Full proofs are in \extended.}

\begin{theorem}[Functional CALM]
\label{thm:functional-calm}
A functional ordered specification is consistent if and only if it is
monotone.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
A monotone $f$ only emits outputs that all of its future outputs \outrelbase,
so the row $(j,f(j))$ at a common \indesc $j$ survives in every run. If $f$ is not monotone, a run that emits $f(i)$ and a run that accepts an
\indesc $k$ of $i$ with $f(i)\not\OutputOrd f(k)$ can never be reconciled at $k$. \removednote{\textbf{explanation}, 10 words, rank 86; \textit{Full proof in Appendix .}} \textcolor{newblue}{\fullproof}
\end{proof}

In the equality-order election, $f(ab\cdot)=\bot$ and $f(aba)=a$ are
incomparable: a run that emits $\bot$ at $ab\cdot$ and one that accepts
$aba$ are the two runs of Section~\ref{sec:election-story} that can never be reconciled. \removednote{\textbf{explanation}, 21 words, rank 63; \textit{In the functional case, consistency has a strong consequence: emitting outputs never changes the state, which ...}}

\begin{corollary}[State is fixed by input]
\label{cor:state-by-input}
In a consistent functional specification, every run ending at current
input $i$ is in the canonical state $\sigma_P(i)$, whatever outputs it emitted
along the way. \removednote{\textbf{explanation}, 53 words, rank 30; \textit{By monotonicity, the outputs a run emits remove no row at an of [math].}}
\end{corollary}
\textcolor{newblue}{\proofinfull}

\textcolor{newblue}{Knowing the input is therefore enough to know the state, and with it the one
output.} Monotonicity also governs legality:

\begin{proposition}[Output-legality boundary]
\label{prop:functional-exposure-boundary}
A functional ordered specification is monotone if and only if every output at
every state reachable without exclusion is legal.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
By Corollary~\ref{cor:state-by-input}, monotone $f$ reaches only canonical
states. \textcolor{newblue}{At $\sigma_P(i)$, $f(i)$ is legal exactly
when $f$ is monotone at $i$.} \removednote{\textbf{explanation}, 4 words, rank 92; \textit{Full proof in Appendix .}} \textcolor{newblue}{\fullproof}
\end{proof}

A monotone specification can thus emit every current
output without exclusion; if monotonicity fails at $i\InputOrd k$ with
$f(i)\not\OutputOrd f(k)$, then at $\sigma_P(i)$ the event $\mathsf{output}(f(i))$
applies $x_{f(i)}$, which excludes input $k$.

\textcolor{newblue}{This is the familiar CALM dichotomy stated at the level of the specification
rather than inside a particular programming language.} The C in CALM has been read
both as consistency and as confluence; Definition~\ref{def:consistency} equates them. Classical CALM fits the pattern with inputs $J$ as sets of facts ordered by
inclusion and output tables $Q(J)$ ordered by inclusion. \textcolor{newblue}{Functional
monotonicity becomes $J\subseteq J'\Rightarrow Q(J)\subseteq Q(J')$,
the standard definition of a monotone query~\cite{abiteboul1995foundations},
which positive Datalog satisfies and negation may not.} \removednote{\textbf{formal definition/notation}, 0 words, rank 103; labels eq:query-monotonicity} Functional CALM
generalizes the statement to arbitrary input and
output orders, whatever formalism expresses the specification. CRDT state specifications have the same functional shape,
with received payloads ordered by inclusion and states by the semilattice
order~\cite{shapiro2011crdt}; Sections~\ref{sec:query-instance}
and~\ref{sec:crdt-instance} treat both.

\paragraph{Exclusion and directedness.}
The examples differ in what happens
to alternative outputs when one is emitted. \textcolor{newblue}{In panel~(f), neither
serialization order \outrel the other, so emitting one requires an
\emph{exclusion} that removes the other while the input stays legal:
emitting $[A,B]$ excludes $[B,A]$.} In panel~(g), larger route sets \outrelbase both the
emitted observation and the outputs of the rows it removes. We call such
removal an \emph{output subsumption}; every removed output is
\outrelpast by a remaining one, so emitting the route observation loses no information
according to the output order and needs no
exclusion: it is a \emph{legal output}.

An output event can thus remove rows in two ways: by exclusion, as in panel~(f) and Section~\ref{sec:functional-calm}, or by subsuming outputs, as in panel~(g). At a given input, an exclusion may remove every row, excluding the input, or only some rows, excluding those outputs. In Figure~\ref{fig:ambiguity-frames}, serialization requires exclusion; traceroute avoids it. The difference between them is the order-theoretic concept of a \emph{directed} fiber:
every finite set of outputs in a given fiber has a common \outnoun in that fiber. Directedness at a single input is not sufficient, however;
we must also \outrelbase prior outputs as input \ingrows. \emph{Lifted Monotonicity}
combines the requirements: as input \ingrows from $i$ to $j$, some output
in the fiber at $j$ \outrel whatever outputs runs emit at $i$. Equivalently,
outputs emitted at inputs with a common \indesc $j$ must have a common
\outnoun in the fiber at $j$: the same test as in Section~\ref{sec:election-story}, but
with many outputs per input. Two transaction scheduling runs that emit $[A,B]$ and $[B,A]$ fail it;
two traceroute runs that emit different route sets pass it, since their union
survives in both.

\subsection{Lifted Monotonicity}
\label{sec:relational-specifications}
\label{sec:lifted-monotonicity}

We begin with our most general definition of a specification, which admits
ambiguity of output.
\begin{definition}[Ordered relational specification, ambiguity, ordered specification]
\label{def:relational-specification}
\textcolor{newblue}{An \emph{ordered relational specification} is
$\Spec=(I,\InputOrd,O,\OutputOrd,P)$ with $P\subseteq I\times O$,
where $(I,\InputOrd)$ and $(O,\OutputOrd)$ are partially ordered sets.} \removednote{\textbf{formal definition/notation}, 45 words, rank 34; \textit{where [math] and [math] are partially ordered sets and [math] is its possibility relation .}; labels eq:relational-specification} \textcolor{newblue}{The
relation $P$ is the specification's \emph{possibility relation} and is total
on $I$.} A row $(i,o)\in P$ is a possible input/output pair: output $o$ is
permitted at input $i$. \textcolor{newblue}{The fiber
$\fiber{i}=\{o\in O:(i,o)\in P\}$ may now contain several
outputs; the specification is \emph{ambiguous} when some fiber does.} \removednote{\textbf{formal definition/notation}, 7 words, rank 89; \textit{of Section may now contain several outputs.}; labels eq:fiber} A
functional specification is the special case $\fiber{i}=\{f(i)\}$. \removednote{\textbf{unclassified}, 14 words, rank 74; \textit{An ordered relational specification is ambiguous when some fiber contains more than one output.}; labels def:ambiguous} We call
either kind simply an \emph{ordered specification}. \removednote{\textbf{unclassified}, 12 words, rank 81; \textit{Specification states and input events ( and ) apply unchanged to relational tables.}} \removednote{\textbf{unclassified}, 12 words, rank 82; \textit{As input from [math] to [math], two things can go wrong.}}
\end{definition}

\textcolor{newblue}{Specification states and input events
(\S\ref{sec:framework-brief})
apply unchanged to relational tables; Section~\ref{sec:lifted-output-events} lifts
output events.}

\textcolor{newblue}{As input
\ingrows from $i$ to $j$, can some output in the fiber at $j$ \outrelbase
whatever outputs runs emit at $i$?} \textcolor{newblue}{Two things can go wrong.} A
\emph{stranded output} is an output in the fiber at $i$ with no \outnoun in
the fiber at some \indesc $j$. An \emph{incompatible pair} is two outputs in
the fiber at $i$ with no common \outnoun there. We rule out
each, combine them, and restate the result in the form runs need.

First, we rule out stranded outputs.

\begin{definition}[Relational Monotonicity]
\label{def:relational-monotonicity}
An ordered relational specification satisfies \emph{Relational Monotonicity}
when every output keeps some \outnoun as input \ingrows:
        \begin{equation}
  i\InputOrd j,\ o\in\fiber{i}
  \quad\Longrightarrow\quad
  \exists u\in\fiber{j}:\ o\OutputOrd u.
  \label{eq:relational-monotonicity}
        \end{equation}
\end{definition}

For singleton fibers this is functional monotonicity,
Equation~\ref{eq:functional-monotonicity}. It treats each output separately,
so it does not rule out incompatible pairs, which concern several outputs in
one fiber. For a set $F\subseteq O$ of outputs, outputs $o_1,\ldots,o_k$ are
\emph{compatible in $F$} if some $u\in F$ \outrel all of them, and
\emph{incompatible in $F$} otherwise.

Second, we rule out incompatible pairs.

\begin{definition}[Directed fiber, Directed Outputs]
\label{def:fiber-directedness}
A fiber $F\subseteq O$ is \emph{directed} when every nonempty finite
$X\subseteq F$ is compatible in $F$. \removednote{\textbf{unclassified}, 0 words, rank 104; labels def:directed-outputs} An ordered relational specification has
\emph{Directed Outputs} when every fiber $\fiber{i}$ is directed.
\end{definition}

A singleton fiber is trivially directed. \textcolor{newblue}{As with common \indescs of inputs, a
common \outnoun need not be
least: directedness requires some upper bound within the fiber for each finite
set, not a least one or a fixed merge operator, so it is weaker than a
join-semilattice.} \removednote{\textbf{example}, 53 words, rank 31; \textit{For example, the fiber [math] under inclusion is directed, since [math] every member, yet [math] and ...}} For example, the fiber
\[
  \{\{a\},\{b\},\{a,b,c\},\{a,b,d\},\{a,b,c,d\}\}
\]
under inclusion is directed, since $\{a,b,c,d\}$ \outrel every member, yet $\{a\}$ and $\{b\}$ have two
incomparable minimal common \outnouns. Thus
the traceroute fiber is directed: the union of finitely many route sets in
it is again in the fiber and refines each. The two transaction orders in
Figure~\ref{fig:ambiguity-frames}(f), by contrast, are incompatible.

\paragraph{Lifted Monotonicity.}
To state both requirements at once, lift $\OutputOrd$ to sets. For nonempty
$A,B\subseteq O$, define
        \begin{equation}
  A\LiftedOrd B
  \;\Longleftrightarrow\;
  \text{every nonempty finite }X\subseteq A\text{ is compatible in }B,
  \label{eq:joint-output-order}
        \end{equation}
the \emph{joint lift} of $\OutputOrd$. We apply it with $A=\fiber{i}$ and
$B=\fiber{j}$ for $i\InputOrd j$. When $X$ is a single output $o$, it asks
that $o$ have a \outnoun in $\fiber{j}$, which rules out stranded outputs. When $X$
holds several outputs, it asks that they have a common \outnoun in $\fiber{j}$; with
$j=i$, this rules out incompatible pairs.

\begin{definition}[Lifted Monotonicity, monotone specification]
\label{def:lifted-monotonicity}
An ordered relational specification satisfies \emph{Lifted Monotonicity}, and
is called \emph{monotone}, when the mapping $i\mapsto\fiber{i}$ is monotone
under the joint lift:
        \begin{equation}
  i\InputOrd j
  \quad\Longrightarrow\quad
  \fiber{i}\LiftedOrd\fiber{j}.
  \label{eq:lifted-monotonicity}
        \end{equation}
\end{definition}

With singleton fibers it is functional monotonicity
(Definition~\ref{def:functional-monotonicity}). \removednote{\textbf{unclassified}, 121 words, rank 13; \textit{As the reading above shows, it implies Relational Monotonicity and Directed Outputs; conversely, for [math] and ...}} \textcolor{newblue}{Lifted
Monotonicity holds exactly when Relational Monotonicity and Directed Outputs
both do.}

\paragraph{The common-\indesc condition.}
Lifted Monotonicity compares an input with its
own expansions. Runs may emit outputs at incomparable inputs, say
$o_1$ at $i_1$ and $o_2$ at $i_2$, and can be reconciled only at a common
\indesc $j$ of both. The next condition asks exactly that: a common
\outnoun of $o_1$ and $o_2$ in the fiber at $j$.

\begin{definition}[Common-\indesc condition]
\label{def:common-expansion-condition}
An ordered specification satisfies the \emph{common-\indesc condition} when,
for every finite set of rows $(i_1,o_1),\ldots,(i_k,o_k)$ of $P$ and every
common \indesc $j$ of $i_1,\ldots,i_k$, some $u\in P(j)$ \outrel every
$o_r$.
\end{definition}

For the empty set of rows the condition asks only that $P(j)$ be nonempty, which
follows from $P$ being total on $I$.

\begin{proposition}[Common-\indesc form of Lifted Monotonicity]
\label{prop:common-expansion-form}
An ordered relational specification satisfies Lifted Monotonicity if and only
if it satisfies the common-\indesc condition.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
Relational Monotonicity carries each $o_r$ to some
\outnoun in the fiber at $j$, and Directed Outputs gives those a common
\outnoun there. \removednote{\textbf{signposting}, 4 words, rank 93; \textit{Full proof in Appendix .}} \textcolor{newblue}{\fullproof}
\end{proof}

\subsection{Lifted output events}
\label{sec:lifted-output-events}

\textcolor{newblue}{Lifted Monotonicity is a property of the possibility relation and its orders.} \textcolor{newblue}{We now return to execution.} For a state $(i,R)$ and $o\in R(i)$, the output event $\mathsf{output}(o)$
again keeps exactly the rows whose outputs \outrelbase $o$, ending at
$(i,R[\mathrm{out}\OutputGe o])$ as in Equation~\ref{eq:output-action}, and
emits $o$. Its removed rows fall into two classes. A row is in
$\Subsumed_o$ when a kept row at the same input \outrel it; its removal is an
\emph{output subsumption}. Every other removed row is in $\Excluded_o$ and is
removed by the canonical exclusion $x_o$. At an input $k$, $x_o$ excludes
input $k$ if it removes every row there; otherwise it excludes each output
whose row it removes at $k$. Output subsumption is not exclusion. \textcolor{newblue}{Emitting $\bot$ under the equality order of Figure~\ref{fig:nonmonotone-frame}
puts the rows for $aba$ and $abb$ in $\Excluded_o$ and excludes those inputs;
emitting $[A,B]$ in Figure~\ref{fig:ambiguity-frames}(f) puts the row for
$[B,A]$ in $\Excluded_o$ and excludes that output at $\{A,B\}$; and the
route sets removed in panel~(g) are in $\Subsumed_o$.} \removednote{\textbf{unclassified}, 20 words, rank 64; \textit{In the functional case, [math] is empty, so this is the output event of Definition .}} \textcolor{newblue}{In the functional
case, $\Subsumed_o$ is empty.} \textcolor{newblue}{Formal definitions are in \extended.}

As before, $o$ is a legal output when $\mathsf{output}(o)$ applies no
exclusion, exactly when $\Excluded_o$ is empty. \textcolor{newblue}{Equivalently, at every legal \indesc $j$ of $i$, $o$ and each
remaining output at $j$ have a common \outnoun at $j$.}

\subsection{The Complete CALM theorem}
\label{sec:complete-calm-theorem}

A lifted output event ends at $(i,R[\mathrm{out}\OutputGe o])$, as in
Equation~\ref{eq:output-action}. \textcolor{newblue}{States, input events, runs,
exclusion-free runs, and consistency are defined as before, and the results
of Section~\ref{sec:executions} used output events only through that
equation.} They lift unchanged: the rows remaining after a run
(Lemma~\ref{lem:run-residual}) and the survivor test
(Proposition~\ref{prop:confluence}). Thus consistency
reduces to Lifted Monotonicity.

\begin{theorem}[Complete CALM]
\label{thm:complete-calm}
An ordered specification is consistent if and only if it satisfies Lifted
Monotonicity, equivalently the common-\indesc condition.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
If the common-\indesc condition holds, a common \outnoun $u\in P(j)$
of everything emitted survives in every run. If outputs emitted at inputs with a common \indesc $j$ are incompatible in
$P(j)$, the runs that emit them share no row at $j$ and can never be
reconciled there. \removednote{\textbf{signposting}, 16 words, rank 70; \textit{Full proof in Appendix .}} \textcolor{newblue}{\fullproof}
\end{proof}

The name CALM, for Consistency As Logical Monotonicity, tied the idea to a
logic language. The new, more general expansion is Consistency As Lifted Monotonicity. The condition that characterizes consistency is now a
property of a specification: Lifted Monotonicity, which for a function is
ordinary monotonicity. \textcolor{newblue}{Section~\ref{sec:distributed-consistency} shows that
both theorems hold for distributed histories.}

The same condition governs legality.

\begin{proposition}[Output-legality boundary]
\label{prop:exposure-boundary}
An ordered specification satisfies Lifted Monotonicity if and only
if every output at every state reachable without exclusion is legal.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
Under the common-\indesc condition, consistency supplies the common \outnoun
the legal-output test asks for. Conversely, the legal-output test at canonical
states, one row at a time, builds the common \outnoun the condition
asks for. \removednote{\textbf{signposting}, 4 words, rank 94; \textit{Full proof in Appendix .}} \textcolor{newblue}{\fullproof}
\end{proof}

For functions, both are monotonicity, so Complete CALM and
Proposition~\ref{prop:exposure-boundary} reduce to Functional CALM and
Proposition~\ref{prop:functional-exposure-boundary}.

\subsection{Coordination}
\label{sec:coordination}

An exclusion creates a coordination problem only if another run can exercise
behavior that it removes.

\textcolor{newblue}{Let an event of a run apply an exclusion.} A run \emph{contradicts} the

an output whose row the exclusion removed. \removednote{\textbf{unclassified}, 39 words, rank 45; \textit{We judge coordination against a specified set of runs [math].}}
\end{definition}

\textcolor{newblue}{We judge coordination against a specified set of runs $\mathcal R$: the
canonical runs of a specification, or the runs a system can perform under any
environment inputs.}

\begin{definition}[Coordination-requiring exclusion, distributed coordination]
\label{def:coordination}
An exclusion \emph{requires coordination} relative to $\mathcal R$ when some
run in $\mathcal R$ contradicts it

\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Coordination-free]
\label{def:coordination-free}
A run or history is \emph{coordination-free} relative to $\mathcal R$ when it

\end{definition}

\emph{Coordination} is an operational mechanism that prevents one run from
contradicting an exclusion applied by another. \textcolor{newblue}{The definitions identify the

mechanism need not apply an exclusion: closing the polls after every voter has

\begin{proposition}[Conflicting events cannot be reconciled]
\label{thm:exclusions-require-coordination}
From a state $(i,R)$, let one run perform an output or exclusion event after which no
output at $i$ \outrel $x$, and let another run perform an output event that
emits $x$. The two resulting states cannot be brought to a common state with
current input $i$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
\textcolor{newblue}{After the second event every output at $i$ \outrel $x$, and after
the first none does; since events never add rows, no row at $i$ can be common
to both.} \removednote{\textbf{signposting}, 4 words, rank 96; \textit{Full proof in Appendix .}} \textcolor{newblue}{\fullproof}
\end{proof}

The proposition rules out reconciliation at $i$, not forever. For example,
suppose $i\InputOrd j$, $\fiber{i}=\{o,x\}$ with $o$ and $x$ incomparable, and
$\fiber{j}=\{u\}$ with $u$ \outrelpart both. The states that emit $o$ and $x$
cannot be reconciled at $i$, although accepting $j$ reconciles them. As
explained after Definition~\ref{def:consistency}, consistency nevertheless
counts the conflict at $i$ as a failure.

The proposition has an operational consequence: a system must prevent the
row-removing event and the contradictory emission of $x$ from occurring in
separate runs. This requires distributed coordination when those runs can
occur at different participants.

\subsection{The Complete CAP Theorem}
\label{sec:complete-cap}

Brewer conjectured that a distributed system cannot be consistent, available,
and partition tolerant at once~\cite{brewer2000towards}; Gilbert and Lynch
proved it for a linearizable register~\cite{gilbert2002cap}. Their theorem
concerns one guarantee, not which guarantees can stay available and
consistent through a partition. Complete CAP answers that broader question for any declared guarantee. A
guarantee is encoded as an ordered specification together with a designated
subset $E$ of its rows: those whose outputs must stay available. This
separates what participants must be able to emit from what may be observed. Outputs of $E$ are \emph{explicit}: some participant must be able to emit them
on its own. The other permitted outputs are \emph{implicit}: none need ever be
emitted, but an observer outside the system may collect outputs from every
side, and the specification decides whether that collection is permitted. Complete CAP says that such a guarantee can be
kept available and consistent through partitions exactly when the
specification satisfies Lifted Monotonicity for $E$: explicit outputs emitted
on different sides of a partition must have a common \outnoun in the
specification at every common \indesc of their inputs. When $E$ is every permitted row, this is Lifted
Monotonicity itself. A partition does not
change which exclusions a specification needs; it removes the ability to make
them jointly, since separated participants cannot jointly apply an exclusion that another participant could contradict.

\begin{definition}[Partitioned history]
\label{def:partitioned-history}
A \emph{partitioned history} divides the participants into two or more
blocks and contains no receive whose matching send lies in another
block; participants still receive environment input and emit outputs.
\end{definition}

\textcolor{newblue}{We fix a system, with a strategy for each participant of every finite
participant set and every choice of initial inputs, each depending only on
local history, and an adversarial environment that may supply
any legal input event to any participant.} \removednote{\textbf{unclassified}, 0 words, rank 105; labels def:local-continuation}

\begin{definition}[Explicit relation, Availability]
\label{def:partition-notions}
A continuation of a partitioned history is \emph{local to} a participant $p$
when the system produces it, it adds only history events of $p$'s block and no
input events, and none of its receives takes a message sent from another
block. \removednote{\textbf{unclassified}, 0 words, rank 106; labels def:explicit-relation} An \emph{explicit relation} $E\subseteq P$ is a set of rows
$(i,o)$ whose output $o$ must stay available at input $i$; write $E(i)$ for
its fiber $\{o:(i,o)\in E\}$. The system has \emph{Availability for $E$}
when, in every partitioned history it produces, each participant $p$, ending
in state $(i,R)$, can emit each output in $R(i)\cap E(i)$ in some continuation
local to $p$. \emph{Availability} is Availability for $P$.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Partition Consistency]
\label{def:partition-consistency}
The system has \emph{Partition Consistency} when the final cut of every
partitioned history it produces is confluent at every common \indesc of the
participants' current inputs. The continuation that confluence asks for need
not stay partitioned and need not be produced by the system.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Lifted Monotonicity for $E$]
\label{def:lm-for-A}
$\Spec$ satisfies \emph{Lifted Monotonicity for $E$} when every finite set of rows of $E$ with
a common \indesc $j$ has a common \outnoun in $\fiber{j}$.
\end{definition}

With $E=P$, Lifted Monotonicity for $E$ is the common-\indesc condition
(Definition~\ref{def:common-expansion-condition}). \removednote{\textbf{unclassified}, 26 words, rank 53; \textit{Unlike Availability, Availability for a smaller [math] can be met by a system whose output events ...}}

\begin{theorem}[Complete CAP]
\label{thm:complete-cap}
For an ordered specification $\Spec$ and an explicit relation
$E\subseteq P$, the following are equivalent:
\begin{enumerate}
\item
some system has Availability for $E$ and Partition Consistency;
\item
$\Spec$ satisfies Lifted Monotonicity for $E$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
Consider the system whose strategies emit each output of
$E$ by the canonical output event of Section~\ref{sec:complete-calm}. It has
Availability for $E$, and Lifted Monotonicity for $E$ gives the outputs it emits a
common \outnoun at every common \indesc of their inputs. Conversely, given one
participant per row of $E$, each in its own block, Availability lets each
emit its row's output, and Partition Consistency then supplies the common

\paragraph{Gilbert and Lynch.}
Their theorem is a case of Complete CAP. Consider a register specification

precedes the read, every output in the fiber answers that same read with $v$. That contradicts

there \outrel $v_0$, and $\Spec$ does not satisfy Lifted Monotonicity. By Complete CAP, then, no system keeps both outputs available and

\section{Composition and Proper Coordination}
\label{sec:composition}

Coordination poses a puzzle. A non-monotone program needs coordination, yet
coordination is itself non-monotone. A key-value store with mutable keys is
non-monotone~\cite{alvaro2011bloom}. State-machine
replication~\cite{schneider1990smr} wraps it in a log. Clients send requests,
the log puts the requested commands in one order, and the key-value store
executes the log and answers each request. The log's specification is also not
monotone: when two commands arrive concurrently, either may be appended next,
and the two alternative orders have no common \outnoun. Yet replicated stores are
consistent, coordinating only in the log. \textcolor{newblue}{Multi-Paxos agrees on each
log entry~\cite{lamport2001paxos}, and the replicas then execute the log
with no further agreement.}

That puzzle is explained by our framework. Specifications are
tables, so they compose by equijoin, and composition preserves Lifted
Monotonicity. Because exclusions are explicit, we
can also ask which component makes a composition's exclusions. When the
upstream component makes every exclusion the composition needs, and the
downstream needs none on the inputs the upstream offers, we call the
coordination \emph{proper}
(\S\ref{sec:proper-coordination}). \removednote{\textbf{signposting}, 4 words, rank 98; \textit{Proofs are in Appendix .}}
\label{sec:sequential-composition}

Intuitively, sequential composition feeds the output of one specification to
the input of the next. The composite is again a table: it pairs an input of
the first specification with every output that the second permits for some
intermediate value that the first permits. Formally, let
$\Spec_1=(I,\InputOrd,M,\preceq_M,P_1)$ and
$\Spec_2=(M,\preceq_M,O,\OutputOrd,P_2)$ be ordered relational specifications
sharing the ordered domain $(M,\preceq_M)$. Their \emph{sequential
composition} has table
\begin{equation}
  P_2\circ P_1
  =\pi_{I,O}(P_1\Join_M P_2)
  =\{(i,o):\exists m.\ (i,m)\in P_1\land(m,o)\in P_2\},
  \label{eq:spec-composition}
\end{equation}
where $\pi$ and $\Join$ are relational projection and equijoin. \textcolor{newblue}{Since $P_1$
is total on $I$ and $P_2$ is total on $M$, every $i\in I$ has a nonempty fiber
in $P_2\circ P_1$.} When the two stages
run as a pipeline, $\Spec_2$ takes the outputs of $\Spec_1$ as its inputs, and
we say that $\Spec_1$ \emph{guards} $\Spec_2$. \removednote{\textbf{signposting}, 22 words, rank 60; \textit{Appendix gives each stage an input of its own as well, as when a store serves ...}}

\begin{lemma}[Composition]
\label{lem:composition}
If $\Spec_1$ and $\Spec_2$ satisfy Lifted Monotonicity, then so does the
ordered specification with table $P_2\circ P_1$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
Take rows of $P_2\circ P_1$ whose inputs have a common \indesc $j$. The
common-\indesc condition for $\Spec_1$ gives an intermediate value at $j$
that \outrel each row's, and that value is a common \indesc
at which the condition for $\Spec_2$ supplies the common \outnoun. \removednote{\textbf{signposting}, 4 words, rank 99; \textit{Full proof in Appendix .}} \textcolor{newblue}{\fullproof}
\end{proof}

By associativity the lemma extends to finite pipelines. \removednote{\textbf{formal definition/notation}, 16 words, rank 71; \textit{For a function [math], its table [math] satisfies Lifted Monotonicity exactly when [math] is monotone ().}} \removednote{\textbf{explanation}, 29 words, rank 50; \textit{The composite is itself an ordered specification.}}

\subsection{Composite CALM}
\label{sec:composite-calm}

\textcolor{newblue}{By Complete CALM, $P_2\circ P_1$ is consistent exactly when it
satisfies Lifted Monotonicity, and then no output needs an exclusion.} That test treats the composite as a single specification. Suppose instead that
each stage runs on its own, with its runs taking as input the outputs of the
stage before. A stage may then need an exclusion even when the composite
needs none. \textcolor{newblue}{A composite can be jointly monotone, with no stage needing a
exclusion, in two ways.} \removednote{\textbf{unclassified}, 360 words, rank 3; \textit{The orders on [math] and [math] are part of the composite's contract with its environment.}; labels prop:joint-monotone} \textcolor{newblue}{First, if every stage is monotone,
as when a monotone client query observes CRDT state
(\S\ref{sec:crdt-instance}), then Lemma~\ref{lem:composition} applies.} \textcolor{newblue}{Second, the upstream can filter out the inputs on which the downstream is not
monotone: a counter capped at $9$ never produces the counts at which a test

(see \extended).} \textcolor{newblue}{When none applies, some stage may have to issue an exclusion even though the
composite is monotone.} \removednote{\textbf{signposting}, 12 words, rank 83; \textit{The replicated store of the opening extends the filtering case of Proposition (2).}}

\subsection{Proper Coordination}
\label{sec:proper-coordination}

\textcolor{newblue}{The replicated store of the opening extends the filtering case.} The
key-value store is not monotone
on its own, but it is monotone on what the log offers: a log fixes the order
of commands, and a longer log only extends the responses. Unlike the
filtering case, the upstream must issue an exclusion to offer only such inputs.

Let $\Spec_1$, which guards $\Spec_2$, be run by a system with any basis, and
let $\Spec_2$ run under a system of its own. Write $\Spec_2|_{\Spec_1}$ for $\Spec_2$ restricted to the outputs of
$\Spec_1$.

\begin{definition}[Proper Coordination]
\label{def:proper-coordination}
A pipeline in which $\Spec_1$ guards $\Spec_2$ is \emph{proper} when
$\Spec_2|_{\Spec_1}$ needs no exclusion: every output at every state it
reaches without exclusion is legal.
\end{definition}

\begin{theorem}[Proper Coordination]
\label{thm:proper-coordination}
Fix any system for $\Spec_1$.
\begin{enumerate}
\item
\textcolor{newblue}{The pipeline is proper exactly when $\Spec_2|_{\Spec_1}$ satisfies
        Lifted Monotonicity.}
\item
If it is proper, $\Spec_2|_{\Spec_1}$ under the empty basis follows
        every run of $\Spec_1$. The result is a run of $P_2\circ P_1$, and
        whenever runs of $\Spec_1$ can be brought to a common state, the
        composite runs are confluent.
\item
If it is proper and $P_2\circ P_1$ violates Lifted Monotonicity,
        $\Spec_1$ cannot emit some output without an exclusion.
\end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
(1) is Complete CALM and the output-legality boundary
(Theorem~\ref{thm:complete-calm}, Proposition~\ref{prop:exposure-boundary})
applied to $\Spec_2|_{\Spec_1}$. (2) Each output event of $\Spec_1$ keeps
only rows above its output, so each input $\Spec_2$ receives lies above the
previous one, and by (1) every answer is legal. (3) The output-legality boundary
of the composite fails at a canonical state, which is the image of a canonical
state of $\Spec_1$. \removednote{\textbf{signposting}, 4 words, rank 100; \textit{Full proof in Appendix .}} \textcolor{newblue}{\fullproof}
\end{proof}

\textcolor{newblue}{The theorem holds whatever basis $\Spec_1$ uses, and a proper pipeline is
itself a guard for a further stage.} Locks in
the key-value store would be pure overhead: by (1), the store restricted to
logs needs no exclusion. \removednote{\textbf{unclassified}, 180 words, rank 6; \textit{We call a system for one specification, viewed through a chosen set of sound boundary observations, ...}} \textcolor{newblue}{The full paper extends these results to more complex
compositions than sequential stages, including
stages with inputs of their own and arbitrary acyclic graphs of stages, checked node
by node much as types are~\cite{hellerstein2026completecalmfull}.}

\subsection{Operational models and scope}
\label{sec:scope}

Automata are a traditional way to model operational behavior

mapping is straightforward: for each input, record the outputs of the runs that
consume it. \textcolor{newblue}{Two standard approaches then apply, both used widely and with
success.} \textcolor{newblue}{Model checkers explore runs on bounded

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

The computing literature is rich with coordination mechanisms for managing
concurrency within and across machines. The CALM Conjecture asked a question
still latent at the time: when are these mechanisms required, and why? It drew
on declarative networking~\cite{loo2009declarative}, which, in Codd's tradition
of separating specification from implementation~\cite{codd1970relational}, wrote
the desired effects of protocols as queries to be analyzed and optimized into a
variety of mechanisms. Could a signature property of declarative
languages---monotonicity---be the criterion?

The intuition was sharpened by a related friction in the literature on parallel
query processing. The Google MapReduce
paper~\cite{dean2004mapreduce} departed from prior work on parallel query
processing~\cite{wilschut1991dataflow,graefe1990volcano}, conflating data
redistribution (shuffle) with barrier coordination required for a non-monotone
reduce operator. This coupling of unrelated concerns required monotone operators
like join to block in the MapReduce model unnecessarily. Cost models that count
rounds, from BSP supersteps to MapReduce rounds, carry the same
conflation~\cite{valiant1990bsp,karloff2010mrc}.

The CALM Conjecture hypothesized that the database community's more judicious
use of barriers could be generalized: coordination---of any kind---is required
if and only if the program is non-monotone. Ameloot et al.\
proved it, but only for relational languages, which to date have
still not become mainstream beyond query processing (more on this below). \removednote{\textbf{unclassified}, 25 words, rank 56; \textit{This paper moves the declarations one level higher, from queries to specifications: all that matters is ...}}

\textcolor{newblue}{This paper moves the declarations one level higher, from queries to views: all
that matters about a specification is the single view mapping a program's inputs
to its output.} Eliding the query logic frees the analysis from any particular
language and broadens what can be specified. Prior work focused on implementing
complex programs
declaratively~\cite{loo2005overlays,loo2009declarative,chu2007declarative,alvaro2011bloom}. This was interesting ``declarative engineering'' with concrete benefits in
analysis and optimization. But the higher-level model here also captures abstract
families of guarantees, like CAP and isolation levels, which have no application or
protocol in mind.

Lifting the abstraction improves the power of analysis as well. Prior program
analyses became enmeshed in the details of implementations, however
declarative. Bloom~\cite{alvaro2011bloom} flagged syntactic non-monotonicity in
programs and conservatively injected coordination code in front of it. Yet
coordination code is itself non-monotone, so adding it to a Bloom program only
adds more non-monotone syntax. Subsequent work moved the question from syntax to declared contracts and
invariants~\cite{sivaramakrishnan2015quelea,kaki2018alone}. Their tools pick,
for each operation or transaction, the weakest consistency or isolation level
that the store offers and that still satisfies the declared properties. We
ask what the specification itself requires, independent of any menu of
levels. \removednote{\textbf{unclassified}, 13 words, rank 78; \textit{Because the canonical exclusions are universal (), the answer holds for every mechanism.}} \textcolor{newblue}{By Canonical Run Universality (Lemma~\ref{lem:canonical-equivalent}), the answer holds for every mechanism.} Our
composition results need no program logic, and the criteria are exact on
specifications, not conservative. \removednote{\textbf{unclassified}, 54 words, rank 29; \textit{The possibility relation makes the boundary concrete.}}

A limitation of prior CALM work was that it was constructive on only one side of
its boundary. Monotone programs need no coordination; for a non-monotone
program, CALM said only that coordination was required somewhere, not what it
had to accomplish or where it belonged. Complete CALM says more. \textcolor{newblue}{A
specification that is not monotone needs specific exclusions: the exclusions
its outputs require.} Every coordination mechanism, from locks to
consensus, is a way of applying these exclusions. The same analysis says which outputs
need exclusions, and whether another participant could contradict them, so
that agreement is required and not just a local decision. Proper coordination
then places the exclusions in a composition: it identifies the stage that
must make them, as the log does in state-machine replication, and certifies
that the stages behind it need none of their own.

Underlying these issues is a reassessment of
tradition: what is a declarative specification, and how little does it require
to be useful? The question is timely. Coding agents increasingly write
imperative code, often rife with bugs, which has renewed interest in formal
specification languages like Lean~\cite{demoura2021lean4}. A higher-level
language keeps agents out of the incidental complexity of implementations and
pairs them with proofs of correctness: neurosymbolic
computing~\cite{an2025neurosymbolic}. This is a renewed opportunity for logic,
dataflow, and functional languages. Today these languages typically specify
protocols and programs in detail, as in declarative networking; this paper
points to specification at a higher level still, opening analyses like ours and
potential program synthesis and optimization. \removednote{\textbf{unclassified}, 40 words, rank 41; \textit{A specification abstracts execution details into a possibility relation over ordered input and output domains.}}


\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

\textcolor{newblue}{A specification abstracts execution details away, and the results here reduce
to a single question: can a participant act on what it knows without being
contradicted by what it learns later?} \textcolor{newblue}{Mutexes, locks, atomic instructions, and
agreement protocols prevent such contradictions by issuing exclusions.} Functional CALM answers the question for functions: a functional specification
stays confluent as input arrives exactly when it is monotone, and then no
output needs an exclusion. This generalizes the classic result from logical fact
sets to arbitrary input and output orders.

Complete CALM extends the answer to specifications that offer several outputs
for one input. The test is Lifted Monotonicity, which combines Relational
Monotonicity with Directed Outputs. When it fails, some reachable output
requires an exclusion. At each input it changes, an exclusion either empties
the fiber or removes particular output rows. Because exclusions are explicit, the model
can also check where a composition coordinates
(\S\ref{sec:composition}). \textcolor{newblue}{A totally ordered log and a
key-value store are both non-monotone at their ordinary specifications, yet
when the log guards the store, the log's exclusions are all the composition
needs and the store makes none.} Multi-Paxos is one way to distribute those
exclusions.

Complete CAP is the partition form of the same equivalence. A system can keep
a guarantee's explicit outputs available, and consistent, through partitions
exactly when they have a common \outnoun at every common \indesc of their inputs;
when every output is explicit, this is Lifted Monotonicity.

The generality of our specification model opens a range of new questions
regarding the ambiguity of specifications and their exclusions. One direction
extends the boolean criteria of this paper to metrics: a Determination
Complexity theory that assesses \emph{how many} exclusions are intrinsically
needed to determine a specification. Another is Determination Provenance: a
given output---or its absence---is determined not only by inputs, but also by
the exclusions made in a run, which brings formal models of provenance to
concurrent systems. A third direction is to provide general-purpose static
analysis of CALM and proper coordination in a real programming framework.
