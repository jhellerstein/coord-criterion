\begin{abstract}
When does a concurrent system intrinsically need coordination?  Too little
coordination produces heisenbugs; too much adds latency and sacrifices
availability.  CALM, CAP, and CRDTs each answer the question for one class of
programs, consistency models, or design patterns, in formalisms that cannot be
compared or combined, which leaves developers guessing.

We provide a simple formalism that unifies the literature and
offers new results.  A minimalist \emph{\spectype} (spec) is a possibility table containing, for
each input, every output a system may produce, with partial orders on inputs and
outputs.  Its runs are \emph{consistent} when they are confluent.
\emph{Functional CALM} generalizes the CALM Theorem from Datalog to any function
between ordered domains: the runs are consistent exactly when the function is
monotone.  \emph{Complete CALM}
extends this to nondeterministic specs, where the test is
\emph{Lifted Monotonicity}; \emph{Complete CAP} follows as a corollary.
Highly Available Transactions, Invariant Confluence, and later CALM refinements map into the same framework.

Prior CALM results stop at monotonicity: for non-monotone
specs, they say only that coordination is needed somewhere.  Our
unified framework says exactly
what coordination a spec needs, and when.  A non-monotone
spec's outputs drive irrevocable \emph{\commitments} that are required for consistency,
enforceable by coordination mechanisms like locks, atomics, or consensus.
\emph{Proper Coordination} identifies which stage of a composition must make the
\commitments and certifies that later stages need none, validating designs such
as consensus logs in front of a replicated store.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Concurrent systems rely on coordination mechanisms ranging from mutexes and
atomic instructions to database locks and consensus protocols. These mechanisms
prevent concurrent actions from producing undesirable outcomes, but they must be
used where they are needed, and ideally only there. Too little coordination
produces heisenbugs: rare, timing-dependent anomalies that are hard to
reproduce. Too much adds overhead and latency, and in distributed settings
sacrifices availability. So when is coordination avoidable, and when is it
intrinsically required? CALM~\cite{ameloot2013relational},
CAP~\cite{gilbert2002cap}, and CRDTs~\cite{shapiro2011crdt} each answer the
question for one class of programs, consistency models, or design patterns, in
formalisms that cannot be compared or combined. This leaves developers
guessing.

The CALM Conjecture proposed a crisp, bidirectional criterion: consistency can
be achieved without coordination exactly when a specification is
monotone~\cite{hellerstein2010declarative}. But the original proof applied only
in a restricted setting: Datalog queries evaluated by relational transducer
networks~\cite{ameloot2013relational,hellerstein2020keeping}. The CAP
Conjecture proposed a related question: which consistency guarantees can remain
available when participants cannot
communicate~\cite{brewer2000towards,brewer2012cap}? Its original proof
provided the answer for only one guarantee: linearizable registers~\cite{gilbert2002cap}. Yet both results are invoked broadly: CALM as a
rule that monotone programs need no coordination, CAP as a general constraint
on distributed systems. The folklore outgrew the proofs.

The apparent connection between CALM and CAP was never established in a common
formalism, and nearby results only complicate the picture. CRDTs use
inflationary updates over join-semilattices for coordination-free
convergence~\cite{shapiro2011crdt}; HATs identify transactional guarantees
available under partition~\cite{bailis2013hat}; invariant confluence asks when
independently reached database states merge
safely~\cite{bailis2014coordination}. \textcolor{newblue}{The diverse formalisms leave cross-cutting
questions unclear, including whether CALM encompasses CAP, whether CRDT join
laws are necessary, why only some transactional isolation levels remain
available during partition, and whether invariant confluence differs from CALM
(\S\ref{sec:instances}).}

We put these results on common ground, in a formalism that clarifies the
literature and offers new results. Prior CALM results stop at the monotone
boundary: for a non-monotone program, they say only that coordination is needed
somewhere, not what it must accomplish or where it belongs. Few real systems
are monotone throughout, so that is where developers most need help. Our
framework is constructive on both sides of the boundary. It answers questions
the literature leaves open: when nondeterministic specifications require
coordination, what that coordination must achieve, and how to tell whether it is
properly placed.

\paragraph{The model: \Spectype[s].}
In systems, as in life, our possibilities narrow over time. Sometimes the world
moves on, and possibilities are lost to us. Sometimes possibilities disappear
because we must decide among them. \removednote{\textbf{unclassified}, 15 words, rank 84; \textit{A decision is incidental when every possibility it removes is covered by a remaining alternative.}} \textcolor{newblue}{A
decision is fateful when it rules out a possibility that no remaining alternative
can cover.} When must a system make a fateful decision?

We ground the problem in a simple model of \emph{\spectype[s]}, or \emph{specs}
for short, independent of any language, protocol, or placement. The designer of
a spec provides a \emph{possibility relation} $P\subseteq I\times O$: a virtual
table that may be infinite, like a mathematical function. Each row in this table
is a possibility observable from the outside: it pairs the input received so far
with a permitted output. The design includes the input domain $I$ and output domain $O$,
with partial orders that help capture the spec's semantics. On inputs, the order
is accumulation: $i\InputOrd j$ means that $j$ \emph{\inrel} $i$ with more input
from the environment. On outputs, the order is subsumption: $o\OutputOrd u$
means that  $u$ \emph{\outrel} $o$ by subsuming (i.e., covering) all spec-relevant information in
$o$. These are both application-specific notions that must be captured well for
a spec to be faithful.

As a system runs, possibilities narrow: the spec's table loses rows. When new
input arrives, the world has moved on, and rows that cannot be reached by
extending that input are lost. When the system decides among possibilities, it
makes a controlled decision to remove rows. \removednote{\textbf{unclassified}, 21 words, rank 71; \textit{The decision is incidental if each removed output is covered by a remaining output that it, ...}} \textcolor{newblue}{The decision is fateful if some
removed output has no remaining output that \outrel it.} Choosing between two candidates in a leader election is fateful: nothing
remaining covers the loser's possibility of a win. \textcolor{newblue}{So is closing the polls early,
ruling out every row with a late ballot.} \removednote{\textbf{unclassified}, 96 words, rank 25; \textit{We call a fateful decision a : an irrevocable decision, enforceable by coordination mechanisms such as ...}} \textcolor{newblue}{We call a fateful decision a
\emph{\commitment}.}

\textcolor{newblue}{Automata have often been used to formally specify distributed
protocols; we show how an automaton can be projected onto an \spectype[] that
captures only observable behavior (\S\ref{sec:scope}).} \textcolor{newblue}{Despite its simplicity, our model supports not only application invariants, but also general-purpose consistency definitions (\S\ref{sec:complete-cap},
\S\ref{sec:hat-instance}).}

\paragraph{Consistency and a criterion for achieving it.}
The model provides clean definitions for the two sides of CALM: Consistency
and Monotonicity. Our Consistency definition is general: the runs of a spec are
\emph{consistent} when they are \emph{confluent}, so that states reached along
different paths can be brought to a common state. Applied to different specs,
this one definition captures classical consistency models, from eventual consistency of replicated data
through linearizability, serializability and other isolation levels. It also captures consistency of application specs.

A spec with one output per input is a function, and its monotonicity is standard: $i\InputOrd j$
implies $f(i)\OutputOrd f(j)$. Nondeterministic specs, such as leader election
and transaction scheduling, need a richer monotonicity property that ensures
not only that outputs grow as inputs grow, but also that different output
choices for the same input can grow by $\OutputOrd$ to a common permitted
output. We call this property \emph{Lifted Monotonicity}, and a spec that has
it \emph{lifted monotone}. It reduces to standard monotonicity for functional specs.

Confluence is an operational property of all the runs a spec allows. Lifted Monotonicity,
by contrast, is a static property of the spec's table: it can be checked on
pairs of rows, without exploring runs. \textcolor{newblue}{\emph{Functional CALM}
(\S\ref{sec:functional-calm-theorem}) and \emph{Complete CALM}
(\S\ref{sec:complete-calm-theorem}) show that the two coincide: the runs a spec allows are consistent exactly when the spec is
lifted monotone.} Hence Consistency As Lifted Monotonicity, a new reading of the
CALM acronym.

\textcolor{newblue}{Distributed histories are captured under the same consistency criteria as
sequential runs (\S\ref{sec:distributed-execution}).} \textcolor{newblue}{Complete CAP
(\S\ref{sec:complete-cap}) says Lifted Monotonicity exactly characterizes specs
that can keep every permitted output both available and consistent during a
partition.} The Gilbert--Lynch CAP Theorem~\cite{gilbert2002cap} follows directly.

\paragraph{Coordination is for \commitment.}
What of coordination? A \commitment isolates the contract a coordination
mechanism achieves, apart from how it achieves it: locks, barriers,
and other mechanisms all enforce \commitments. Our criterion identifies exactly
the \commitments a spec's outputs require, independent of mechanism. In a composition of specs, \emph{Proper Coordination}
identifies which stage must make the \commitments and certifies that later
stages need none. \textcolor{newblue}{In state-machine replication, for example, the consensus log
makes every \commitment; the replicated store makes none, whether it employs coordination or not
(\S\ref{sec:composition}).}

\paragraph{Specs and monotonicity.}
A spec is a possibility table for a function over ordered types. To avoid ambiguity, we state this precisely.

\begin{definition}[Functional \spectype, total]
\label{def:functional-specification}
\textcolor{newblue}{A \emph{functional \spectype} is a tuple
$\Spec=(I,\InputOrd,O,\OutputOrd,P)$,
where $(I,\InputOrd)$ and $(O,\OutputOrd)$ are partially ordered input and
output types, and $P\subseteq I\times O$ is its \emph{possibility relation}:
a typed relation with schema $(\mathrm{in},\mathrm{out})$ in which every
input appears in at least one row ($P$ is \emph{total} on $I$) and at most one
(a functional dependency).} \textcolor{newblue}{The unique output paired with $i$ induces a
function $f:I\to O$.} \removednote{\textbf{unclassified}, 2 words, rank 107; \textit{with [math]}}
\end{definition}

The two orders require thoughtful design. Inputs grow as ballots arrive: $i\InputOrd j$, read ``$j$ \emph{\inrel} $i$'', means that
$j$ has everything $i$ has and possibly more. So $ab\cdot$ \inrel $a\cdot\cdot$,
while $aa\cdot$ and $ab\cdot$ are incomparable. Outputs grow as announcements
say more: $o\OutputOrd u$, read ``$u$ \outrel $o$'', means that $u$ makes every
claim $o$ makes. Either winner \outrel $\bot$, which claims nothing, while $a$
and $b$ are incomparable. A functional spec is monotone when growing input only
ever extends its output: \removednote{\textbf{unclassified}, 11 words, rank 95; \textit{A functional [math], with induced function [math], is monotone if}}

\begin{definition}[Functional monotonicity]
\label{def:functional-monotonicity}
\textcolor{newblue}{$\Spec$ is \emph{monotone} if}
\begin{equation}
  i\InputOrd j
  \quad\Longrightarrow\quad
  f(i)\OutputOrd f(j).
  \label{eq:functional-monotonicity}
\end{equation}
\end{definition}

The election is monotone: to give an example, the input sequence
$a\cdot\cdot\InputOrd ab\cdot\InputOrd aba$ produces the output sequence
$\bot\OutputOrd\bot\OutputOrd a$.

\paragraph{States and input events.}
As a run proceeds, the possibilities narrow. Each panel of
Figure~\ref{fig:election-frames} shows one moment: the ballots received so
far, and the rows that can still be reached from them. We call such a moment
a specification state. \removednote{\textbf{unclassified}, 48 words, rank 46; \textit{Given a specification [math], a specification state is a pair [math], where [math] is the current ...}; labels def:residual-state}

\begin{definition}[Specification state]
\label{def:state}
\textcolor{newblue}{A \emph{specification state} $\sigma=(i,R)$ consists of a \emph{current input}
$i$ and a \emph{remainder table} $R\subseteq P$ that contains a row with input
$i$ and otherwise only rows whose inputs \inrelbase $i$.}
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[\Commitment]
\label{def:commitment}
\textcolor{newblue}{A \emph{\commitment} removes a nonempty set of rows from a state's remainder
table and leaves the current input unchanged, such that no removed row $(k,u)$
has a remaining row $(k,w)$ with $u\OutputOrd w$.}
\end{definition}

\textcolor{newblue}{In a functional spec each input has one row, so a \commitment rules out the
inputs of the rows it removes.} When $o$ is not legal, we make it legal via a \commitment
that removes the rows whose outputs conflict with $o$. Emitting $o$ only once it is legal keeps a run from
contradicting $o$ later on, when the environment may already have acted on it.

\begin{definition}[Output event, canonical \commitment]
\label{def:output-event}
At a state $(i,R)$ with current output $o$, the \emph{output event}
$\mathsf{output}(o)$ atomically keeps only the rows whose outputs \outrelbase $o$,
and then emits $o$: \removednote{\textbf{unclassified}, 0 words, rank 108; labels def:canonical-commitment-functional}
\begin{equation}
  (i,R)\xrightarrow{\mathsf{output}(o)}\bigl(i,\ R[\mathrm{out}\OutputGe o]\bigr).
  \label{eq:output-action}
\end{equation}
\textcolor{newblue}{If $o$ is not legal at $(i,R)$, the event first removes the rows whose outputs
do not \outrelbase $o$; this removal is $o$'s \emph{canonical \commitment} $x_o$,
after which $o$ is legal.} \textcolor{newblue}{In a functional table each input has only one row,
so $x_o$ rules out those rows' inputs.}
\end{definition}

An output event thus always emits a legal output, and applies a \commitment
exactly when its output was not already legal. \removednote{\textbf{formal caution}, 89 words, rank 31; \textit{The current row [math] remains, since [math] itself, so the event always ends at a specification ...}} \textcolor{newblue}{At a determined state the

\paragraph{Runs and consistency.}
Runs are the foundation of the consistency side of CALM. A spec's runs are
sequences of its events, like those that produce panels~(a)--(c). \removednote{\textbf{unclassified}, 29 words, rank 61; \textit{Input and output events are specification events : they change the specification state.}; labels def:specification-event, def:transition-system} \removednote{\textbf{unclassified}, 97 words, rank 24; \textit{A canonical run , or just run , is any finite transition sequence of the transition ...}}

\begin{definition}[Specification event, canonical run, \commitment-free]
\label{def:run}
\textcolor{newblue}{Input and output events are \emph{specification events}, or just \emph{events}.} \textcolor{newblue}{A \emph{canonical run}, or just \emph{run}, is a

\emph{\commitment-free} when no output event in it applies a \commitment; the
states such runs reach are \emph{reachable without \commitment}.}
\end{definition}

Different runs may retain different rows. Under the equality order, rows
$(ab\cdot,\bot)$ and $(aba,a)$ have the \emph{common \indesc} $aba$, an input
that \inrel both of their inputs, but no output at $aba$ \outrel both $\bot$ and
$a$: none is a \emph{common \outnoun} of them. So a run that emits $\bot$ at
$ab\cdot$ must apply $x_\bot$, and can never be reconciled at $aba$ with a run
that receives $aba$ and emits $a$. Under the equality order this removal is
$\bot$'s canonical \commitment, required by the spec; in panel~(d), under the
original order, the registrar made the same removal by choice.

\indesc of their current inputs. \removednote{\textbf{unclassified}, 94 words, rank 28; \textit{Consistency requires confluence at every common of the runs' current inputs, not just at some larger ...}}
\end{definition}

\textcolor{newblue}{Consistency as we define it has to hold after every output.} \textcolor{newblue}{An inconsistent

\subsection{The Functional CALM theorem}
\label{sec:functional-calm-theorem}

\textcolor{newblue}{Monotonicity is a
static property of a spec; consistency is an operational property
of the runs it allows.} Functional CALM bridges the two: the runs are consistent exactly when
the spec is monotone.

Events are
relational selections, and selections commute, so a run's remainder table
depends only on its last input and the outputs it emitted: \removednote{\textbf{unclassified}, 15 words, rank 85; \textit{If a run ends at current input [math] after emitting [math], its remainder table is}; labels eq:run-residual}

\begin{lemma}[Rows remaining after a run]
\label{lem:run-residual}
\textcolor{newblue}{If a run ends with the current input $i$ after emitting
$o_1,\ldots,o_m$, its remainder table is \(P[\mathrm{in}\InputGe i,\ \mathrm{out}\OutputGe o_1,\ \ldots,\
  \mathrm{out}\OutputGe o_m]\): the rows whose input \inrel $i$ and whose
output \outrel each $o_r$.} \removednote{\textbf{unclassified}, 54 words, rank 45; \textit{Input and output events are both relational selections, and selections commute; successive input selections reduce to ...}}
\end{lemma}
\textcolor{newblue}{\proofinfull}

A set of runs can be continued to a common state exactly when their current
remainder tables share a row at the target input:

\begin{proposition}[Survivor test]
\label{prop:confluence}
Runs ending at states $(i_r,R_r)$, $r=1,\ldots,k$, are confluent at a common
\indesc $j$ of their current inputs if and only if some row $(j,u)$ is in every
$R_r$. For any such $u$, $\mathsf{input}(j)$ followed by $\mathsf{output}(u)$
brings every run to the same state,
$\bigl(j,\ P[\mathrm{in}\InputGe j,\ \mathrm{out}\OutputGe u]\bigr)$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
First assume the runs are confluent at $j$. Rows are never added, so a row of
the common state at $j$ was already in every $R_r$. Now assume $(j,u)$ is in
every $R_r$. Then $\mathsf{input}(j)$ followed by $\mathsf{output}(u)$ brings
every run to the same state, so the runs are confluent at $j$. \removednote{\textbf{explanation}, 116 words, rank 22; \textit{Full proof in Appendix .}} \textcolor{newblue}{\fullproof}
\end{proof}

\textcolor{newblue}{Consistency thus ensures that runs never diverge irreparably: there is no
``split brain''.} \textcolor{newblue}{Whatever events a finite collection of runs contains, at any
common \indesc of their current inputs, some state is reachable by every run
and contradicts none of their outputs.} \textcolor{newblue}{Consistency is a safety property of the spec's runs:
it describes no protocol and promises no delivery or termination.} With the survivor test, consistency comes down to legality:

\begin{lemma}[Confluence and legality]
\label{lem:confluence-legality}
The runs of an \spectype are consistent if and only if no output event of any
run applies a \commitment.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
\textcolor{newblue}{First assume the runs are consistent, and suppose some output event applies
$x_o$, removing a row $(k,x)$.} \removednote{\textbf{unclassified}, 16 words, rank 82; \textit{It removes some row [math] such that no row at [math] both [math] and [math].}} \textcolor{newblue}{A run from the state before that event that
instead takes $\mathsf{input}(k)$ and emits $x$ shares no row at $k$ with it,
so by Proposition~\ref{prop:confluence} the two are not confluent at $k$, a
contradiction.} \removednote{\textbf{unclassified}, 66 words, rank 37; \textit{Now assume no output event applies a , and take a family of runs and a ...}} \textcolor{newblue}{Now assume no output event applies a \commitment.} \textcolor{newblue}{Every output
is then legal where it is emitted, so the rows the runs keep at their current

\textcolor{newblue}{In the election under the equality order, emitting $\bot$ at $ab\cdot$ applies

\begin{corollary}[State is fixed by input]
\label{cor:state-by-input}
In a monotone functional specification, every run ending at current

\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]

\begin{theorem}[Functional CALM]
\label{thm:functional-calm}
The runs of a functional \spectype are consistent if and only if the spec is
monotone.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
\textcolor{newblue}{By Lemma~\ref{lem:confluence-legality}, the runs are consistent iff
no output event of a run applies a \commitment.} First assume they are

\paragraph{Classical CALM and CRDTs.}
Functional CALM restates the familiar CALM dichotomy at the level of the spec
rather than inside a particular programming language. Classical CALM fits, with inputs $J$ as sets of facts ordered by
inclusion and output views $Q(J)$ ordered by inclusion. \textcolor{newblue}{Functional
monotonicity becomes $J\subseteq J'\Rightarrow Q(J)\subseteq Q(J')$,
the standard definition of a monotone query~\cite{abiteboul1995foundations};
positive Datalog satisfies it, and negation may not.} \removednote{\textbf{formal definition/notation}, 0 words, rank 110; labels eq:query-monotonicity} Functional CALM
generalizes the statement to arbitrary input and
output orders, whatever formalism expresses the spec. \textcolor{newblue}{CRDT state specs have the same functional shape,
with received payloads ordered by inclusion and states by the semilattice
order~\cite{shapiro2011crdt} (\S\ref{sec:query-instance},
\S\ref{sec:crdt-instance}).}

\begin{definition}[Directed fiber, directed fibers]
\label{def:fiber-directedness}
A fiber $F\subseteq O$ is \emph{directed} when every nonempty finite
$X\subseteq F$ is compatible in $F$. A spec has \emph{directed fibers} when every fiber is directed.
\end{definition}

A singleton fiber is trivially directed. \textcolor{newblue}{Each traceroute fiber is directed
too: the union of finitely many permitted route sets is itself a permitted
route set, and it contains each of them.} \textcolor{newblue}{The two transaction orders in
Figure~\ref{fig:ambiguity-frames}(f), by contrast, are incompatible.}

Directedness is more permissive than a join-semilattice. \textcolor{newblue}{It asks only that
each finite set of outputs in a fiber have some common \outnoun within the
fiber, while a join-semilattice requires a \emph{least} one.} For example, the fiber
$\{\{a\},\{b\},\{a,b,c\},\{a,b,d\},\{a,b,c,d\}\}$ under inclusion is directed,
since $\{a,b,c,d\}$ \outrel every member, but $\{a\}$ and $\{b\}$ have no least
common \outnoun: $\{a,b,c\}$ and $\{a,b,d\}$ are both minimal. \removednote{\textbf{unclassified}, 34 words, rank 56; \textit{To state both requirements at once, we lift [math] from outputs to sets of outputs.}; labels eq:joint-output-order}

\textcolor{newblue}{An \spectype satisfies \emph{Lifted Monotonicity}, and is called

\paragraph{The common-extension condition.}
Lifted Monotonicity is stated for one input and its extensions, but runs may
emit outputs at incomparable inputs, say $o_1$ at $i_1$ and $o_2$ at $i_2$. Such runs can be reconciled only at a common \indesc $j$ of both, which calls
for a condition on rows at different inputs: a common \outnoun of $o_1$ and
$o_2$ in the fiber at $j$. The two conditions turn out to be equivalent. \textcolor{newblue}{In the traceroute spec, one run may trace from \texttt{s} and emit
$\{\texttt{s}{\to}\texttt{s1}\}$, while another traces from \texttt{t} and
emits $\{\texttt{t}{\to}\texttt{s2}\}$.} At their common
\indesc $\{\texttt{s},\texttt{t}\}$, the route set
$\{\texttt{s}{\to}\texttt{s1},\ \texttt{t}{\to}\texttt{s2}\}$ is a common
\outnoun of both.

\begin{definition}[Common-extension condition]
\label{def:common-expansion-condition}
An \spectype satisfies the \emph{common-extension condition} when,
for every finite set of rows $(i_1,o_1),\ldots,(i_k,o_k)$ of $P$ and every
common \indesc $j$ of $i_1,\ldots,i_k$, some $u\in P(j)$ \outrel every
$o_r$. \removednote{\textbf{unclassified}, 22 words, rank 70; \textit{For the empty set of rows the condition asks only that [math] be nonempty, which follows ...}}
\end{definition}

\begin{proposition}[Common-extension form of Lifted Monotonicity]
\label{prop:common-expansion-form}
An \spectype satisfies Lifted Monotonicity if and only
if it satisfies the common-extension condition.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
First assume Lifted Monotonicity, and take rows $(i_r,o_r)$ with a common
\indesc $j$. \removednote{\textbf{unclassified}, 69 words, rank 35; \textit{Lifted Monotonicity gives Relational Monotonicity, which carries each [math] to some in the fiber at [math], ...}} \textcolor{newblue}{Lifted Monotonicity at $i_r\InputOrd j$ carries each $o_r$ to an
\outnoun in the fiber at $j$, and at $j$ itself it gives those a common
\outnoun there.} \textcolor{newblue}{Now assume the common-extension condition.} \textcolor{newblue}{Lifted
Monotonicity is its special case in which all the rows share one input.} \textcolor{newblue}{\fullproof}
\end{proof}

\subsection{Lifted output events}
\label{sec:lifted-output-events}

To connect Lifted Monotonicity to consistency, we return to
runs, where nondeterminism raises a new question: when emitting one output
removes others, is that removal a \commitment? \textcolor{newblue}{The output event itself is unchanged from the functional case: at a state $(i,R)$ with $o\in R(i)$,
$\mathsf{output}(o)$ emits $o$ and keeps the rows whose outputs \outrelbase $o$,
ending at $(i,R[\mathrm{out}\OutputGe o])$ (Equation~\ref{eq:output-action}).}

\textcolor{newblue}{However, removing a row $(k,x)$ does not always lose the information in its output.} \textcolor{newblue}{If
some output $u\in R(k)$ \outrel both $x$ and $o$, the row $(k,u)$ is kept, and
a later output $u$ reports everything $x$ would have.} \removednote{\textbf{unclassified}, 69 words, rank 36; \textit{This is output subsumption, and we put [math] in [math].}} \textcolor{newblue}{We separate the removed
rows accordingly.}

\begin{definition}[Output subsumption, nondeterministic legality]
\label{def:subsumed-excluded}
\textcolor{newblue}{At a state $(i,R)$ with $o\in R(i)$, let $(k,x)\in R$ be a row with
$o\not\OutputOrd x$.} \textcolor{newblue}{It is \emph{subsumed} when some $u\in R(k)$ has
$x\OutputOrd u$ and $o\OutputOrd u$, and \emph{excluded} otherwise; $\Subsumed_o$
and $\Excluded_o$ are the sets of such rows.} \textcolor{newblue}{The \emph{canonical \commitment}
$x_o$ removes $\Excluded_o$, and $o$ is \emph{legal} when $\Excluded_o$ is
empty.}
\end{definition}

\textcolor{newblue}{The output event first applies $x_o$ and then removes $\Subsumed_o$, and only
the first removal is a \commitment.} \textcolor{newblue}{No row of $\Excluded_o$ has a remaining row
$(k,w)$ with $x\OutputOrd w$: such a $w$ either \outrel $o$ or is itself
subsumed, and either way $(k,x)$ would be subsumed.} \textcolor{newblue}{So $x_o$ is a \commitment
in the sense of Definition~\ref{def:commitment}.} \textcolor{newblue}{In a functional table no
other row shares an input with $(k,x)$, so every removed row is excluded, and
legality and $x_o$ are as in Definitions~\ref{def:exposable}
and~\ref{def:output-event}.} If $x_o$ removes every row at $k$, it rules out the
input $k$; otherwise it rules out only the excluded outputs at $k$. \removednote{\textbf{unclassified}, 14 words, rank 89; \textit{In the functional case every removed row is excluded, so [math] is as before.}}

The two specs of Figure~\ref{fig:ambiguity-frames} fall on opposite sides of this split. Emitting $[A,B]$ in panel~(f) puts the row for $[B,A]$ in $\Excluded_o$, ruling out that output at $\{A,B\}$. \textcolor{newblue}{The route sets removed in panel~(g) are in $\Subsumed_o$, so that output needs no \commitment.} \removednote{\textbf{signposting}, 72 words, rank 34; \textit{Appendix gives the formal definitions.}} \removednote{\textbf{unclassified}, 66 words, rank 38; \textit{Specification states, input events, runs, -free runs, and consistency are defined as before.}}

\subsection{The Complete CALM theorem}
\label{sec:complete-calm-theorem}

\textcolor{newblue}{Moving from functions to nondeterministic specs changes only which rows
removed by an output event count as a \commitment: those in $\Excluded_o$, but
not those in $\Subsumed_o$ (\S\ref{sec:lifted-output-events}).} \textcolor{newblue}{The rest of the
framework carries over.} \textcolor{newblue}{States, input events, runs, and consistency are defined as in \S\ref{sec:framework-brief}; the
rows-remaining lemma (Lemma~\ref{lem:run-residual}), the Survivor test
(Proposition~\ref{prop:confluence}), and Lemma~\ref{lem:confluence-legality},
with legality as in Definition~\ref{def:subsumed-excluded}, hold as stated, since
they depend on output events only through Equation~\ref{eq:output-action}.} \textcolor{newblue}{By Lemma~\ref{lem:confluence-legality}, the runs are consistent exactly when
every output they emit is legal, so the question is when every output is legal
at every state a run can reach.}

\begin{proposition}[No-\commitment test]
\label{prop:exposure-boundary}
An \spectype satisfies Lifted Monotonicity if and only
if every output at every state reachable without \commitment is legal.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
First assume Lifted Monotonicity, in its common-extension form
(Proposition~\ref{prop:common-expansion-form}). At a state reachable without
\commitment, an output $o$ and each output remaining at a legal \indesc $j$
have a common \outnoun at $j$, which the state keeps
(Lemma~\ref{lem:run-residual}), so $o$ is legal. Now assume every such output
is legal, and take rows $(i_r,o_r)$ with a common \indesc $j$. Each $o_r$ is
legal at $\sigma_P(i_r)$, so, taking the rows one at a time, some $u\in P(j)$
\outrel every $o_r$, and Lifted Monotonicity follows. \removednote{\textbf{signposting}, 4 words, rank 103; \textit{Full proof in Appendix .}} \textcolor{newblue}{\fullproof}
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Complete CALM]
\label{thm:complete-calm}
The runs of an \spectype are consistent if and only if the spec satisfies
Lifted Monotonicity.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
By Lemma~\ref{lem:confluence-legality}, the runs are consistent exactly when
no output event of a run applies a \commitment. First assume they are
consistent. \textcolor{newblue}{Every output at every state reachable without \commitment is then
legal, since emitting one that is not would apply a \commitment, so Lifted
Monotonicity follows (Proposition~\ref{prop:exposure-boundary}).} Now assume
Lifted Monotonicity. Every output at every state reachable without
\commitment is then legal (Proposition~\ref{prop:exposure-boundary}), so by
induction on its length every run is \commitment-free, and consistency
follows. \removednote{\textbf{signposting}, 25 words, rank 66; \textit{Full proof in Appendix .}} \textcolor{newblue}{\fullproof}
\end{proof}

\textcolor{newblue}{So the runs of the traceroute spec in Figure~\ref{fig:ambiguity-frames} are
consistent, while transaction runs that emit $[A,B]$ and $[B,A]$ are not, since
that fiber is not directed.}

\textcolor{newblue}{Complete CALM allows us to apply Lifted Monotonicity to check consistency
without assessing runs.} Lifted
Monotonicity is checked on the table itself, and pairs of rows suffice: if every
two rows with a common \indesc $j$ have a common \outnoun in $\fiber{j}$, then
so does every finite family of such rows, by replacing two rows at a time with
one row at $j$ that \outrel both. \removednote{\textbf{unclassified}, 55 words, rank 44; \textit{The condition that characterizes consistency of runs is a property of the spec alone: Lifted Monotonicity, ...}} \textcolor{newblue}{\jmh{I don't understand the last sentence.}}

\subsection{Distributed histories and consistent cuts}
\label{sec:histories}
\label{sec:distributed-consistency}

Until now, runs have been independent: each state changes only through that
run's input and output events. We now place one such run at each participant in
a finite set $\mathcal N$. Participant $p$ starts in the canonical state
$\sigma_P(i_p)$ and still changes its state only through its own specification
events. The new ingredient is communication. Besides learning input directly
from the environment, a participant can learn cumulative input from another
participant.

Participants exchange messages by $\mathsf{send}(m)$ and $\mathsf{receive}(m)$. \textcolor{newblue}{A \emph{message} $m=(j,d)$ carries an input $j$, or $\varnothing$ if it
carries none, and application-specific data $d$, such as timestamps, version
vectors, or state identifiers.} Receiving a message does not change the
specification state. \textcolor{newblue}{A participant at $(i,R)$ that receives $(j,d)$ may incorporate $j$ by its own $\mathsf{input}(k)$ for a legal common \indesc $k$
of $i$ and $j$.} \textcolor{newblue}{The system chooses $k$ (\S\ref{sec:systems}); the data $d$ can
influence that choice but never changes the state directly.}

Send and receive events connect the participants' locally ordered runs. Adding
an edge from each send to its matching receive gives the standard model of a
distributed history; the transitive closure of these edges and the local orders
is happens-before. \removednote{\textbf{unclassified}, 12 words, rank 94; \textit{Fix an , participants [math], and an initial input [math] for each.}}

\begin{definition}[Distributed history, consistent cut]
\label{def:distributed-history}
\textcolor{newblue}{A \emph{distributed history} $H$ is a finite set of \emph{history events}, each
an occurrence at one participant of a specification event, $\mathsf{send}(m)$,
or $\mathsf{receive}(m)$.} \removednote{\textbf{unclassified}, 96 words, rank 26; \textit{a total local order on each participant's history events;}; labels def:happens-before, def:consistent-cut} \textcolor{newblue}{Each participant's events are totally ordered, every
receive has a matching send at another participant, and each participant $p$'s
specification events, in order, form a run from $\sigma_P(i_p)$.} \textcolor{newblue}{A
\emph{consistent cut} $C$ is a set of history events closed under
happens-before: whenever $C$ contains an event, it contains every event that
happens before it~\cite{lamport1978time,chandy1985distributed}.}
\end{definition}

\textcolor{newblue}{The ``consistent cut'' term is standard and unrelated to our notion of consistency.} \removednote{\textbf{formal definition/notation}, 20 words, rank 76; \textit{A consistent cut contains a prefix of each participant's history events, so by item 3 it ...}; labels def:participant-run} \textcolor{newblue}{A history
is itself a consistent cut, its \emph{final cut}.} \removednote{\textbf{unclassified}, 151 words, rank 15; \textit{Let [math] be a common of the participants' current inputs at a consistent cut [math].}; labels def:distributed-consistency, prop:distributed-consistency} \textcolor{newblue}{At a
consistent cut $C$, the specification events of $C$ at participant $p$,
in local order, form its \emph{participant run}, ending in some state
$\sigma_p$.} \removednote{\textbf{unclassified}, 40 words, rank 51; \textit{The endpoint of a consistent cut [math] is [math].}; labels def:endpoint, fig:participant-runs} \removednote{\textbf{formal definition/notation}, 27 words, rank 63; \textit{A continuation of a history at a consistent cut [math] is a history [math] that extends ...}} \textcolor{newblue}{For a common \indesc $j$ of the participants' current inputs,
confluence asks whether the history can be extended so that every participant
reaches the same state $(j,R)$.}

\begin{corollary}[Complete CALM at consistent cuts]
\label{cor:distributed-calm}
\textcolor{newblue}{A spec satisfies Lifted Monotonicity if and only if, in every finite
distributed history, every consistent cut is confluent at every common
\indesc of the participants' current inputs.}
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
\textcolor{newblue}{First assume Lifted Monotonicity.} \textcolor{newblue}{The participant runs at a cut are a finite
family of runs, so Complete CALM makes them confluent.} \textcolor{newblue}{Now assume every
consistent cut is confluent.} \textcolor{newblue}{Any finite family of runs is the set of
participant runs at the final cut of a history whose participants exchange no
messages, so the runs are consistent, and Complete CALM gives Lifted
Monotonicity.} \removednote{\textbf{signposting}, 86 words, rank 32; \textit{Full proof in Appendix .}} \textcolor{newblue}{\fullproof}
\end{proof}

\textcolor{newblue}{In the traceroute spec, the runs of Section~\ref{sec:lifted-monotonicity} that traced from \texttt{s} and from \texttt{t} may be at different participants; they reconcile at $\{\texttt{s},\texttt{t}\}$ by emitting the union of their route sets.} \textcolor{newblue}{The spec guarantees only that such
a reconciling output exists; choosing it, communicating it, and scheduling the events are up
to the system (\S\ref{sec:systems}).}

\textcolor{newblue}{For functional specs,
Corollary~\ref{cor:state-by-input} also gives convergence: participants with
the same current input are in the same state, regardless of message paths,
arrival order, or outputs emitted.} \removednote{\textbf{unclassified}, 184 words, rank 11; \textit{In any history of a monotone functional specification, every participant whose current input at a consistent ...}; labels cor:convergence} \textcolor{newblue}{This is the convergence that strong eventual consistency~\cite{shapiro2011crdt}

$\Spec$ (Definition~\ref{def:run}) that is \emph{observably equivalent} to
$\rho$: the two accept the same inputs and emit the same outputs, and after each
event the canonical run keeps every row that $\rho$ keeps (\extended).} \removednote{\textbf{unclassified}, 220 words, rank 7; \textit{events only remove rows, so dropping them can only keep more.}}

\subsection{The Complete CAP theorem}
\label{sec:complete-cap}

Brewer conjectured that a distributed system cannot be consistent, available,
and partition tolerant at once~\cite{brewer2000towards}; Gilbert and Lynch
proved it as an impossibility result for a linearizable register~\cite{gilbert2002cap}. Complete CAP addresses the conjecture more broadly:
which specifications are subject to the CAP
conjecture, and which are not?

\textcolor{newblue}{To pose the question, we separate the outputs that participants emit from
the conclusions an observer may draw by combining them.} The spec must permit
such combinations, but no participant emits them, and availability does not
need them.

\begin{definition}[Explicit possibility relation]
\label{def:explicit-relation}
An \emph{explicit possibility relation}, or \emph{explicit relation}, is a set
$E\subseteq P$ of rows whose outputs participants actually emit. Write $E(i)$
for its outputs at input $i$.
\end{definition}

\textcolor{newblue}{Like Gilbert and Lynch, we examine a replicated register: a key-value store
with a single key, starting at $v_0$.} \removednote{\textbf{unclassified}, 4 words, rank 104; \textit{It starts at [math].}} Participant $p$ receives write $w(v)$
and emits its acknowledgment; concurrently, participant $q$ receives the read
$r$ and emits $v_0$. These two outputs are in $E$. A client that collects
both responses sees the pair, which is in $P\setminus E$: a linearizable
register spec permits it, since a linearization may place the read before the
write, and must, since the pair \outrel both local outputs. \removednote{\textbf{unclassified}, 15 words, rank 86; \textit{Had the write finished before the read began, the spec would not permit the pair.}}

Both availability and consistency are judged on histories in which a partition
separates the participants.

\begin{definition}[Partitioned history]
\label{def:partitioned-history}
A \emph{partitioned history} divides the participants into two or more
sides and contains no receive whose matching send lies on another side. Same-side messages are allowed, and participants still receive environment
input and emit outputs.
\end{definition}

We consider a system whose participants each follow a strategy that depends
only on their local history, and an environment that may send any legal input
to any participant. Availability asks that, during a partition, each
participant can still emit every explicit output its state permits. \removednote{\textbf{unclassified}, 0 words, rank 113; labels def:local-continuation}

\begin{definition}[Availability]
\label{def:partition-notions}
Consider a partitioned history that the system produces, in which participant
$p$ ends in state $(i,R)$. A \emph{same-side continuation} for $p$ extends that
history with events the system can produce on $p$'s side alone, with no new
input and no message from another side. Participant $p$ can emit $o$ when some same-side continuation for $p$ emits
$o$. \textcolor{newblue}{The system has \emph{Availability for $E$} when, in every
such history, each participant $p$ can emit every output in $R(i)\cap E(i)$.}
\end{definition}

\textcolor{newblue}{Partition Consistency asks that the sides' states can still be brought to a
common state.}

\begin{definition}[Partition Consistency]
\label{def:partition-consistency}
The system has \emph{Partition Consistency} when the final cut of every
partitioned history it produces is confluent at every common \indesc of the
participants' current inputs.
\end{definition}

The \emph{canonical system for $E$} is the system whose participants follow
canonical runs and emit only outputs of $E$.

\begin{definition}[Escaping CAP]
\label{def:escapes-cap}
\textcolor{newblue}{A spec \emph{escapes CAP} for explicit relation $E$ when its canonical system for $E$ has
Availability for $E$ and Partition Consistency.}
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Lifted Monotonicity for $E$]
\label{def:lm-for-A}

\section{Composition and Proper Coordination}
\label{sec:composition}

A replicated key-value store poses a puzzle. With mutable values, it is
non-monotone, and its replicas can diverge without coordination~\cite{alvaro2011bloom}. The pragmatic solution is
to impose a consensus-driven log upstream of the replicas, \`a la
Multi-Paxos~\cite{lamport2001paxos}, so all replicas execute the same commands
in the same order. But consensus is itself non-monotone: when two commands
arrive concurrently, either may be appended next, and the two alternative orders
have no common \outnoun. In essence, the standard solution to non-monotonicity
is more non-monotonicity! Why does it work?

That puzzle is explained by our framework. As we'll see, two specs compose by equijoin,
and the composition of two monotone specs is also monotone. Moreover, for non-monotone specs,
as in our puzzle, we
can reason about which stage actually makes \commitments under composition. When the
upstream stage makes every \commitment the composition needs, and the
downstream needs none on the inputs the upstream offers, we say the pipeline's
coordination is \emph{proper}
(\S\ref{sec:proper-coordination}). \removednote{\textbf{signposting}, 4 words, rank 106; \textit{Proofs are in Appendix .}}
\label{sec:sequential-composition}

Formally, consider two \spectype[s]
${\Spec_1=(I,\InputOrd,M,\preceq_M,P_1)}$ and
${\Spec_2=(M,\preceq_M,O,\OutputOrd,P_2)}$
in which the output domain of $\Spec_1$ is the input domain of $\Spec_2$. Their
\emph{sequential composition} is the \spectype from $I$ to $O$ with table
\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
  P_2\circ P_1
  &=\pi_{I,O}(P_1\Join_M P_2)\\
  &=\{(i,o):\exists m.\ (i,m)\in P_1\land(m,o)\in P_2\}
\end{split}
  \label{eq:spec-composition}
\end{equation}
where $\pi$ and $\Join$ are relational projection and equijoin. \textcolor{newblue}{Every $i\in I$
has a nonempty fiber in $P_2\circ P_1$, since $P_1$ is total on $I$ and $P_2$ is
total on $M$.} We run the two specs as a pipeline: $\Spec_2$ takes the outputs of $\Spec_1$ as
its inputs, and we say that $\Spec_1$ \emph{guards} $\Spec_2$. \removednote{\textbf{unclassified}, 23 words, rank 69; \textit{Appendix allows each spec to have an input of its own as well, as when a ...}} \textcolor{newblue}{In the
replicated store, the log guards the store: $\Spec_1$ orders the proposed
commands into a log, and $\Spec_2$ executes the log and outputs the
responses.}

\begin{lemma}[Composition]
\label{lem:composition}
If $\Spec_1$ and $\Spec_2$ satisfy Lifted Monotonicity, then so does the
\spectype with table $P_2\circ P_1$. \removednote{\textbf{unclassified}, 85 words, rank 33; \textit{By Proposition , it suffices to check the common-extension condition.}}
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
\textcolor{newblue}{We check the common-extension condition
(Proposition~\ref{prop:common-expansion-form}).} \textcolor{newblue}{Rows $(i_r,o_r)$ of
$P_2\circ P_1$ with a common \indesc $j$ come from intermediate values $m_r$.} \textcolor{newblue}{The condition for $\Spec_1$ gives $m\in P_1(j)$ that \outrel every $m_r$, and
the condition for $\Spec_2$ at $m$ gives $o\in P_2(m)$ that \outrel every
$o_r$, so $(j,o)$ is a row of $P_2\circ P_1$.} \textcolor{newblue}{\fullproof}
\end{proof}

By associativity the lemma extends to finite pipelines. \removednote{\textbf{formal definition/notation}, 16 words, rank 83; \textit{For a function [math], its table [math] satisfies Lifted Monotonicity exactly when [math] is monotone ().}} \removednote{\textbf{unclassified}, 9 words, rank 98; \textit{We return to the replicated store of the opening.}}

\subsection{Proper Coordination}
\label{sec:proper-coordination}

\textcolor{newblue}{The key-value store is not
monotone on its own, but it is monotone on what the log offers: a fixed order of commands
that grows by sequence extension.} The log
issues a \commitment to offer only such inputs, so the store need make no
\commitments.

More generally, let $\Spec_1$, which guards $\Spec_2$, be run by a system with any basis, and
let $\Spec_2$ run under a system of its own. Write $\Spec_2|_{\Spec_1}$ for $\Spec_2$ restricted to the outputs of
$\Spec_1$. \removednote{\textbf{unclassified}, 19 words, rank 79; \textit{We say that [math] satisfies Lifted Monotonicity on a set [math] when its restriction to inputs ...}}

\begin{definition}[Proper Coordination]
\label{def:proper-coordination}
A pipeline in which $\Spec_1$ guards $\Spec_2$ is \emph{proper} when
$\Spec_2|_{\Spec_1}$ needs no \commitment: every output at every state it
reaches without \commitment is legal.
\end{definition}

\begin{theorem}[Proper Coordination]
\label{thm:proper-coordination}
\textcolor{newblue}{Let $\Spec_1$ guard $\Spec_2$.}
\begin{enumerate}
\item
\textcolor{newblue}{The pipeline is proper exactly when $\Spec_2|_{\Spec_1}$ satisfies
        Lifted Monotonicity.}
\item
Suppose the pipeline is proper, and fix any system for $\Spec_1$. Then $\Spec_2|_{\Spec_1}$ can take each output of a run $\rho$ of that
        system as its next input and answer it without \commitment. Replacing
        each output of $\rho$ by these answers, and dropping the \commitment
        events of $\rho$, gives a canonical run of $P_2\circ P_1$. Such runs are
        confluent whenever the runs of the chosen system for $\Spec_1$ are.
\item
If the pipeline is proper and $P_2\circ P_1$ does not satisfy Lifted
        Monotonicity, some output of $\Spec_1$ needs a \commitment. \removednote{\textbf{unclassified}, 93 words, rank 29; \textit{(1) is the no- test (Proposition ) applied to [math].}}
\end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
\textcolor{newblue}{(1) is Proposition~\ref{prop:exposure-boundary} applied to
$\Spec_2|_{\Spec_1}$; (2) and (3) are proved in \extended.}
\end{proof}

This answers the opening puzzle. By (1), the store restricted to logs needs
no \commitment, so locks in the store would be pure overhead. By (3), since
the replicated store as a whole is not monotone, some output of the log needs
a \commitment: the upstream log is where that belongs, and consensus provides

\subsection{Composite CALM}
\label{sec:composite-calm}

Proper coordination shows how an upstream
stage can discharge a pipeline's \commitment obligations. \emph{Composite CALM} asks a stronger question: when does a pipeline need no
\commitment at any stage? If every stage is monotone, the answer is immediate:
by Lemma~\ref{lem:composition} the composite is monotone too, and no stage needs
a \commitment. The interesting case has a non-monotone stage, and it raises a
simpler puzzle than our opening one.

Consider the spec for an incrementing counter that is capped at $9$, and
compose it with a downstream spec for a double-digit threshold test, which
outputs \textsf{false} for values up through $9$ and \textsf{true} from $10$ on. \removednote{\textbf{unclassified}, 19 words, rank 80; \textit{Under the usual boolean order, as in LVars , false is only a lower bound, and ...}}



\textcolor{newblue}{Suppose that every answer must be final: unchanging in the face of more input.} Then the output order is equality, and the test cannot
emit \textsf{false} without a \commitment, since a later count of $10$ would
contradict it. Yet when composed after the capped counter, the pair of
stages needs no \commitment. When do composed specs erase the need for
\commitment entirely? \removednote{\textbf{unclassified}, 141 words, rank 18; \textit{The composite is itself an .}}

\textcolor{newblue}{The answer lies in what the upstream offers.} \textcolor{newblue}{The test fails monotonicity only
when the count passes from single to double digits, and the capped counter
never does so.} Restricted to the counts it does produce, the test always
answers \textsf{false}; it is constant, and so monotone under any output order,
including equality. \textcolor{newblue}{By Theorem~\ref{thm:proper-coordination}(1), the pipeline
is proper, and since the capped counter is itself monotone, neither stage needs
a \commitment.} \removednote{\textbf{unclassified}, 553 words, rank 5; \textit{This is proper coordination in which the upstream itself needs no .}; labels prop:joint-monotone, sec:intersection, def:intersection, prop:intersection-bound} \textcolor{newblue}{In general, an upstream stage can erase a downstream stage's
need for \commitment by filtering out the inputs on which the downstream is not
monotone; when the upstream is also monotone, the whole pipeline is
\commitment-free.} \textcolor{newblue}{Some \commitment-free composites arise in other ways (see
\extended).}

\textcolor{newblue}{The full paper extends these results to longer pipelines, in which the

compositions than sequential stages, including
stages with inputs of their own and arbitrary acyclic graphs of stages, checked node

\emph{transaction history}: the transactions executed so far, partially
ordered so that one precedes another when the second ran on a state that
included the first's writes.} \textcolor{newblue}{One history \inrel another when it adds
transactions, none of which precedes a transaction already present.} \textcolor{newblue}{Legal
inputs include only those histories in which each transaction preserves
$\mathcal I$ on the state it ran on.} \textcolor{newblue}{An output is a database state; outputs
are ordered by set-containment of versioned data.} \textcolor{newblue}{The \emph{state} of a
history is the initial state together with every version its transactions
write.} \textcolor{newblue}{Call a sub-history \emph{closed} when it contains every transaction
that precedes one of its own, and \emph{valid} when the state of each of its
closed sub-histories satisfies $\mathcal I$.} At input $h$, each valid
closed sub-history of $h$ contributes its state as a permitted output. An output may therefore leave out some of the input's transactions, but what
it includes must be closed and valid.

\begin{proposition}[Invariant confluence as Lifted Monotonicity]
\label{prop:iconfluence-lm}
$T$ is invariant confluent if and only if $\Spec_{\mathcal I}$ satisfies Lifted
Monotonicity, that is, if and only if the runs of $\Spec_{\mathcal I}$ are consistent. \removednote{\textbf{signposting}, 166 words, rank 12; \textit{See Appendix .}}
\end{proposition}

replicas can concurrently assign the same unused ID (say, 7) to two employees.} \textcolor{newblue}{The history with both assignments \inrel each history with only one.} \textcolor{newblue}{But every state that \outrel both single-assignment
states has two employees with ID~7, violating the invariant, so $T$ is not
invariant confluent.} \textcolor{newblue}{Unique IDs ask each assignment of ID~7 to choose one

\subsection{Operational models and scope}
\label{sec:scope}

\textcolor{newblue}{An I/O automaton~\cite{lynch1987io} projects onto a spec that pairs each
execution's receive sequence with its reply sequence, ordered by prefix.} \textcolor{newblue}{Every automaton execution is matched by a run of this spec, but not
necessarily vice versa.} \textcolor{newblue}{For example, the automaton may choose not to produce
an output $o$ for a given input $i$ in one execution, even though $(i,o)$ is
within spec from another execution.} \textcolor{newblue}{Modeling the automaton as a system, with
its choices as \commitment events, captures the executions precisely: the
automaton is confluent exactly when its \commitments never remove every row
its canonical runs could still agree on.} \textcolor{newblue}{Model checkers can refute
consistency on bounded inputs~\cite{newcombe2015aws}, and mechanized
proofs~\cite{hawblitzel2015ironfleet} can show, by the standard obligations of
refinement~\cite{abadi1991refinement}, that a declared spec stands in for the
projected one; \extended{} gives the details.}

\textcolor{newblue}{Our conditions are semantic.} \textcolor{newblue}{Confluence guarantees that
a common \outnoun exists, not that a deployed system reaches it; protocols,
liveness, and cost belong to the operational model in which a spec
is realized.} \textcolor{newblue}{Power et al.'s free termination~\cite{power2025freetermination}
likewise corresponds to a determined canonical state in our model, but they
additionally study fairness and eventual response, liveness concerns whose
semantic generalization remains future work.}


\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

The computing literature is rich with coordination mechanisms. Prior to the CALM
Conjecture, the justifications for these mechanisms were often situational. In the best cases---serializability being a prime example---there is a crisp,
whole-system invariant, and coordination mechanisms provably enforce system
correctness. \textcolor{newblue}{But mechanisms like locks, atomics and total orders are also used
liberally for guaranteeing local operational properties of concurrent data
structures or systems, and have often been used poorly in both software and
academic literature.}

MapReduce~\cite{dean2004mapreduce} conflated barriers with communication. \textcolor{newblue}{In database systems, query
engines often pipeline data between operators in a query plan, and block only
where a non-monotone operator, such as aggregation, needs all of its input to
produce an output~\cite{wilschut1991dataflow,graefe1990volcano}.} \textcolor{newblue}{MapReduce
instead coupled barrier coordination into every communication phase,
so even monotone parallel operators like join had to wait for coordination.} Parallel complexity models that count rounds, including Valiant's BSP model,
similarly conflate communication and
coordination~\cite{valiant1990bsp,karloff2010mrc}. \removednote{\textbf{unclassified}, 13 words, rank 92; \textit{It was clear to the database community that this was not intrinsically required .}}

Rather than repairing a specific model, the CALM Conjecture asked the underlying
question: when is coordination inherent, and why? The question was
first addressed in the context of declarative
networking~\cite{loo2009declarative}, another query processing setting. \textcolor{newblue}{That
agenda took Codd's idea of declarative
specification~\cite{codd1970relational} to a broader domain,
demonstrating that full distributed systems could be implemented in declarative
languages, with practical
payoffs~\cite{loo2005overlays,loo2009declarative,chu2007declarative,alvaro2010boom,alvaro2011bloom}.} \textcolor{newblue}{That validation, combined with query processing intuition, suggested the answer
to the CALM question: coordination of any kind is required if and only if the
system's declarative specification is non-monotone.} Ameloot et al.\ proved it
for relational languages~\cite{ameloot2013relational}.

  
\textcolor{newblue}{This paper raises the abstraction level yet further: from logic programs to simple
\spectype[s].} All that matters about a spec is which outputs it
permits for each input; the analysis needs no language or programming model,
declarative or otherwise. By going a level higher to specs, we not only set
aside the lack of mainstream adoption of declarative languages; we also capture
families of guarantees, like CAP and isolation levels, that have no application
or protocol in mind.




Raising the level to specs also enables deeper analysis. Bloom~\cite{alvaro2011bloom} flagged syntactic non-monotonicity in programs and
conservatively injected coordination code in front of it. Coordination code is
itself non-monotone, so this only adds more non-monotone syntax, leaving the
puzzle of Section~\ref{sec:composition}. Later tools moved from syntax to
declared contracts and invariants in specific
contexts~\cite{sivaramakrishnan2015quelea,kaki2018alone}, picking for each
operation the weakest consistency or isolation level a store offers that still
satisfies them. \textcolor{newblue}{We ask what a spec itself requires, independent of any menu of
levels, and by observable equivalence (Lemma~\ref{lem:canonical-equivalent}) the
answer holds for every mechanism.} \removednote{\textbf{unclassified}, 37 words, rank 54; \textit{Our work is also constructive on both sides of the boundary ().}}

\textcolor{newblue}{Underlying these issues is a reassessment of Codd's tradition: how
little structure must be declared to be
useful?} The question is timely. Coding agents today translate from natural
language to imperative code, which is often rife with bugs. \removednote{\textbf{unclassified}, 104 words, rank 23; \textit{This has renewed interest in interposing formal specification languages like Lean as a narrow waist in ...}} \textcolor{newblue}{This has renewed
interest in formal specification languages like
Lean~\cite{demoura2021lean4} as targets for agents~\cite{hellerstein2023codd},
coupling AI-driven heuristics to deterministic proofs of
correctness~\cite{an2025neurosymbolic}.}



\textcolor{newblue}{Our \spectype[s] and composition point to the possibility of
synthesizing programs at an even higher level.}





\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

\textcolor{newblue}{The simplicity of \spectype[s] provides a unified foundation for the
coordination literature.} It also clarifies the intrinsic purpose of
coordination: removing possibilities via \commitments. \removednote{\textbf{unclassified}, 198 words, rank 9; \textit{The question of coordination-free consistency becomes clear in this model: when must a system make a ...}} \textcolor{newblue}{This simplicity
leads to the generality of Complete CALM: a spec's runs are consistent exactly
when it is lifted monotone, which reduces to textbook monotonicity for
functional specs, and Complete CAP follows as a corollary.} \textcolor{newblue}{\Commitments
lead to Proper Coordination, explaining how non-monotone specs are handled
consistently.}







\textcolor{newblue}{The model opens a range of new questions.} One direction
extends the boolean criterion of this paper to a quantity: a Determination
Complexity theory that assesses \emph{how many} \commitments are intrinsically
needed to determine a spec. \textcolor{newblue}{Another is Determination Provenance: a
given output---or its absence---is determined not only by inputs, but also by
the \commitments made in a run, bringing formal models of provenance to
concurrent systems.} A third direction is to provide general-purpose static
analysis and synthesis of properly coordinated systems in practical programming frameworks.
