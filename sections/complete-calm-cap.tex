\section{Complete CALM and Complete CAP}
\label{sec:complete-calm}

The previous two sections developed orthogonal generalizations of Functional CALM:
distributed histories give Consistency a distributed reading, while relational
specifications add output choice.  We now combine them.  First we let systems
make their own commitments, identify coordination with commitment, and state
when conflicting output events cannot coexist; then Complete CALM equates Consistency, Lifted Monotonicity, and
Distributed Consistency, and Complete CAP states the resulting partition
boundary.

relational generalizations combined here.

\subsection{Systems}
\label{sec:coordination}

Sections~\ref{sec:functional-calm}--\ref{sec:ambiguous-specifications} studied
the output events the specification itself supplies.  A system may commit
differently, for example by sealing more than an output requires or by taking a
lock before it chooses, so we now let systems bring their own commitments.
We first define commitments in general.

\begin{definition}[Commitment, seal, choice]
\label{def:commitment}
A commitment removes rows from the remainder table without changing the
current input.  Formally, a \emph{commitment} is a partial function on
specification states that leaves the current input unchanged and removes a
nonempty set of rows, each with no remaining row at the same input that
\outrel it: whenever $c(i,R)=(i,R')$, we have $R'\subsetneq R$ and
\begin{equation}
  (k,u)\in R\setminus R',\ (k,w)\in R'
  \quad\Longrightarrow\quad
  u\not\OutputOrd w.
  \label{eq:commitment-excludes}
\end{equation}
A commitment \emph{seals} a legal input $k$ when it empties the fiber at $k$,
and \emph{chooses} at $k$ when it removes some but not all of the rows at $k$.
\end{definition}

The condition is judged at the table a commitment leaves, so a row removed
together with every row that \outrel it counts as a commitment's removal, not a
retirement.  The seals and choices of Definitions~\ref{def:output-event}
and~\ref{def:lifted-output-event} are commitments
(Appendix~\ref{app:bases}).  A system declares a \emph{commitment basis}
(Definition~\ref{def:basis}) and may apply several of its commitments in one
output event; Appendix~\ref{app:bases} develops both.  With several outputs per
input, such an event also retires rows.

\begin{definition}[Output event of a system]
\label{def:system-output-event}
\label{def:compound-output-event}
Let $(i,R)$ be a state and $o\in R(i)$.  An output event $\Out{\gamma}{o}$
under a basis $\mathcal B$ applies a finite, possibly empty sequence
$\gamma=c_1\cdots c_n$ of commitments from $\mathcal B$, each $c_m$ defined at
the state its predecessors leave, leaving a table $R_c$, and then retires rows
and emits $o$, all atomically; $\varepsilon$ is the empty sequence.  The event
is \emph{legal} when $(i,o)\in R_c$ and every row of $R_c$ ruled out by $o$ is
\outrelpast by a retained row that \outrel $o$:
\begin{equation}
  (k,x)\in R_c\setminus R_c[\mathrm{out}\OutputGe o]
  \quad\Longrightarrow\quad
  \exists u\in R_c[\mathrm{out}\OutputGe o](k).\ x\OutputOrd u.
  \label{eq:lifted-validity}
\end{equation}
The event then retires those \outrelpast rows and ends at
$(i,R_c[\mathrm{out}\OutputGe o])$.
\end{definition}

With one row per input no row can be retired, and legality says that every
row the commitments leave has an output that \outrel $o$:
$R_c\subseteq R[\mathrm{out}\OutputGe o]$.

\begin{definition}[Canonical basis, canonical event]
\label{def:canonical-commitment-basis}
The \emph{canonical commitment basis} collects the seals and choices of
Definition~\ref{def:lifted-output-event},
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal B_S
    =\{s_o:o\in O,\ \operatorname{dom}(s_o)\neq\varnothing\}
     \cup
     \{c_o:o\in O,\ \operatorname{dom}(c_o)\neq\varnothing\},
  \label{eq:canonical-commitment-basis-relational}
\end{equation}
where $s_o$ is defined at the states with $o\in R(i)$ and $K_o\neq\varnothing$,
and $c_o$ at those with $o\in R(i)$ and $U_o\neq\varnothing$.  At $(i,R)$, let
$\kappa_o$ apply $s_o$ when $K_o\neq\varnothing$ and $c_o$ when
$U_o\neq\varnothing$, so $\kappa_o$ is one of $\varepsilon$, $s_o$, $c_o$, or
$s_o\,c_o$.  The \emph{canonical event} for $o$ is $\Out{\kappa_o}{o}$: the
output event of Definition~\ref{def:lifted-output-event}, and with one row per
input that of Definition~\ref{def:output-event}.
\end{definition}

\begin{lemma}[Containment, canonical support, and commitment-free events]
\label{lem:canonical-commitment-normal-form}
Let $(i,R)$ be a state and $o\in R(i)$.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Every legal output event $\Out{\gamma}{o}$ takes $(i,R)$ to some $(i,R')$ with
        $R'\subseteq R[\mathrm{out}\OutputGe o]$, and removes the rows at
        inputs in $K_o$ and the rows in $U_o$ by commitment.
  \item The canonical event $\Out{\kappa_o}{o}$ is legal and takes $(i,R)$ to
        $(i,R[\mathrm{out}\OutputGe o])$, so it retains every row that any legal
        $\Out{\gamma}{o}$ retains.
  \item If $\Out{\varepsilon}{o}$ is legal, it takes $(i,R)$
        to $(i,R[\mathrm{out}\OutputGe o])$; it is legal exactly when $o$ is
        exposable.
\end{enumerate}
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
(1) A legal event ends at $R_c[\mathrm{out}\OutputGe o]\subseteq
R[\mathrm{out}\OutputGe o]$, since its commitments only remove rows.  A row at
$k\in K_o$, or in $U_o$, has no \outrelpart row in $R[\mathrm{out}\OutputGe
o](k)$, and hence none in $R_c[\mathrm{out}\OutputGe o](k)$; it cannot be
retired, so a legal event must remove it by commitment.  (2) The canonical
commitments remove exactly the rows at inputs in $K_o$ and the rows in $U_o$, all
of them ruled out by $o$, so $R_c[\mathrm{out}\OutputGe o]=R[\mathrm{out}\OutputGe
o]$, which contains $(i,o)$.  Every remaining ruled-out row is in $D_o$, and its
\outrelpart row lies at an input outside $K_o$ and is not in $U_o$, so it
survives the commitments.  The event is therefore legal and ends at
$R[\mathrm{out}\OutputGe o]$; by (1) no legal event keeps more.  (3) With no
commitment, $R_c=R$, and a legal event ends at $R[\mathrm{out}\OutputGe o]$.
It is legal exactly when every ruled-out row is in $D_o$, that is, when $K_o$
and $U_o$ are empty, which is exposability.
\end{proof}

\begin{definition}[System, canonical system]
\label{def:system}
A \emph{system} consists of one commitment basis $\mathcal B$
(Definition~\ref{def:basis}), shared by all participants, and a strategy for
each participant (Section~\ref{sec:histories}).  Its histories are distributed
histories (Definition~\ref{def:distributed-history}) whose output events are
labeled $\Out{\gamma}{o}$, with $\gamma$ a finite sequence of commitments from
$\mathcal B$ (Definition~\ref{def:system-output-event}); each such event is
legal at the participant's state where it occurs, atomically applies $\gamma$,
and emits $o$, and the environment observes $o$, not $\gamma$.  Their
transition projections are runs under $\mathcal B$
(Definition~\ref{def:run-under-basis}).  A system is \emph{canonical} when
$\mathcal B$ is the canonical basis $\mathcal B_S$ and each output event it
performs is the canonical event $\Out{\kappa_o}{o}$
(Definition~\ref{def:canonical-commitment-basis}) at the participant's state
where it occurs; its histories are then exactly those of
Section~\ref{sec:histories}.
\end{definition}

\paragraph{Two systems, two choices.}
The specification says which serialization orders are permitted; a system
decides which binding choices it makes and when.
Figure~\ref{fig:cc-commitments} shows two systems' choices on the same
three-transaction fiber of Figure~\ref{fig:ambiguity-frames}---one captures
two-phase locking (2PL), the other multi-version timestamp ordering (MVTO).
In 2PL, racing for a lock commits orderings among a subset of transactions:
in this case it commits only to $A$ before $B$, leaving $C$'s position
open.  By contrast, timestamp assignment in MVTO orders a transaction with respect to all others:
in this case, $B$ is assigned first place.  Both are choices: they remove
serialization orders while the input stays legal, but leave different
alternatives open.  Each is one of the commitments a system applies in emitting
a full serial order, and neither is the canonical choice $c_o$.
Appendix~\ref{app:cc-bases} gives both systems' commitments in full, including
abort commitments, which fix a transaction as aborted.

We call the runs of
Sections~\ref{sec:functional-calm}--\ref{sec:ambiguous-specifications}
\emph{canonical runs}: they are the runs under $\mathcal B_S$ whose output
events are all canonical (Definition~\ref{def:run-under-basis}).  Every system run
has an observably equivalent canonical run.  Lemma~\ref{lem:canonical-equivalent}
(Appendix~\ref{app:bases}) shows that every run under any basis, including
either protocol above, is equivalent to a canonical run, one that accepts the
same inputs and emits the same outputs, whose remainder table contains the
original's after each event.  Consistency is therefore judged on canonical
runs.

\subsection{Coordination}
\label{sec:coordination-def}

Canonical runs commit only by seals and choices.  That is not a design
decision: no commitment can do anything else.

\begin{proposition}[Commitments seal inputs or choose outputs]
\label{prop:seal-or-choose}
\leavevmode
\begin{enumerate}
  \item At each input where a commitment removes rows of the state it is
        applied to, it either removes every row, \emph{sealing} that input, or
        removes some outputs and keeps others, \emph{choosing} among them.
  \item Every run under any commitment basis is equivalent to a canonical run
        that keeps at least as many rows after each event and whose only
        commitments are the seals $s_o$ and the choices $c_o$.
  \item In a functional specification every commitment only seals.
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
(1) At an input $k$, either no row remains or some does.  (2) Canonical
equivalents (Lemma~\ref{lem:canonical-equivalent}) give the run, and $s_o$ only
seals and $c_o$ only chooses (Definition~\ref{def:lifted-output-event}).
(3) Each fiber has one row, so removing a row at $k$ removes every row there.
\end{proof}

So the effect of any coordination mechanism---a lock, a timestamp, an agreement
protocol---is contained in a combination of seals and choices.  Because each
commitment acts on a given state, the classification is unambiguous.  A seal
may remove the last rows that earlier choices left at an input; attributing the
seal across that sequence is a question of provenance, which this paper does
not pursue.

Consistency is confluence: independently reached states can still meet.
Coordination is its operational counterpart: the system makes a binding decision
that removes some otherwise possible behavior.  In our transition system, that
act is exactly a commitment, which seals or chooses
(Proposition~\ref{prop:seal-or-choose}).  A mutex, lock,
atomic ordering mechanism, or agreement protocol is in scope when it serves
this role.  Computation or communication that removes no such alternative is
not coordination merely because it uses one of these mechanisms.

\begin{definition}[Coordination]
\label{def:coordination}
A run or history \emph{coordinates} if and only if one of its output events
$\Out{\gamma}{o}$ (Definition~\ref{def:system-output-event}) has $\gamma\neq\varepsilon$, that is, applies a commitment that seals an input or chooses among outputs,
removing an alternative that the current input still permits and that no
remaining row at the same input \outrel.  It is \emph{coordination-free}, equivalently
commitment-free, when every output event is some $\Out{\varepsilon}{o}$; such
events may still perform output retirements.
\end{definition}

The next result explains why commitments are where coordination lives: a
choice and an output it rules out leave states that cannot meet at the
current input.

\begin{proposition}[Conflicting output events cannot coexist]
\label{thm:commitments-require-coordination}
Let $\sigma=(i,R)$ be a state and $x\in R(i)$.  Suppose one participant's
event $\Out{\gamma}{o}$ takes $\sigma$ to $\sigma'=(i,R')$ with no output in
$R'(i)$ that \outrel $x$ (necessarily $\gamma\neq\varepsilon$, since no
retirement can remove $(i,x)$).  Suppose a second participant performs a legal
$\Out{\gamma'}{x}$ from $\sigma$, reaching $t=(i,R_t)$.  Then $\sigma'$ and $t$ cannot be brought to a
common state with current input $i$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
By containment (Lemma~\ref{lem:canonical-commitment-normal-form}), every
output in $R_t(i)$ \outrel $x$, and no output in $R'(i)$ does.  The fibers
$R'(i)$ and $R_t(i)$ are therefore disjoint.  A common state with current
input $i$ has a row at $i$, and since events never add rows, that row would
belong to both.
\end{proof}

The proposition is a witness of non-confluence at the current input itself: the
first participant's commitment is a choice at $i$ that rules out $x$, not a
seal, since a commitment cannot empty the fiber at the current input: a state
keeps a row there.
Mutual exclusion follows once the system's states must stay confluent.  For a
canonical system (Definition~\ref{def:system}), Consistency of the
specification gives that confluence; a non-canonical system can lose it through
commitments it did not need, as when a traceroute system discards the larger
route sets.  If a system's states must stay confluent while both events remain
available to different participants, it must exclude one of them.  In a
shared-memory system a mutex or atomic instruction provides that exclusion; in
a distributed system, agreement is one way to establish it.  Static authority
or a predetermined strategy can instead make one event unavailable, in which
case no runtime agreement is needed.  Seals raise a related conflict that the
proposition does not cover: an output that seals an \indesc of its input
conflicts with input another participant may already have accepted.
Appendix~\ref{app:cap-response} treats this case, which underlies the
Gilbert--Lynch theorem (Corollary~\ref{cor:gilbert-lynch}).

\subsection{The Complete CALM theorem}
\label{sec:complete-calm-theorem}

Complete CALM turns the coordination boundary into a property of the
specification.  It equates Consistency---confluence of canonical runs---with
Lifted Monotonicity.  The distributed execution model
supplies the equivalent third view, Distributed Consistency.

\begin{theorem}[Complete CALM]
\label{thm:complete-calm}
For an ordered relational specification, the following are equivalent:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Consistency;
  \item Lifted Monotonicity;
  \item Distributed Consistency.
\end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Items~(1) and~(3) are equivalent by
Proposition~\ref{prop:distributed-consistency}.  By
Theorem~\ref{thm:common-refinement}, Consistency is equivalent to the
common-\indesc condition, which is form~(3) of
Proposition~\ref{prop:lifted-monotonicity-decomposition} and hence equivalent
to Lifted Monotonicity.
\end{proof}

The name CALM, for Consistency As Logical Monotonicity, tied the idea to a
logic language.  The condition that characterizes consistency is instead a
property of the specification: Lifted Monotonicity, which for a function is
ordinary monotonicity.

The theorem gives a specification-level test for history confluence, and it
derives exposability rather than assuming it.  By the exposure boundary
(Proposition~\ref{prop:exposure-boundary}), Lifted Monotonicity holds
exactly when every output at every state reachable without commitment is
exposable, so emitting outputs needs no commitment and is coordination-free.
When it fails, some finite family of rows whose inputs have a common \indesc
$j$ has no common \outnoun in $\fiber{j}$; by the exposure boundary some output
is then not exposable at a state reachable without commitment, so emitting it
requires a commitment and hence coordination.  When different
participants could each emit one of two conflicting outputs,
Proposition~\ref{thm:commitments-require-coordination} applies.

Output choice also separates safety from liveness.  Confluence guarantees that
at any consistent cut of a history (Section~\ref{sec:histories}), some continuation brings every
participant to a common state.  In the functional case, finding that
continuation is trivial: the state is
determined by the input (Corollary~\ref{cor:state-by-input}), so delivering the
full input suffices.  With several outputs per input, the state also depends on
which outputs have been emitted (Lemma~\ref{lem:run-residual}).  Participants at
a common input $j$ can reconcile by emitting some $u\in\fiber{j}$ that \outrel
everything any of them has emitted, and Lifted Monotonicity guarantees that
such a $u$ exists.
Whether they reach it is a matter of strategy.  Participants that choose
independently may keep emitting new outputs, each needing its own
reconciliation.  One strategy that terminates, once participants stop emitting other outputs,
is deterministic reconciliation: participants that share knowledge of the
inputs and emitted outputs apply the same rule for choosing $u$, and arrive at
the same state without any commitment.  Section~\ref{sec:discussion} returns to this distinction.

\subsection{The Complete CAP Theorem}
\label{sec:cap-corollary}

Brewer conjectured that a distributed system cannot be consistent, available,
and partition tolerant at once~\cite{brewer2000towards}; Gilbert and Lynch
proved it for a linearizable register~\cite{gilbert2002cap}.  The intuition
behind the conjecture is broader than any one guarantee: a guarantee that
needs coordination cannot survive a partition, and one that needs none can.
Complete CALM lets us state that intuition for any declared guarantee and
prove it in both directions.  A partition does not change which commitments a
specification needs; it removes the ability to make them jointly, since
participants separated by a partition cannot agree to seal an input or to make
a choice.

\begin{definition}[Partitioned history, Availability, Partition Consistency]
\label{def:partition-notions}
\leavevmode
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \emph{Partitioned history.}  A \emph{partitioned history} divides the
        participants into two or more components and contains no receive whose
        matching send lies in another component; participants still receive
        environment input and emit outputs.
  \item \emph{System.}  Fix a system (Definition~\ref{def:system}), with a
        strategy for each participant of every finite participant set and
        every choice of initial inputs; each strategy depends only on its
        participant's local history.
  \item \emph{Environment.}  The environment is adversarial: it may supply any
        legal input event to any participant.
  \item \emph{Availability.}  The system has \emph{Availability} when, at every
        consistent cut of every partitioned history it produces, each
        participant in state $(i,R)$ can emit every output in its fiber $R(i)$
        in some continuation, produced by the system, that adds only events of
        its own component and no input event.  No output waits for a message
        from another component, though an output event may apply commitments
        locally, within the participant's component.
  \item \emph{Partition Consistency.}  The system has \emph{Partition
        Consistency} when every such history is confluent at every consistent
        cut and every common \indesc of the participants' current inputs.  The
        continuation that confluence asks for need not stay partitioned and
        need not be produced by the system.
\end{enumerate}
\end{definition}

This is the \emph{interface form} of Availability: every output the
specification permits must remain available, not merely some response to each
operation.

\begin{theorem}[Complete CAP]
\label{thm:complete-cap}
For an ordered relational specification $\Spec$, the following are equivalent:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item some system has Availability and Partition Consistency;
  \item $\Spec$ satisfies Lifted Monotonicity.
\end{enumerate}
Moreover, when they hold, a commitment-free system has both properties: the
canonical basis with strategies that may emit any exposable output $o$ by
$\Out{\varepsilon}{o}$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Suppose first that $\Spec$ satisfies Lifted Monotonicity.  Take the canonical
basis and strategies that may emit any exposable output $o$ by
$\Out{\varepsilon}{o}$ at any time.  Every
history of this system is commitment-free (its output events apply no
commitment), so each projection is a canonical
run (Section~\ref{sec:determination}) and every state it reaches is reachable
without commitment.  By the exposure boundary
(Proposition~\ref{prop:exposure-boundary}), every output in each
participant's fiber $R(i)$ is exposable, and emitting it needs neither a commitment nor a
message: this gives Availability.  The system is canonical, so its histories
are those of Section~\ref{sec:histories}, and Distributed
Consistency, which Complete CALM supplies, gives Partition Consistency.

Conversely, let finite rows $(i_r,o_r)$ have a common \indesc $j$.  For the
empty family, totality gives $\fiber{j}\neq\varnothing$.  Otherwise, place one
participant $p_r$ per row, with initial input $i_r$, each in its own
component, and one more participant with initial input $j$ in a further
component, so that there are at least two.  At the cut containing no events,
$p_r$ is at $\sigma_S(i_r)$ and $o_r$ is in its fiber at $i_r$, so Availability
supplies a continuation within its component in which $p_r$ emits $o_r$.  No
message crosses components and strategies depend only on local history, so
these continuations combine into one partitioned history that the system
produces.  At its final cut,
Partition Consistency brings all participants to a common state with current
input $j$.  That state has a row $(j,u)$, and since events never add rows,
$(j,u)$ already lies in each $p_r$'s remainder table.  Containment
(Lemma~\ref{lem:canonical-commitment-normal-form}) gives $o_r\OutputOrd u$ for
every $r$.  Form~(3) of
Proposition~\ref{prop:lifted-monotonicity-decomposition} gives Lifted
Monotonicity.
\end{proof}

The proof of (1)$\Rightarrow$(2) never assumes that the system is
commitment-free, so local commitments cannot enlarge the class of
specifications with both properties; the proof of (2)$\Rightarrow$(1) shows
that a monotone specification needs none.
Partition Consistency asks that outputs from different components have a
common \outnoun, not be identical or totally ordered, and this is why the
characterization runs in both directions.  When Lifted Monotonicity fails, some
permitted output either waits for another component, sacrificing Availability,
or completes independently at the cost of Partition Consistency.

\paragraph{Answering every operation.}
Gilbert and Lynch ask for less than the interface form: every request
must receive \emph{some} response.  Asking for some response is meaningful only
once the specification says which outputs count as answers, since a specification
that accepts an abort or a null result at every input is trivially available.
Appendix~\ref{app:cap-response} makes that obligation part of the declared
specification and proves a \emph{response form} of Complete CAP: a system can
answer every operation through any partition, with responses that have a
common \outnoun, exactly when the specification is consistent on some \emph{response
interface}, a total choice of answers that can always continue its own earlier
answers (Theorem~\ref{thm:complete-cap-response}).  Lifted Monotonicity implies
the response form, and for functional specifications the two forms coincide.

The Gilbert--Lynch theorem follows as a corollary of the same machinery as
the response form.  A
\emph{replica} is a participant holding a copy of the register, and the theorem
is the case where a replica's only permitted answer is invalidated by input it
cannot see (Lemma~\ref{lem:forced-answer}).  A replica cut off
from a completed write must still answer a read
(Appendix~\ref{app:registers}), its local view permits only
the initial value, and the full history permits only the written one.  In our
terms, a single emitted output has no \outnoun at some \indesc of its input,
a failure of Relational Monotonicity
(Corollary~\ref{cor:gilbert-lynch}).  The appendix states the modeling bridge
from Gilbert and Lynch's asynchronous model, and explains how the guarantee,
the response obligation, and the deployment (which participant may receive
which inputs and emit which rows; Appendix~\ref{app:deployment}) together
decide a CAP question.

\paragraph{Deployment.}
Whether a commitment requires agreement across a partition also depends on
which participant may hold which input and make which choice.
Appendix~\ref{app:deployment} restricts Availability to the rows each
participant is authorized to emit, sets aside the degenerate case in which one
participant holds all relevant authority, and shows that a causal register (one whose reads reflect
causally preceding writes) needs no cross-component coordination when each participant answers only its
own reads.  With the central results in place, we now compare their
consequences across the surrounding literature.
