\section{Composition and Tight Coordination}
\label{sec:composition}

A replicated key-value store poses a puzzle.  With mutable values, it is
non-monotone, and its replicas can diverge without coordination~\cite{alvaro2011bloom}. The pragmatic solution is
to impose a consensus-driven log upstream of the replicas, \`a la
Multi-Paxos~\cite{lamport2001paxos}, so all replicas execute the same commands
in the same order. But consensus is itself non-monotone: when two commands
arrive concurrently, either may be appended next, and the two alternative orders
have no common extension.  In essence, the standard solution to non-monotonicity
is more non-monotonicity! Why does it work?

That puzzle is explained by our framework.  As we'll see, two specs compose by equijoin,
and the composition of two monotone specs is also monotone. Moreover, for non-monotone specs,
as in our puzzle, we
can reason about which stage actually makes \commitments under composition.  When the
downstream needs no \commitment on the inputs the upstream offers, the
upstream is a \emph{sufficient guard}, and its \commitments then suffice for
the whole pipeline.  When, in addition, the upstream makes no \commitment the
composition does not need, it is a \emph{tight guard}, and the pipeline's
coordination is tight (\S\ref{sec:tight-coordination}).  Proofs are in
Appendix~\ref{app:composition}.
\label{sec:sequential-composition}

Formally, consider two \spectype[s]
${\Spec_1=(I,\InputOrd,M,\preceq_M,P_1)}$ and
${\Spec_2=(M,\preceq_M,O,\OutputOrd,P_2)}$
in which the output domain of $\Spec_1$ is the input domain of $\Spec_2$.  Their
\emph{sequential composition} is the \spectype from $I$ to $O$ with table
\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
  P_2\circ P_1
  &=\pi_{I,O}(P_1\Join_M P_2)\\
  &=\{(i,o):\exists m.\ (i,m)\in P_1\land(m,o)\in P_2\}
\end{split}
  \label{eq:spec-composition}
\end{equation}
where $\pi$ and $\Join$ are relational projection and equijoin.  Every $i\in I$
has a nonempty fiber in $P_2\circ P_1$, since $P_1$ is total on $I$ and $P_2$ is
total on $M$ (it has a row at every $m\in M$).  We run the two specs as a pipeline: $\Spec_2$ takes the outputs of $\Spec_1$ as
its inputs, and we say that $\Spec_1$ \emph{guards} $\Spec_2$.
Appendix~\ref{sec:parameterized-composition} allows each spec to have an input
of its own as well, as when a store serves reads that bypass the log.

\begin{lemma}[Composition]
\label{lem:composition}
If $\Spec_1$ and $\Spec_2$ satisfy Lifted Monotonicity, then so does the \spectype with table $P_2\circ P_1$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
By Proposition~\ref{prop:common-expansion-form}, it suffices to check the
common-extension condition.  Take finitely many rows $(i_r,o_r)$ of $P_2\circ P_1$ and a common extension $j$ of their inputs.  Each row comes from an intermediate value $m_r$ with $(i_r,m_r)\in P_1$ and $(m_r,o_r)\in P_2$.  The rows $(i_r,m_r)$ are confluent at $j$ in $P_1$, through some $m\in P_1(j)$.  So $m$ extends every $m_r$, the rows $(m_r,o_r)$ are confluent at $m$ in $P_2$, through some $o\in P_2(m)$, and $(j,o)$ is a row of $P_2\circ P_1$ through which the rows $(i_r,o_r)$ are confluent at $j$.  Full proof
in Appendix~\ref{app:composition}.
\end{proof}

By associativity the lemma extends to finite pipelines.  For a function $f$,
its table $\{(i,f(i))\}$ satisfies Lifted Monotonicity exactly when $f$ is
monotone (\S\ref{sec:lifted-monotonicity}).

\subsection{Tight Coordination}
\label{sec:tight-coordination}

We return to the replicated store of the opening.  The key-value store is not
monotone on its own, but it is monotone on what the log offers: a log fixes
the order of commands, and a longer log only extends the responses.  The log
issues a \commitment to offer only such inputs, so the store need make no
\commitments.

More generally, let $\Spec_1$, which guards $\Spec_2$, be run by a system with any basis, and
let $\Spec_2$ run under a system of its own.  Write $\Spec_2|_{\Spec_1}$ for $\Spec_2$ restricted to the outputs of
$\Spec_1$.  We say that $P_2$ satisfies Lifted Monotonicity \emph{on} a set
$D\subseteq M$ when its restriction to inputs in $D$ does.

\begin{definition}[Sufficient guard]
\label{def:sufficient-guard}
$\Spec_1$ is a \emph{sufficient guard} for $\Spec_2$ when
$\Spec_2|_{\Spec_1}$ needs no \commitment: every output at every state it
reaches without \commitment is legal.
\end{definition}

Applying the No-\commitment test (Proposition~\ref{prop:exposure-boundary})
to $\Spec_2|_{\Spec_1}$ gives a static test.

\begin{proposition}[Guard test]
\label{prop:guard-test}
$\Spec_1$ is a sufficient guard for $\Spec_2$ exactly when
$\Spec_2$ satisfies Lifted Monotonicity on the outputs of $\Spec_1$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
Apply the No-\commitment test to $\Spec_2|_{\Spec_1}$, whose inputs are the
outputs of $\Spec_1$.  Full proof in Appendix~\ref{app:composition}.
\end{proof}

A sufficient guard leaves no \commitment to the downstream stage.

\begin{theorem}[Sufficient guards]
\label{thm:sufficient-guards}
Suppose $\Spec_1$ is a sufficient guard for $\Spec_2$, and fix any
system for $\Spec_1$.  Then $\Spec_2|_{\Spec_1}$ answers each output of a run
$\rho$ of that system without \commitment, and the pipeline's runs are
consistent whenever those of the system for $\Spec_1$ are.  Specifically,
replacing each output of $\rho$ by its answers, and dropping the \commitment
events of $\rho$, gives a canonical run of $P_2\circ P_1$, and such runs are
confluent at an input whenever the runs of the system for $\Spec_1$ are.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
The outputs of a run of $\Spec_1$ form a chain, since each output event keeps
only rows above its output, so they are a valid input sequence for
$\Spec_2|_{\Spec_1}$, which answers each without \commitment.  Each row
$(i,m)$ that the run of $\Spec_1$ passes through is a row of $P_1$.  It joins
with a row $(m,o)$ of $P_2$ to yield the projected row $(i,o)$ of
$P_2\circ P_1$, and these rows form a run of $P_2\circ P_1$.  Full proof in
Appendix~\ref{app:composition}.
\end{proof}

Conversely, the upstream cannot do without \commitments when the composite is
not monotone.

\begin{corollary}[Upstream \commitments]
\label{cor:upstream-commitments}
If $\Spec_1$ is a sufficient guard for $\Spec_2$ and the composite
$P_2\circ P_1$ is not lifted monotone, some output of $\Spec_1$ needs a
\commitment.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
Otherwise $\Spec_1$ satisfies Lifted Monotonicity by the No-\commitment test,
and $\Spec_2|_{\Spec_1}$ does by Proposition~\ref{prop:guard-test}.  The join
in $P_2\circ P_1$ uses only outputs of $\Spec_1$, so $P_2\circ P_1$ is also the
composite of $\Spec_1$ and $\Spec_2|_{\Spec_1}$, and it satisfies Lifted
Monotonicity by Lemma~\ref{lem:composition}, a contradiction.
\end{proof}

This answers the opening puzzle.  By Proposition~\ref{prop:guard-test}, the
store restricted to logs needs no \commitment, so locks in the store would be
pure overhead, and by Theorem~\ref{thm:sufficient-guards} the pipeline is
consistent whenever the log is.  The store as a whole is not monotone, so by
Corollary~\ref{cor:upstream-commitments} some output of the log needs a
\commitment: the upstream log is where that belongs, and consensus provides
it.

Longer pipelines nest: when $\Spec_1$ is a sufficient guard for $\Spec_2$,
the composite $P_2\circ P_1$ can itself guard a third stage, and the theorem applies again
(Appendix~\ref{app:composition}).

A downstream implementation may still take \commitments the specs do not
require, as a store that takes locks behind a log would.  An implementation of
$\Spec_2$ alone, rather than of $\Spec_2|_{\Spec_1}$, may also fail to answer
some upstream outputs (Appendix~\ref{app:components}).

\paragraph{Necessary and tight guards.}
A sufficient guard may still make more \commitments than the composition
needs.  Suppose two commands $A$ and $B$ commute,
and the store's outputs do not record their order, as when it reports its
state.  Emitting the log $[A]$ then rules out the log $[B,A]$ by \commitment,
yet the composite needs no \commitment there, since both logs lead to the same
output.  That \commitment is \emph{unnecessary}: it prevents a
failure of Lifted Monotonicity in the upstream that leads to no failure in the
composite.

\begin{definition}[Necessary guard, tight guard]
\label{def:necessary-guard}
\label{def:tight-guard}
$\Spec_1$ is a \emph{necessary guard} for $\Spec_2$ when it makes no
unnecessary \commitment: whenever rows $(i,m)$ and $(k,x)$
of $P_1$ with $i\InputOrd k$ are not confluent at $k$ in $P_1$, some rows
$(i,o)$ and $(k,y)$ of $P_2\circ P_1$, with $o\in P_2(m)$ and $y\in P_2(x)$,
are not confluent at $k$ in $P_2\circ P_1$.

$\Spec_1$ is a \emph{tight guard} for $\Spec_2$ when it is both a
sufficient and a necessary guard, and the pipeline's coordination is then
\emph{tight}.
\end{definition}

Necessity compares the upstream's canonical \commitments with those the
composite needs.  Which \commitments are canonical depends on $\preceq_M$, and
the prefix order of the commuting log demands too many for the store.  We compare
$\preceq_M$ with a relation derived from $P_2$: $m'$ \emph{covers} $m$,
written $m\sqsubseteq m'$, when every finite set of outputs in $P_2(m)$ has a
common extension in $P_2(m')$.  Confluence of rows and canonical
\commitments \emph{under} $\sqsubseteq$ use $\sqsubseteq$ in place of
$\preceq_M$.
For a functional downstream $f$, $m\sqsubseteq m'$ exactly when
$f(m)\OutputOrd f(m')$.

\begin{theorem}[Guard Tightening]
\label{thm:guard-tightening}
Let $\Spec_1$ guard $\Spec_2$.
\begin{enumerate}
    \item An order on $M$ makes $\Spec_1$ a sufficient guard for $\Spec_2$
        exactly when it is contained in $\sqsubseteq$ on the outputs of
        $\Spec_1$.
    \item If $\Spec_1$ is a sufficient guard for $\Spec_2$, every \commitment
        the composite needs is a canonical \commitment under $\sqsubseteq$.
        If each $P_2(m)$ has a greatest element, as when $\Spec_2$ is
        functional, this holds without that assumption, and the converse
        holds too.  So if $\sqsubseteq$ is an
        order (reflexive, antisymmetric, transitive), ordering $M$ by it makes
        $\Spec_1$ a tight guard.
\end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
(1) is Proposition~\ref{prop:guard-test}, through the
common-extension condition.  (2) Suppose
$m,x\sqsubseteq m'$ for some $m'\in P_1(k)$.  Then answers $o\in P_2(m)$ and
$y\in P_2(x)$ have extensions in $P_2(m')$, which is directed, so some
$u\in P_2(m')$ \outrel both, and $(i,o)$ and $(k,y)$ are confluent at $k$ in
the composite.  With
greatest answers $t\in P_2(m)$ and $s\in P_2(x)$, a common extension of $t$
and $s$ in the composite comes from some $m'$ with $m,x\sqsubseteq m'$.  When
$\sqsubseteq$ is an order, (1) makes $\Spec_1$ a sufficient guard under it,
and its canonical \commitments are then exactly the composite's.  Full proof in
Appendix~\ref{app:composition}.
\end{proof}

A greatest element of $P_2(m)$ is one answer to $m$ that extends every
other.  A functional downstream has one trivially, and the gap example below,
where $P_2(z)=\mathbb N$, has none.  Since every order under which $\Spec_1$ is
a sufficient guard is contained in $\sqsubseteq$, and a smaller order leaves at least as many pairs not confluent, each such order requires at least the
\commitments that $\sqsubseteq$ does.  At the other extreme, equality on $M$
requires the most, since every order on $M$ contains it.

Guard Tightening is the route to tight coordination: when the
downstream's answers have greatest elements, an upstream whose order requires
no more \commitments than $\sqsubseteq$ makes exactly the \commitments the
composition needs.  When the store answers each command in turn, as in
state-machine replication, $\sqsubseteq$ is the prefix order, and the
consensus log is a tight guard: execution is monotone and reflects the prefix
order, so every \commitment the log makes is one the store's clients can
observe (Appendix~\ref{app:tight}).  When the store reports only its state and
$A$ and $B$ commute, $[A,B]$ and $[B,A]$ cover each other and fail
antisymmetry.  The prefix order keeps them apart: $(\{A\},[A])$ and
$(\{A,B\},[B,A])$ are not confluent at $\{A,B\}$ under the prefix order but
confluent at $\{A,B\}$ under $\sqsubseteq$.  So the commuting log is a
sufficient guard but not a necessary one.  Tightening means identifying
values that cover each other, as a log that orders only non-commuting
commands does (Generalized Paxos~\cite{lamport2005generalized},
EPaxos~\cite{moraru2013epaxos}).  Reversing the order
on $M$ in the boolean toggle below is reordering by $\sqsubseteq$.  Without
greatest answers, a gap can remain.  Let $\Spec_1$ emit $z$ at input
$\mathit{lo}$ and any $n_k$ at a larger input $\mathit{hi}$, and let $\Spec_2$
answer $z$ with any number and $n_k$ with $k$.  No $n_k$ covers $z$, since no single
answer $k$ extends every number the downstream may answer to $z$, so $(\mathit{lo},z)$ and each row at $\mathit{hi}$ are not confluent at
$\mathit{hi}$ under $\sqsubseteq$, and so under every order on $M$ under which
$\Spec_1$ is a sufficient guard.  Yet the composite permits every number at both
inputs and needs no \commitment: a single implementation could answer at
$\mathit{lo}$ and later give the same answer at $\mathit{hi}$.  The upstream
must commit before it sees the downstream's answer, and the remaining gap is
the price of splitting the work into stages.

\subsection{Composite CALM}
\label{sec:composite-calm}

Sufficient guards show how an upstream
stage can discharge a pipeline's \commitment obligations.
\emph{Composite CALM} asks a stronger question: when does a pipeline need no
\commitment at any stage?  If every stage is monotone, the answer is immediate:
by Lemma~\ref{lem:composition} the composite is monotone too, and no stage needs
a \commitment.  The interesting case has a non-monotone stage, and it raises a
simpler puzzle than our opening one.

Consider the spec for an incrementing counter that is capped at $9$, and
compose it with a downstream spec for a double-digit threshold test, which
outputs \textsf{false} for values up through $9$ and \textsf{true} from $10$ on.
Under the usual boolean order, as in LVars~\cite{kuper2013lvars}, \textsf{false}
is only a lower bound, and the test is monotone.  Suppose instead that every
answer must be final: unchanging in the face of more input.  Then the output order is equality, and the test cannot
emit \textsf{false} without a \commitment, since a later count of $10$ would
contradict it.  Yet when composed after the capped counter, the pair of
stages needs no \commitment.  When do composed specs erase the need for
\commitment entirely?

The composite is itself an \spectype.  By Complete CALM, its runs are
consistent exactly when it satisfies Lifted Monotonicity, and then no output needs a
\commitment (Theorem~\ref{thm:complete-calm},
Proposition~\ref{prop:exposure-boundary}).  The orders on $I$ and $O$ are part
of the composite's contract with its environment.  The order on $M$ is not:
the composite's table never mentions it, and some order on $M$ may make both
stages monotone.  A composite can be jointly monotone, with no stage needing a
\commitment, in three ways.

First, every stage is monotone, as noted above.  The CRDT observations of Section~\ref{sec:crdt-instance} have this shape.  A
client query $q$ over the replica state $s$ is the composition $q\circ s$, and
compositions of monotone functions are monotone, so monotonicity of $q$ on the
whole state domain suffices.

Second, the upstream can filter out the inputs on which the downstream is not
monotone.  In the example above, the test fails monotonicity only when the
count passes from single to double digits, and the capped counter never does
so.  Restricted to the counts it does produce, the test always answers
\textsf{false}; it is constant, and so monotone under any output order,
including equality.  By Proposition~\ref{prop:guard-test}, the capped counter
is a sufficient guard, and since it is itself monotone, neither stage needs
a \commitment (Proposition~\ref{prop:joint-monotone}(2)).  This is tight
coordination in which the upstream itself needs no \commitment.

Third, a different order on $M$ can make two non-monotone stages monotone.
Consider two stages that each negate a boolean, so that their composite is the
identity.  We order the booleans with false below true for $I$, $O$, and $M$.
Neither stage is monotone, since each maps true, the larger value, to false.
Reversing the order on $M$, so that true lies below false, makes both stages
monotone, and the composite is the identity either way
(Appendix~\ref{app:composition}).

\begin{proposition}[Jointly monotone composites]
\label{prop:joint-monotone}
The composite $P_2\circ P_1$ satisfies Lifted Monotonicity, so its runs are
consistent and it needs no \commitment, in each of the following cases.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item $\Spec_1$ and $\Spec_2$ satisfy Lifted Monotonicity.
  \item $\Spec_1$ satisfies Lifted Monotonicity; $\Spec_2$ satisfies it on
        $\Spec_1$'s outputs.
  \item $\Spec_1$ and $\Spec_2$ satisfy Lifted Monotonicity via a different order
        on $M$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
(1) is Lemma~\ref{lem:composition}.  (2) The proof of the lemma uses only
intermediate values in fibers of $\Spec_1$.  (3) The composite joins the two
tables on the intermediate value and never compares two intermediate values, so
its table does not depend on the order on $M$.  The lemma then applies under
the different order.  Full proof in Appendix~\ref{app:composition}.
\end{proof}

The three cases do not cover every monotone composite
(Appendix~\ref{app:composition}).  When no case applies, some stage may have to issue a \commitment even though the composite is monotone.

\subsection{Intersection}
\label{sec:intersection}

Sequential composition feeds the outputs of one spec to the inputs of the
next.  Specs can also be combined on a shared interface.  An application spec,
such as a replicated store that answers reads by a fixed rule, may promise
nothing about isolation, while a consistency property, such as an isolation
level, constrains the same reads.  A system that meets both may produce only
the rows that both permit.

\begin{definition}[Intersection]
\label{def:intersection}
Let $\Spec_1=(I,\InputOrd,O,\OutputOrd,P_1)$ and
$\Spec_2=(I,\InputOrd,O,\OutputOrd,P_2)$ share their ordered domains.  Their
\emph{intersection} $\Spec_1\cap\Spec_2$ has table $P_1\cap P_2$.  It is an
\spectype when $P_1\cap P_2$ is total on $I$: every input has an output that
both permit.
\end{definition}

The shared domains matter.  For example, isolation levels are defined on
histories of reads, writes, and commits, so an application spec must describe
its inputs and outputs in those terms before it can be intersected with one.

Intersection does not preserve Lifted Monotonicity.  Let $a$ and $b$ be
incomparable outputs, each below both $c$ and $d$, with $c$ and $d$
incomparable, and let both specs have a single input.  The fiber $\{a,b,c\}$ is
directed, since $c$ \outrel each of its members, and so is $\{a,b,d\}$.  The
intersection of the two fibers, $\{a,b\}$, is not: no output in it \outrel
both $a$ and $b$.  Two specs that need no \commitment can thus have an
intersection that does.  A
monotone bound rules this out.

\begin{proposition}[Intersection under a monotone bound]
\label{prop:intersection-bound}
Let $g:I\to O$ be monotone.  Suppose that every output $\Spec_1$ permits at an
input $j$ lies below $g(j)$, and that $\Spec_1$ and $\Spec_2$ both permit $g(j)$
at every $j$.  Then $\Spec_1\cap\Spec_2$ is an \spectype and satisfies Lifted
Monotonicity.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
The intersection permits $g(j)$ at every $j$, so it is total.  An output it
permits at $i\InputOrd j$ lies below $g(i)$, and $g(i)\OutputOrd g(j)$, so
$g(j)$ \outrel every output it permits at inputs below $j$.  Full proof in
Appendix~\ref{app:intersection}.
\end{proof}

A store whose answers follow a monotone rule therefore stays monotone when
intersected with any property that permits the rule's answers.
Section~\ref{sec:hat-instance} uses this construction for isolation levels and
a key-value store.
