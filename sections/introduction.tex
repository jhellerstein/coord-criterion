\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Concurrent systems rely on coordination mechanisms ranging from mutexes and
atomic instructions to database locks and consensus protocols.
These mechanisms prevent concurrent actions from producing undesirable
outcomes.  They also bring costs: overhead, latency, and
availability in distributed settings.  Avoiding unnecessary coordination
is therefore a central goal in distributed systems and databases.  This paper
asks what coordination mechanisms fundamentally accomplish, and
when that effect is intrinsically required.

The CALM Conjecture
proposed a crisp, bidirectional answer: consistency can be achieved without
coordination exactly when the specification is
monotone~\cite{hellerstein2010declarative}.  Ameloot et al.\ proved it in both
directions, but only for Datalog queries evaluated by relational
transducer networks~\cite{ameloot2013relational,hellerstein2020keeping}.  Gilbert and Lynch proved Brewer's CAP conjecture, but only for linearizable
registers~\cite{brewer2000towards,gilbert2002cap}.  Both results are
invoked far more broadly: CALM as a general rule that monotone programs
need no coordination, CAP as a general constraint on distributed systems.  The folklore outgrew the proofs.

The need for a common criterion extends beyond CALM and CAP.  CRDTs use
inflationary updates over join-semilattices for coordination-free
convergence~\cite{shapiro2011crdt}; HATs identify transactional guarantees
available under partition~\cite{bailis2013hat}; and invariant confluence asks
when independently reached database states merge safely~\cite{bailis2014coordination}.
Yet these results speak different formal languages, leaving unclear whether
CALM encompasses CAP, whether CRDT join laws are necessary, why only some
transactional isolation levels remain available during partition, and whether
invariant confluence differs from CALM (Section~\ref{sec:instances}).

Our answer begins with a simple observation.  In systems, as in life,
possibilities narrow over time.  Some are out of our hands: the world moves on,
and possibilities are lost.  Others are left for us to decide, and the
consequences vary.  Incidental decisions close no doors: any possibilities
they remove are carried forward by those that remain.  Fateful decisions make
some outcomes impossible: they exclude a possibility that nothing remaining
can carry forward.  Which specifications can be implemented without fateful
decisions?

The foundation of our results is a minimalist model of specification: a
\emph{table of possibilities} pairing inputs with permitted outputs.  The model is purely
declarative: it describes what can be observed, without fixing a language,
protocol, or placement.  The input domain $(I,\InputOrd)$ has a partial order:
$i\InputOrd j$ means that $j$ \inrel $i$ with more input from the environment.
The output domain $(O,\OutputOrd)$
has its own partial order: $o\OutputOrd u$ means that $u$ \outrel $o$, carrying
every claim in $o$ forward.
State-machine-style specifications can be compiled into this form via standard techniques
in formal verification,
eliding operational details that are not externally observable (\S\ref{sec:operational-models}).
Despite its simplicity, the model captures not only application invariants,
but also general-purpose consistency definitions such as linearizability and
causal consistency (Appendix~\ref{app:registers}), and transactional isolation
levels (\S\ref{sec:hat-instance}, Appendix~\ref{app:cc-bases}).

A specification is \emph{consistent} when the executions it admits are
\emph{confluent}: states reached along different paths can be brought to
a common state.

Our first result, \emph{Functional CALM}
(\S\ref{sec:functional-calm-theorem}), covers specifications that pair each
input with one output.  It proves that consistency is equivalent to
monotonicity: \(i\InputOrd j\) implies \(f(i)\OutputOrd f(j)\), so the output at
\(j\) \outrel any output emitted at \(i\).  The mnemonic is: \emph{Whenever we
learn, our answers refine.}  If monotonicity fails, some output requires a
fateful decision: an input exclusion that rules out later input which could
contradict it.  The result
covers relational query languages and the usual CRDT state specification.

The functional case imposes a functional dependency on the table of
possibilities, $\mathrm{in}\to\mathrm{out}$: each input permits one output.
Specifications like leader election and transaction ordering drop that
dependency, so the table may contain several rows with the same input.  Their
criterion also needs Directed Outputs, ensuring that output decisions are
incidental: whatever outputs different runs choose, some permitted output
carries them all forward.  Together, the conditions are
\emph{Lifted Monotonicity}, with two maxims: \emph{Whenever we learn, our
answers refine; whatever we choose, our answers align.}  \emph{Complete CALM}
proves that consistency is equivalent to Lifted Monotonicity
(\S\ref{sec:complete-calm-theorem}).

Distributed histories have the same consistency criterion
(Section~\ref{sec:distributed-execution}).  An exclusion \emph{requires
coordination} when another run could contradict it, and \emph{distributed
coordination} when that run is at another participant.  Complete CAP
(Section~\ref{sec:complete-cap}) then says that a specification with Lifted
Monotonicity can keep every permitted output available and confluent during a
partition; any other cannot.  The Gilbert--Lynch theorem for linearizable
registers follows directly.

This paper makes five contributions:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \emph{Ordered relational specifications.} Tables over ordered input and
        output domains describe permitted behavior; exclusions remove input or output possibilities.
  \item \emph{Consistency as confluence.} One definition covers general
        consistency models and application-specific requirements, and it is
        equivalent to distributed consistency.
  \item \emph{Functional CALM.} For functions between ordered domains,
        consistency is equivalent to monotonicity.
  \item \emph{Complete CALM and CAP.} In general, consistency is equivalent
        to Lifted Monotonicity, and Complete CAP gives the matching
        availability boundary under partition for any declared set of outputs
        that must stay available.
  \item \emph{Composition and Proper Coordination.} Specifications
        compose by equijoin, and we show that in a composition, upstream
        stages can free downstream stages from issuing exclusions (\S\ref{sec:composition}).
\end{enumerate}

The paper develops these contributions in order, then relates the criterion
to earlier results (Section~\ref{sec:instances}) and the broader CALM agenda
(Section~\ref{sec:discussion}).  The appendices provide proofs and further
applications; Appendix~\ref{app:notation} collects notation and defined terms.
