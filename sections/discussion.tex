\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Design lessons}

The criterion is diagnostic.  Before choosing a coordination mechanism, ask
four questions.

\paragraph{Did executions differ, or were several answers allowed?}
A functional specification fixes one output per input, but input may arrive
incompletely or in different orders; a relational specification also permits
several outputs at one input.  Monotonicity covers the first case, and Lifted
Monotonicity also covers the second.  Nondeterminism in an implementation does
not model permitted alternatives; they must appear as rows of the
specification.  Either way, useful output need not wait for determination: the
election can emit $\bot$ while either candidate may still win.

\paragraph{Was an alternative discovered impossible, or made impossible?}
Receiving a decisive ballot and closing the polls both leave a single result.
The first retires rows by input; the second removes rows that input still
permits, a commitment.  Classifying removals by their effect on the
specification, not by the operation's name, is what lets one criterion apply
across system models, and it leaves coordination two jobs: sealing input and
choosing among outputs (Proposition~\ref{prop:seal-or-choose}).

\paragraph{Did replicas converge, or did they preserve what they reported?}
Convergent state says nothing by itself about observations already returned: a
last-writer-wins object converges while replacing a visible value.  The output
order states which observations must be preserved, so a CRDT state, a read of
it, and a query over it can lie on different sides of the boundary
(Section~\ref{sec:crdt-instance}).

\paragraph{Was a mechanism used, or a commitment made?}
An uncontended lock excludes nothing, while a local controller may make a
binding choice without communicating.  Barriers, end-of-input markers, and
closing a round seal input; locks, timestamps, leader election, and consensus
choose among outputs.  Because commitments are explicit, \emph{Proper
Coordination} can test what a mechanism accomplished: committing a shared log
order makes the composed log and store monotone
(Appendix~\ref{app:composition}), without claiming that this mechanism is the
only way to get there.

\subsection{Scope}
\label{sec:consistency-as-confluence}
\label{sec:design-boundary}

Our conditions are semantic.  Confluence guarantees that a common \outnoun
exists, not that a deployed system can reach it: a participant may lack the
authority to emit it (Appendix~\ref{app:deployment}), and reaching it still
needs an algorithm, message delivery, and a termination argument.  Likewise,
participant runs set aside delivery and strategy details, so the equivalence
of consistency and distributed consistency preserves the confluence question,
not every implementation property.  A state machine induces a table by pairing
environment histories with their observable outputs.  Summarizing histories by
machine states is a modeling choice that the specification designer makes.  Protocols, liveness, and cost remain
the work of the operational model in which a specification is realized.

\subsection{A declarative foundation}

\emph{The Declarative Imperative} proposed that declarative database languages
could provide a foundation for distributed programming, and stated CALM in
terms of monotonic Datalog~\cite{hellerstein2010declarative}.  That left the
coordination question entangled with a program: its rules, language
extensions, and operational semantics.  Our specification table is declarative
at a lower layer: it says only which input/output behaviors are permitted and
which observations \outrelbase others.  In retrospect, the durable part of the
original thesis was not that distributed programs must be written in one
declarative language, but that the coordination boundary should be stated on a
monotone declarative object.  Here that object is the specification table.
