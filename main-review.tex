\begin{abstract}

\textcolor{reviewed}{When does a concurrent system intrinsically need coordination?} \textcolor{reviewed}{Too little
coordination produces heisenbugs; too much adds latency and sacrifices
availability.} \textcolor{reviewed}{CALM, CAP, and CRDTs each answer the question for one class of
programs, consistency models, or design patterns, in formalisms that cannot be
compared or combined, which leaves developers guessing.}

\textcolor{reviewed}{We put them on common ground, in a formalism that clarifies the literature and
offers new results.} \textcolor{reviewed}{A minimalist \spectype is a \emph{possibility table} containing, for
each input, every output a system may produce, with partial orders on inputs and
outputs.} \textcolor{reviewed}{It is \emph{consistent} when all its runs are confluent.} \textcolor{reviewed}{\emph{Functional CALM} generalizes the CALM Theorem from Datalog to any function
between ordered domains: consistency is monotonicity.} \textcolor{reviewed}{\emph{Complete CALM}
extends this to nondeterministic specifications, where the test is
\emph{Lifted Monotonicity}, and \emph{Complete CAP} follows as a corollary.} \textcolor{reviewed}{Highly Available Transactions, Invariant Confluence, and later refinements of
CALM map into the same framework.}

\textcolor{reviewed}{Prior CALM results stop at monotonicity: for non-monotone
specifications, they say only that coordination is needed somewhere.} Our
unified framework offers constructive results on both sides: it says exactly
how much coordination a specification needs, and where. \textcolor{reviewed}{A non-monotone
specification's outputs determine irrevocable \emph{\commitments} that are required for consistency,
enforceable by coordination mechanisms like locks, atomics, or consensus.} \textcolor{reviewed}{\emph{Proper Coordination} identifies which stage of a composition must make the
\commitments and certifies that later stages need none, validating designs such
as consensus logs in front of a replicated store.}

\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

\textcolor{reviewed}{Concurrent systems rely on coordination mechanisms ranging from mutexes and
atomic instructions to database locks and consensus protocols.} \textcolor{reviewed}{These mechanisms
prevent concurrent actions from producing undesirable outcomes, but they must be
used where they are needed, and ideally only there.} \textcolor{reviewed}{Too little coordination
produces heisenbugs: rare, timing-dependent anomalies that are hard to
reproduce.} \textcolor{reviewed}{Too much adds overhead and latency, and in distributed settings
sacrifices availability.} \textcolor{reviewed}{So when is coordination avoidable, and when is it
intrinsically required?} \textcolor{reviewed}{CALM~\cite{ameloot2013relational},
CAP~\cite{gilbert2002cap}, and CRDTs~\cite{shapiro2011crdt} each answer the
question for one class of programs, consistency models, or design patterns, in
formalisms that cannot be compared or combined.} \textcolor{reviewed}{This leaves developers
guessing.}

\textcolor{reviewed}{The CALM Conjecture proposed a crisp, bidirectional criterion:
consistency can be achieved without coordination exactly when a specification
is monotone~\cite{hellerstein2010declarative}.} \textcolor{reviewed}{But the original proof applied
only in a restricted setting: Datalog queries evaluated by relational transducer
networks~\cite{ameloot2013relational,hellerstein2020keeping}.} \textcolor{reviewed}{A related
question comes from the CAP conjecture: which consistency guarantees can remain
available when participants cannot communicate?} \textcolor{reviewed}{There, the original proof
applied in a different restricted setting: linearizable
registers~\cite{brewer2000towards,gilbert2002cap}.} \textcolor{reviewed}{Both results are invoked
quite broadly: CALM as a general rule that monotone programs need no
coordination, CAP as a general constraint on distributed systems.} \textcolor{reviewed}{The folklore
outgrew the proofs.}

\textcolor{reviewed}{The apparent connection between CALM and CAP was never established in a common
formalism, and nearby results only complicate the picture.} \textcolor{reviewed}{CRDTs use
inflationary updates over join-semilattices for coordination-free
convergence~\cite{shapiro2011crdt}; HATs identify transactional guarantees
available under partition~\cite{bailis2013hat}; invariant confluence asks when
independently reached database states merge
safely~\cite{bailis2014coordination}.} \textcolor{reviewed}{The diverse formalisms leave cross-cutting
questions unclear, including whether CALM encompasses CAP, whether CRDT join
laws are necessary, why only some transactional isolation levels remain
available during partition, and whether invariant confluence differs from CALM
(Section~\ref{sec:instances}).}

\textcolor{reviewed}{We put these results on common ground, in a formalism that clarifies the
literature and offers new results.} \textcolor{reviewed}{Prior CALM results stop at the monotone
boundary: for a non-monotone program, they say only that coordination is needed
somewhere, not what it must accomplish or where it belongs.} \textcolor{reviewed}{Few real systems
are monotone throughout, so that is where developers most need help.} \textcolor{reviewed}{Our
framework is constructive on both sides of the boundary.} \textcolor{reviewed}{It answers questions
the literature leaves open: when nondeterministic specifications require
coordination, what that coordination must achieve, and how to tell whether it is
properly placed.}

\subsection{The Model: \Spectype[s]}
\textcolor{reviewed}{In systems, as in life, our possibilities narrow over time.} \textcolor{reviewed}{Sometimes the world
moves on, and possibilities are lost to us.} \textcolor{reviewed}{Sometimes possibilities disappear
because we must decide among them.} \textcolor{reviewed}{A decision is incidental when
every possibility it removes is covered by an alternative that remains.} \textcolor{reviewed}{A
decision is fateful when it rules out a possibility that no remaining
alternative can cover.} \textcolor{reviewed}{When must a system make a fateful decision?}

\textcolor{reviewed}{We ground the problem in a simple model of \emph{\spectype[s]}, or \emph{specs}
for short, independent of any language, protocol, or placement.} \textcolor{reviewed}{The designer of
a spec provides a \emph{possibility relation} $P\subseteq I\times O$: a virtual
table that may be infinite, like a mathematical function.} \textcolor{reviewed}{Each row in this table
is a possibility observable from the outside: it pairs the input received so far
with a permitted output.} \textcolor{reviewed}{The design includes the input domain $I$ and output domain $O$,
with partial orders that help capture the spec's semantics.} \textcolor{reviewed}{On inputs, the order
is accumulation: $i\InputOrd j$ means that $j$ \emph{\inrel} $i$ with more input
from the environment.} \textcolor{reviewed}{On outputs, the order is subsumption: $o\OutputOrd u$
means that  $u$ \emph{\outrel} $o$ by subsuming all spec-relevant information in
$o$.} \textcolor{reviewed}{These are both application-specific notions that must be captured well for
a spec to be faithful.}

\textcolor{reviewed}{State-machine-style specs can be projected into this form, by skipping
operational details that are not externally observable (Section~\ref{sec:operational-models}).} Despite its simplicity, our model captures not only application invariants,
but also general-purpose consistency definitions (Appendix~\ref{app:registers},
Section~\ref{sec:hat-instance}, Appendix~\ref{app:cc-bases}).

\textcolor{reviewed}{As a system runs, possibilities narrow: the spec's table loses rows.} \textcolor{reviewed}{When new
input arrives, the world has moved on, and rows that cannot be reached by
extending that input are lost.} \textcolor{reviewed}{When the system decides among possibilities, it
makes a controlled decision to remove rows.} \textcolor{reviewed}{The decision is incidental if each
removed output is covered by a remaining output that \outrel it, and fateful if
not.} \textcolor{reviewed}{Choosing between two candidates in a leader election is fateful: nothing
remaining covers the loser's possibility of a win.} \textcolor{reviewed}{So is closing the polls,
which rules out every row with a later ballot.} \textcolor{reviewed}{We call a fateful decision a
\emph{\commitment}: an irrevocable decision, enforceable by coordination
mechanisms such as locks and consensus.}

\subsection{The Criterion: Lifted Monotonicity}
\textcolor{reviewed}{The model provides clean definitions for the key ingredients of CALM:
Consistency and Monotonicity.} \textcolor{reviewed}{A spec is \emph{consistent} when all its possible
executions are \emph{confluent}: states reached along different paths can be
brought to a common state.} \textcolor{reviewed}{We will see that this definition is general.} \textcolor{reviewed}{The spec is a parameter:
specs and confluence together capture a range of classical consistency models,
from linearizability, through serializability and other transactional isolation
levels, to the eventual consistency guarantees of replicated data.}

\textcolor{reviewed}{For a spec that permits exactly one output per input, the possibility table is a
function, and monotonicity is standard: $i\InputOrd j$ implies
$f(i)\OutputOrd f(j)$.} \textcolor{reviewed}{\emph{Functional CALM} (Section~\ref{sec:functional-calm-theorem})
proves that consistency is equivalent to monotonicity in this setting, which
covers prior CALM work and CRDT state.} \textcolor{reviewed}{Many specs are nondeterministic: leader
election and transaction scheduling permit several outputs for one input.} \textcolor{reviewed}{These need a second invariant: whatever outputs different runs choose at the
same input, some permitted output must \outrelbase them all.} \textcolor{reviewed}{Together, the two
invariants make every output decision incidental, and we call the pair
\emph{Lifted Monotonicity}.} \textcolor{reviewed}{\emph{Complete CALM} (Section~\ref{sec:complete-calm-theorem})
proves that Consistency is equivalent to Lifted Monotonicity, a new reading of
the CALM acronym.}

\subsection{Coordination is for \commitment}
\textcolor{reviewed}{What of coordination?} \textcolor{reviewed}{A \commitment isolates the contract a coordination
mechanism achieves, apart from how it achieves it: locks, agreement protocols,
and other mechanisms all enforce \commitments.} \textcolor{reviewed}{Our criterion identifies exactly
the \commitments a spec's outputs require, independently of the mechanism used.} \textcolor{reviewed}{A system that omits a required \commitment can become inconsistent; one that makes more \commitments coordinates needlessly.} \textcolor{reviewed}{In a composition of specs, \emph{Proper Coordination}
identifies which stage must make the \commitments and certifies that later
stages need none.} \textcolor{reviewed}{In state-machine replication, for example, the consensus log
makes every \commitment and the replicated store makes none
(Section~\ref{sec:composition}).}

Placement determines whether conflicting choices can arise at different
participants. \textcolor{reviewed}{Distributed histories are captured under the same consistency criteria as
centralized ones (Section~\ref{sec:distributed-execution}).} \textcolor{reviewed}{Complete CAP
(Section~\ref{sec:complete-cap}) says Lifted Monotonicity exactly characterizes specs
that can keep every permitted output available and confluent during a
partition.} \textcolor{reviewed}{The Gilbert--Lynch theorem follows directly.}

\subsection{Contributions}
\textcolor{reviewed}{We make contributions in four categories:}

\textcolor{reviewed}{\noindent
\emph{1. A unified model.} A system's observable behavior is captured via
        \spectype[s].} \textcolor{reviewed}{Consistency is defined as
        confluence of a spec's runs, unifying general consistency models,
        application-specific requirements, and distributed consistency.}

\textcolor{reviewed}{\noindent
\emph{2. A unified criterion.} For functions between ordered domains,
        Functional CALM shows that consistency is equivalent to monotonicity.} \textcolor{reviewed}{For nondeterministic specs,
        we prove Complete CALM: Consistency As Lifted Monotonicity.} \textcolor{reviewed}{Complete CAP is a corollary:
        the CAP tradeoff holds only under failure of Lifted Monotonicity.}

\textcolor{reviewed}{\noindent
\emph{3. Constructive results for coordination.} We define \commitments,
        the underlying contracts that coordination mechanisms can enforce,
        and identify exactly the \commitments a non-monotone spec requires.} \textcolor{reviewed}{Specs compose by equijoin, and Proper Coordination identifies the stage
        that must make the \commitments, certifying that later stages need
        none.}

\textcolor{reviewed}{\noindent
\emph{4. A map of the literature.} Highly Available Transactions, Invariant
        Confluence, CRDT observation, and later refinements of CALM are all
        explained via Complete CALM (Section~\ref{sec:instances}).}

\vspace{0.5em}
\noindent
The paper develops these contributions in order, then relates the criterion to
earlier results (Section~\ref{sec:instances}) and the broader CALM agenda
(Section~\ref{sec:discussion}). The appendices provide proofs and further
applications; Appendix~\ref{app:notation} collects notation and defined terms.

\textcolor{reviewed}{We introduce the framework with a small election, illustrated in
Figure~\ref{fig:election-frames}.} \textcolor{reviewed}{Each row of the possibility table pairs
the ballots received so far with the permitted result; the column orders say how inputs \ingrow and which later outputs \outrelbase earlier ones.}

\textcolor{reviewed}{Three voters $v_1,v_2,v_3$ each cast at most one immutable ballot for $a$ or
$b$.} \textcolor{reviewed}{The output is whichever candidate has two ballots, or $\bot$ while there
is no winner.} \textcolor{reviewed}{An input is a vector of the ballots received so far, one entry per voter, with a
dot for a ballot not yet received.} \textcolor{reviewed}{We write it as a string: $ab\cdot$ means
$v_1$ voted $a$, $v_2$ voted $b$, and $v_3$'s ballot has not arrived.} \textcolor{reviewed}{For brevity,
the figure shows only inputs \inrelpart $a\cdot\cdot$.}

\textcolor{reviewed}{The two orders require thoughtful design.} \textcolor{reviewed}{The \emph{input order}
must record accumulated ballots: $ab\cdot$ \inrel $a\cdot\cdot$, while
$aa\cdot$ and $ab\cdot$ are incomparable.} \textcolor{reviewed}{The \emph{output order} says which
announcements are safe to build on: $\{\bot \OutputOrd a, \bot \OutputOrd b\}$.} \textcolor{reviewed}{$\bot$ claims nothing, so either winner
\outrel it, while $a$ and $b$ are incomparable.} \textcolor{reviewed}{In general, the functional table is \emph{monotone}
when}
\[
  i\InputOrd j\quad\Longrightarrow\quad f(i)\OutputOrd f(j).
\]
\textcolor{reviewed}{Here, for example, the input sequence $a\cdot\cdot\InputOrd ab\cdot\InputOrd aba$
monotonically produces the output sequence $\bot\OutputOrd\bot\OutputOrd a$.}

\paragraph{Input and determination.}
\textcolor{reviewed}{Each panel of Figure~\ref{fig:election-frames} shows a \emph{specification
state}: the \emph{current input} and the \emph{remainder table} of rows still
possible from it.} \textcolor{reviewed}{When $v_2$'s ballot for $b$ arrives, the input event
$\mathsf{input}(ab\cdot)$ produces panel~(b).} \textcolor{reviewed}{The table passively loses the rows
whose inputs do not extend $ab\cdot$, such as $aa\cdot$: those possibilities are
lost.} \textcolor{reviewed}{The \emph{upper fiber} (Definition~\ref{def:fiber}) is the set of outputs still possible in the
remainder table: $\{\bot,a,b\}$ in panel~(b).} \textcolor{reviewed}{If $v_2$ instead votes for $a$
(panel~(c)), the upper fiber is a singleton, $\{a\}$, and we call such
states \emph{determined}.} \textcolor{reviewed}{In this case, input alone has determined an outcome,
without \commitment.}

\textcolor{reviewed}{Panel~(d), by contrast, adds a decision by the \emph{system}: the implementation
that receives the ballots and announces the result (Section~\ref{sec:systems}).} \textcolor{reviewed}{Here
the system acts as a registrar that, when it emits $\bot$ at $ab\cdot$, also
closes the polls.} \textcolor{reviewed}{Closing the polls is a \emph{\commitment}: it removes the
still-possible rows $aba$ and $abb$, though the current input stays $ab\cdot$.} \textcolor{reviewed}{The upper fiber becomes $\{\bot\}$, so $\bot$ is the determined result.} \textcolor{reviewed}{The spec did not require this \commitment: emitting $\bot$ alone would remove
nothing, since every output still possible extends $\bot$.} \textcolor{reviewed}{Closing the polls is
the registrar's own fateful decision.}

\paragraph{Output and consistency.}
\textcolor{reviewed}{Emitting $o$ is an \emph{output event} $\mathsf{output}(o)$: it keeps only the
rows whose outputs \outrelbase $o$, since later outputs must not contradict it.} \textcolor{reviewed}{In a functional table, $o$ is a \emph{legal output} when everything in the
upper fiber already \outrel $o$, so the event removes nothing.} \textcolor{reviewed}{Thus
$\bot$ is legal in panels~(a) and~(b), and $a$ in panel~(c).} \textcolor{reviewed}{When $o$ is not
legal, the event removes the rows that conflict with $o$, ruling out their
inputs; that removal is a \commitment.}

\textcolor{reviewed}{Figure~\ref{fig:nonmonotone-frame} considers a variant of our example that
changes only the output order: all outputs are incomparable, including $\bot$
for no winner.} \textcolor{reviewed}{Under this order (known as the \emph{equality order}), $\bot$ is
incompatible with future winners, so emitting it requires a \commitment.} \textcolor{reviewed}{That
change in the output order is Functional CALM in miniature: the spec is monotone
under the original order, so later outputs \outrelbase every earlier one; under
the equality order it is non-monotone, and some outputs can be emitted only with a \commitment.}

\textcolor{reviewed}{To see why, we follow the events.} \textcolor{reviewed}{A \emph{run} is a sequence of input and output
events from a starting state, like the sequences that produce the panels of
Figure~\ref{fig:election-frames}.} \textcolor{reviewed}{Under the equality order, rows
$(ab\cdot,\bot)$ and $(aba,a)$ have the common \indesc $aba$, but no output at
$aba$ \outrel both $\bot$ and $a$.} \textcolor{reviewed}{So a run that emits $\bot$ at $ab\cdot$ and
a run that receives $aba$ and emits $a$ can never be reconciled at $aba$.} \textcolor{reviewed}{Emitting $\bot$ is safe only with the \commitment that rules out $aba$ and
$abb$; without it, the two runs would diverge for good.} \textcolor{reviewed}{This argument uses only
the table's rows and its two orders, so the need for the \commitment is a
property of the spec, not of any system that implements it.}

\textcolor{reviewed}{Runs are the foundation of the consistency side of CALM.} \textcolor{reviewed}{Input and output
events form a transition system over specification states, and a spec is
\emph{consistent} when its runs are \emph{confluent}: runs whose inputs have a
common \indesc can always be continued to a common state there.} \textcolor{reviewed}{Section~\ref{sec:functional-calm-theorem} shows that for functional tables consistency corresponds exactly to
monotonicity.} Appendix~\ref{app:notation} collects the notation for reference.

\subsection{Specifications and states}
\label{sec:functional-specifications}

\begin{definition}[Functional \spectype, total]
\label{def:functional-specification}
\textcolor{reviewed}{A \emph{functional \spectype} is a tuple}
\[
  \Spec=(I,\InputOrd,O,\OutputOrd,P),
\]
\textcolor{reviewed}{where $(I,\InputOrd)$ and $(O,\OutputOrd)$ are partially ordered input and
  output types, and $P\subseteq I\times O$ is its \emph{possibility relation}:
  a typed relation with schema $(\mathrm{in},\mathrm{out})$ in which every
  input appears in at least one row ($P$ is \emph{total} on $I$) and at most one
  (a functional dependency):}
\[
  (i,o)\in P\ \land\ (i,o')\in P
  \quad\Longrightarrow\quad o=o'.
\]
\end{definition}

\textcolor{reviewed}{The unique output paired with $i$ induces the function}
\[f:(I,\InputOrd)\to(O,\OutputOrd)\]
with $P=\{(i,f(i)):i\in I\}.$
We call
$\InputOrd$ the \emph{input order}. Because an output may depend on everything
a system has received, we model inputs as \emph{cumulative}: an input
records everything received so far, not a single message, and $i\InputOrd j$
means that $j$ contains at least the input information in $i$. We say that $j$
\emph{\inrel} $i$, or is an \emph{\indesc} of $i$; every input is an \indesc of itself. \textcolor{reviewed}{We call $\OutputOrd$ the
\emph{output order}: $o\OutputOrd u$ means that $u$ subsumes every
externally visible claim in $o$ and may add more, and we say that $u$
\emph{\outrel} $o$.} In either domain, an element above another is an extension of it. We write $\InputGe$ and $\OutputGe$ for the converse orders.

A \emph{common \indesc} $j$ of
$i_1,\ldots,i_k$ is an input that \inrel every $i_r$, and a
\emph{common \outnoun} of $o_1,\ldots,o_k$ is an output that \outrel each $o_r$.

A row $(k,u)$ is \emph{\outrelpast} by another row $(k,w)$ at the same input
when $w$ \outrel $u$.

\begin{definition}[Functional monotonicity]
\label{def:functional-monotonicity}
A functional \spectype $\Spec$, with induced function $f$, is
\emph{monotone} if
\begin{equation}
  i\InputOrd j
  \quad\Longrightarrow\quad
  f(i)\OutputOrd f(j).
  \label{eq:functional-monotonicity}
\end{equation}
\end{definition}

Thus an output emitted on partial input is \outrelpast by the output at every \indesc.

\paragraph{Relational notation.}
For a relation $R\subseteq I\times O$, we say that a row $(k,u)$ is
\emph{at input} $k$. A predicate on the columns
$\mathrm{in}$ and $\mathrm{out}$ in square brackets selects rows, and commas
separate conjuncts: $R[\mathrm{in}\InputGe j,\ \mathrm{out}\OutputGe o]$ is the
table of rows whose input \inrel $j$ and whose output \outrel $o$. \textcolor{reviewed}{As in
relational algebra, $\pi_{\mathrm{out}}(R)$ is the set of outputs that appear in
$R$.}

A spec describes every permitted input/output pair. During execution,
input arrives and outputs are emitted, so only some of those pairs remain
possible. The changing state of the spec, illustrated by the non-gray text in
the panels of Figure~\ref{fig:election-frames}, is defined as follows.

\begin{definition}[Specification state, current input, remainder table]
\label{def:residual-state}
Given a specification $\Spec=(I,\InputOrd,O,\OutputOrd,P)$, a \emph{specification state} is a
pair $\sigma=(i,R)$, where $i\in I$ is the \emph{current input} and
$R\subseteq P$ is the \emph{remainder table}, such that:
\begin{enumerate}
\item
$R$ contains a row at $i$; and
\item
$R$ contains only rows at \indescs of $i$: every row $(k,o)\in R$ satisfies $i\InputOrd k$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Legal input]
\label{def:legal-input}
An input $k$ is \emph{legal} at a state $\sigma=(i,R)$ when $R$ contains a row at $k$.
\end{definition}

\textcolor{reviewed}{Rows are never added during execution.} The \emph{canonical state at $i$} is
the state at $i$ with no rows removed: it keeps every row of $P$ at $i$ and its
\indescs, and it is the state of a system that has received $i$ and emitted
nothing:
\begin{equation}
  \sigma_P(i)=\bigl(i,\ P[\mathrm{in}\InputGe i]\bigr).
  \label{eq:canonical-state}
\end{equation}
An input event from a canonical state leads to another canonical state, and
runs start at one.

\textcolor{reviewed}{Two sets of outputs recur throughout: those a table permits at a given input,
and those it still permits there or once more input arrives.} \textcolor{reviewed}{In panel~(b) of
Figure~\ref{fig:election-frames}, the first is $\{\bot\}$ at $ab\cdot$, and the
second, shaded, is $\{\bot,a,b\}$.}

\begin{definition}[Fiber, upper fiber]
\label{def:fiber}
\textcolor{reviewed}{Let $R$ be a table and $k$ an input.} \textcolor{reviewed}{The \emph{fiber} of $R$ at $k$ is
$R(k)=\pi_{\mathrm{out}}(R[\mathrm{in}=k])$, the outputs paired with $k$.} \textcolor{reviewed}{The
\emph{upper fiber} of $R$ at $k$ is $\pi_{\mathrm{out}}(R[\mathrm{in}\InputGe
k])$, the outputs paired with $k$ or any of its \indescs.}
\end{definition}

A functional fiber has at most one value. \textcolor{reviewed}{For a state $\sigma=(i,R)$, the
upper fiber at $i$ is $\pi_{\mathrm{out}}(R)$: every output still possible.}

\subsection{Input and output events}
\label{sec:functional-events}

Two events change state: input events incorporate new information, while
output events record what the system has emitted. Each may remove rows from
the remainder table.

\paragraph{Input events.}
For a state $(i,R)$ and legal input $j$ with $i\InputOrd j$, the input event is
\begin{equation}
  (i,R)\xrightarrow{\mathsf{input}(j)}
  \bigl(j,\ R' = R[\mathrm{in}\InputGe j]\bigr).
  \label{eq:input-event}
\end{equation}
It makes $j$ current and causes the remainder table to lose the rows whose
input does not \inrelbase $j$. We call this \emph{input loss}.

An input event names the cumulative input $j$: everything received so far,
including all of $i$, not just what arrived since $i$. Thus $\mathsf{input}(j)$ then $\mathsf{input}(k)$ has the
effect of $\mathsf{input}(k)$ alone; $\mathsf{input}(i)$ at
$(i,R)$ has no effect.

\paragraph{Output events.}
A system controls what it tells the world, but each later output must
\outrelbase the earlier ones.

At state $(i,R)$, the current input has a unique row $(i,o)$, so $o$ is the only
output the system may emit.

\begin{definition}[Legal output]
\label{def:exposable}
\textcolor{reviewed}{The output $o$ at $(i,R)$ is a \emph{legal output} when every output in the
upper fiber \outrel $o$.}
\end{definition}

Equivalently, since the only row at a legal input $j$ is $(j,f(j))$, $o$ is
legal exactly when $f(j)$ \outrel $o$ for every legal input $j$.

Since every later output must \outrelbase $o$, emitting a non-legal $o$ requires
a \commitment that rules out every legal input whose output does not
\outrelbase it.

\begin{definition}[Output event]
\label{def:output-event}
\textcolor{reviewed}{At a state $(i,R)$ with current output $o$, the \emph{output event}
$\mathsf{output}(o)$ performs two steps atomically: it keeps exactly the rows
whose outputs \outrelbase $o$, and emits $o$ to the environment:}
\begin{equation}
  (i,R)\xrightarrow{\mathsf{output}(o)}\bigl(i,\ R' = R[\mathrm{out}\OutputGe o]\bigr).
  \label{eq:output-action}
\end{equation}
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Canonical \commitment]
\label{def:canonical-commitment-functional}
When $o$ is not legal, equivalently $R'\neq R$, the transition applies the
\emph{canonical \commitment} $x_o(i,R)=(i,R')$. For every row it removes, no
row remains at the same input. It is a \commitment.
\end{definition}

\textcolor{reviewed}{Thus $o$ is legal exactly when $\mathsf{output}(o)$ applies no \commitment.} The
current row $(i,o)$ remains, since $o$ \outrel itself, so the event always ends
at a specification state. A \commitment removes rows that input alone would
keep; after the canonical \commitment, the inputs of the removed rows are no
longer legal. This section uses only canonical \commitments. Systems
may issue more \commitments than an output requires, as the registrar does in
Figure~\ref{fig:election-frames}(d), but such extra \commitments are a
system's choice, not the spec's (Section~\ref{sec:systems}).

\paragraph{Determination.}
A state is \emph{determined} when its upper fiber has one element: one result
remains, whatever legal input still arrives. This is a property of the state
alone. Under the functional dependency each input determines its own
output; a determined state goes further, since its current input determines
the output of every input that may still arrive. A functional spec
can therefore have undetermined states, as in panel~(b) of
Figure~\ref{fig:election-frames}. \emph{Determination} is the process of
reaching a determined state. An input event can do so through input loss, as in panel~(c), and a
\commitment can do so by emptying fibers that would otherwise remain possible,
as in panel~(d). An event from a determined state always leads to another determined state:
events only remove rows, and a row at the current input always remains.

\subsection{Executions and consistency}
\label{sec:executions}

When runs take different paths through the spec, can they still be
brought to a common state? The answer reduces to a check on a single row.

\begin{definition}[Specification event]
\label{def:specification-event}
Input and output events are \emph{specification events}: they change the
specification state. We call them simply \emph{events} when unambiguous.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Transition system]
\label{def:transition-system}
The \emph{transition system} consists of specification states and
specification events.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Canonical run]
\label{def:run}
\textcolor{reviewed}{A \emph{canonical run}, or just \emph{run}, is any finite transition sequence of the transition system from the
canonical state $\sigma_P(i)$ at some input $i$.}
\end{definition}

We compare a system's choices with every behavior the declared spec permits,
not only the behaviors that system happens to produce.

A run is \emph{\commitment-free} when none of its output events applies a
\commitment. A state is \emph{reachable without \commitment} when
some \commitment-free run reaches it.

A run's state has a simple description. Input and output events are both
relational selections, and selections commute. Since inputs only \ingrow during a run, the selection for the last
input subsumes the earlier ones.

\begin{lemma}[Rows remaining after a run]
\label{lem:run-residual}
If a run ends at current input $i$ after emitting
$o_1,\ldots,o_m$, its remainder table is
\begin{equation}
  P[\mathrm{in}\InputGe i,\ \mathrm{out}\OutputGe o_1,\ \ldots,\
    \mathrm{out}\OutputGe o_m],
  \label{eq:run-residual}
\end{equation}
the rows whose input \inrel $i$ and whose output \outrel each $o_r$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
See Appendix~\ref{app:proofs-sec2}.
\end{proof}

For example, in the equality-order variant of
Figure~\ref{fig:nonmonotone-frame}, a run that emits $\bot$ at $ab\cdot$ keeps
$P[\mathrm{in}\InputGe ab\cdot,\ \mathrm{out}\OutputGe\bot]$: the single row
$(ab\cdot,\bot)$, as the figure shows.

To compare runs that end at different inputs, we consider a common \indesc
of those inputs.

\begin{definition}[Confluence at an input]
\label{def:confluence}
\textcolor{reviewed}{A finite family of runs ending at states $(i_1,R_1),\ldots,(i_k,R_k)$ is \emph{confluent at} $j$, a common \indesc of their current inputs, when each can be continued to a common state with current input $j$.}
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Consistency]
\label{def:consistency}
An \spectype is \emph{consistent} when every finite family of
runs is confluent at every common \indesc of their current inputs.
\end{definition}

Consistency requires confluence at every common \indesc, not just at some
later one, because conflicting outputs could otherwise escape before the runs
reconcile. In the equality-order election, $f(ab\cdot)=\bot$ and $f(aba)=a$ are
incomparable. One run can emit $\bot$ at $ab\cdot$, while another accepts
$aba$ and can emit $a$. The two runs cannot reconcile at their common \indesc $aba$, and waiting cannot
retract either output from the environment. More generally, even when later
input could reconcile the internal states, the environment may already have
acted on the outputs through channels the system does not see, as in Lamport's
``anomalous behavior''~\cite{lamport1978time}, and the reconciling input may
never arrive.

Confluence has a simple test: some row at the common \indesc must survive
in every run.

\begin{proposition}[Survivor test]
\label{prop:confluence}
Runs ending at states $(i_r,R_r)$, $r=1,\ldots,k$, are confluent
at $j$ if and only if some row at $j$ survives in every final state:
\begin{equation}
  (j,u)\in R_r\quad\text{for every }r,\ \text{for some }u\in\fiber{j}.
  \label{eq:reconciliation}
\end{equation}
\textcolor{reviewed}{For any such $u$, $\mathsf{input}(j)$ followed by $\mathsf{output}(u)$ takes
every final state to the state
$\bigl(j,\ P[\mathrm{in}\InputGe j,\ \mathrm{out}\OutputGe u]\bigr)$.}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
\textcolor{reviewed}{Rows are never added, so the row of a common state at $j$ was there
in every run.} Full proof in Appendix~\ref{app:proofs-sec2}.
\end{proof}

In a functional spec the only row at $j$ is $(j,f(j))$, so the test
asks whether $(j,f(j))$ survives in every run.

Consistency guarantees that runs never diverge irreparably: there is no
``split brain.''  Whatever events a finite collection of runs has taken, at any
input \inrelpart all of their inputs there is a state that every run can reach. That state honors all of their emitted outputs and accepts all of that input,
and Proposition~\ref{prop:confluence} identifies it. Consistency is a
property of the spec, not of a protocol (Section~\ref{sec:scope}).

\subsection{The Functional CALM theorem}
\label{sec:functional-calm-theorem}

\textcolor{reviewed}{Monotonicity is defined by a spec's table and orders alone, consistency by its
runs.} \textcolor{reviewed}{Functional CALM shows that these coincide.}

\begin{theorem}[Functional CALM]
\label{thm:functional-calm}
\textcolor{reviewed}{A functional \spectype is consistent if and only if it is
monotone.}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
A monotone $f$ only emits outputs that all of its future outputs \outrelbase,
so the row $(j,f(j))$ at a common \indesc $j$ survives in every run. If $f$ is not monotone, a run that emits $f(i)$ and a run that accepts an
\indesc $k$ of $i$ with $f(i)\not\OutputOrd f(k)$ can never be reconciled at $k$. Full proof in Appendix~\ref{app:proofs-sec2}.
\end{proof}

In the functional case, consistency has a strong consequence: emitting outputs
never changes the state, which depends only on the input.

\begin{corollary}[State is fixed by input]
\label{cor:state-by-input}
\textcolor{reviewed}{In a consistent functional specification, every run ending at current
input $i$ is in the canonical state $\sigma_P(i)$, whatever outputs it emitted
along the way.}
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
\textcolor{reviewed}{By monotonicity, the outputs a run emits remove no row at an \indesc
of $i$.} Full proof in Appendix~\ref{app:proofs-sec2}.
\end{proof}

To be confluent at a common \indesc $j$, each run therefore needs only the
single event $\mathsf{input}(j)$, which takes it to $\sigma_P(j)$, the state of a
run that starts with all of the input. Knowing the input is enough to know the state, and with it the one
output $f(j)$.

\textcolor{reviewed}{Monotonicity also governs legality:}

\begin{proposition}[No-\commitment test]
\label{prop:functional-exposure-boundary}
\textcolor{reviewed}{A functional \spectype is monotone if and only if every output at
every state reachable without \commitment is legal.}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
By Corollary~\ref{cor:state-by-input}, monotone $f$ reaches only canonical
states. At $\sigma_P(i)$, which input alone reaches, $f(i)$ is legal exactly
when $f$ is monotone at $i$. Full proof in Appendix~\ref{app:proofs-sec2}.
\end{proof}

A monotone spec can thus emit every current output without \commitment. If a
spec is non-monotone, then for some $i\InputOrd k$,
$f(i)\not\OutputOrd f(k)$, so at $\sigma_P(i)$ the event
$\mathsf{output}(f(i))$ applies $x_{f(i)}$ and rules out input $k$.

\textcolor{reviewed}{Functional CALM is the familiar CALM dichotomy stated at the level of the spec
rather than inside a particular programming language.} \textcolor{reviewed}{Classical CALM fits, with inputs $J$ as sets of facts ordered by
inclusion and output views $Q(J)$ ordered by inclusion.} \textcolor{reviewed}{Functional
monotonicity becomes}
        \begin{equation}
  J\subseteq J'
  \quad\Longrightarrow\quad
  Q(J)\subseteq Q(J'),
  \label{eq:query-monotonicity}
        \end{equation}
\textcolor{reviewed}{the standard definition of a monotone query~\cite{abiteboul1995foundations};
positive Datalog satisfies it, and negation may not.} \textcolor{reviewed}{Functional CALM
generalizes the statement to arbitrary input and
output orders, whatever formalism expresses the spec.} \textcolor{reviewed}{CRDT state specs have the same functional shape, with received payloads ordered by inclusion and states by the semilattice order~\cite{shapiro2011crdt}; Sections~\ref{sec:query-instance}
and~\ref{sec:crdt-instance} treat both.}

\section{Complete CALM}
\label{sec:complete-calm}

\textcolor{reviewed}{We now extend CALM to nondeterministic specs, whose fibers may contain several outputs: we define Lifted Monotonicity (\S\ref{sec:relational-specifications}), lift output events (\S\ref{sec:lifted-output-events}), and prove Complete CALM (\S\ref{sec:complete-calm-theorem}).}

\subsection{Nondeterministic specs and Lifted Monotonicity}
\label{sec:relational-specifications}

\begin{definition}[\Spectype]
\label{def:relational-specification}
\textcolor{reviewed}{An \emph{\spectype} is}
        \begin{equation}
  \Spec=(I,\InputOrd,O,\OutputOrd,P),
  \qquad P\subseteq I\times O,
  \label{eq:relational-specification}
        \end{equation}
where $(I,\InputOrd)$ and $(O,\OutputOrd)$ are partially ordered sets and
  $P\subseteq I\times O$ is its \emph{possibility relation}. We depict $P$ as
a table and call its members rows; it need not be finite. The relation $P$
  is total on $I$: every input appears in at least one row. \textcolor{reviewed}{A row $(i,o)\in P$
  is a possible input/output pair: output $o$ is permitted at input $i$.} As in
  Section~\ref{sec:functional-specifications}, the fiber at $i$ is
        \begin{equation}
  \fiber{i}=\{o\in O:(i,o)\in P\},
  \label{eq:fiber}
        \end{equation}
and it may contain several outputs. \textcolor{reviewed}{A functional \spectype is the special
  case $\fiber{i}=\{f(i)\}$.}
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Nondeterministic \spectype]
\label{def:nondeterministic}
An \spectype is \emph{nondeterministic} when some fiber
contains more than one output. Specification nondeterminism describes
which results are permitted, not how a system chooses among them, and does not
by itself imply a \commitment.
\end{definition}

Specification states and input events
(Sections~\ref{sec:functional-specifications} and~\ref{sec:functional-events})
apply unchanged to relational tables.

\textcolor{reviewed}{Figure~\ref{fig:ambiguity-frames} contrasts two specs with several outputs in
one fiber: serial schedules for two transactions and route sets observed by a
traceroute-like tool.} \textcolor{reviewed}{For the discussion here, transaction outputs are simply
serial schedules, to which executions correspond via equivalence;
Section~\ref{sec:hat-instance} and Appendix~\ref{app:cc-bases} study more
thorough transactional specs.}

In the transaction spec, no output \outrel both $[A,B]$ and $[B,A]$, so
emitting either requires a \commitment that removes the other. In the
traceroute spec, every route set that an output removes is \outrelpast by a
remaining one that also \outrelbase the emitted observation, so the removal is
an \emph{output subsumption}, not a \commitment. Lifted Monotonicity, defined
below, combines two requirements. Relational Monotonicity accounts for input
growth: each output keeps an \outnoun as input \ingrows. Directedness, a
standard notion from order theory, asks for common \outnouns within each
fiber.

\label{sec:lifted-monotonicity}
\begin{definition}[Relational Monotonicity]
\label{def:relational-monotonicity}
\textcolor{reviewed}{An \spectype satisfies \emph{Relational Monotonicity}
when every output keeps some \outnoun as input \ingrows:}
        \begin{equation}
  i\InputOrd j,\ o\in\fiber{i}
  \quad\Longrightarrow\quad
  \exists u\in\fiber{j}:\ o\OutputOrd u.
  \label{eq:relational-monotonicity}
        \end{equation}
\end{definition}

\textcolor{reviewed}{For singleton fibers this is functional monotonicity,
Equation~\ref{eq:functional-monotonicity}.} It treats each output separately,
so it does not require several outputs in one fiber to have a common \outnoun. \textcolor{reviewed}{For a set $F\subseteq O$ of outputs, outputs $o_1,\ldots,o_k$ are
\emph{compatible in $F$} if some $u\in F$ \outrel all of them, and
\emph{incompatible in $F$} otherwise.}

\begin{definition}[Directed fiber]
\label{def:fiber-directedness}
\textcolor{reviewed}{A fiber $F\subseteq O$ is \emph{directed} when every nonempty finite
$X\subseteq F$ is compatible in $F$.}
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Directed fibers]
\label{def:directed-outputs}
\textcolor{reviewed}{An \spectype has \emph{directed fibers} when every
fiber $\fiber{i}$ is directed.}
\end{definition}

\textcolor{reviewed}{A singleton fiber is trivially directed.} A common \outnoun, like a common
\indesc, need not be unique or least. For inputs, take two independent
transactions $u$ and $v$, with schedules ordered by subsequence: $uv$ and $vu$
are both minimal common \indescs of $u$ and $v$, and neither \inrel the other. For outputs, directedness asks only that each finite set have some common
\outnoun in the fiber, not a least one or one given by a fixed merge operator,
so it is weaker than requiring a join-semilattice. For example, under
inclusion the fiber $\{\{a\},\{b\},\{a,b,c\},\{a,b,d\},\{a,b,c,d\}\}$ is
directed, since $\{a,b,c,d\}$ \outrel every member, yet $\{a\}$ and $\{b\}$
have two minimal common \outnouns, $\{a,b,c\}$ and $\{a,b,d\}$. Those two are
incomparable but compatible, since $\{a,b,c,d\}$ \outrel both: compatibility
does not require comparability. The traceroute fiber of
Figure~\ref{fig:ambiguity-frames}(g) is directed for a simpler reason: finitely
many of its route sets always have a common \outnoun in the fiber, their union. The two transaction orders in panel~(f), by contrast, are incompatible.

\paragraph{Lifted Monotonicity.}
To state both requirements at once, we lift $\OutputOrd$ to sets. For nonempty
$A,B\subseteq O$, define
        \begin{equation}
  A\LiftedOrd B
  \;\Longleftrightarrow\;
  \text{every nonempty finite }X\subseteq A\text{ is compatible in }B,
  \label{eq:joint-output-order}
        \end{equation}
the \emph{joint refinement lift} of $\OutputOrd$. We apply it with $A=\fiber{i}$ and
$B=\fiber{j}$ for $i\InputOrd j$. When $X$ is a single output $o$, it asks
that $o$ have an \outnoun in $\fiber{j}$, which is Relational Monotonicity. When $X$ holds several outputs, it asks that they have a common \outnoun in
$\fiber{j}$; with $j=i$, this is directedness.

\begin{definition}[Lifted Monotonicity]
\label{def:lifted-monotonicity}
An \spectype satisfies \emph{Lifted Monotonicity}, and
is called \emph{monotone}, when the mapping $i\mapsto\fiber{i}$ is monotone
under the joint refinement lift:
        \begin{equation}
  i\InputOrd j
  \quad\Longrightarrow\quad
  \fiber{i}\LiftedOrd\fiber{j}.
  \label{eq:lifted-monotonicity}
        \end{equation}
\end{definition}

\textcolor{reviewed}{With singleton fibers, Lifted Monotonicity is functional monotonicity
(Definition~\ref{def:functional-monotonicity}).} By the reading above, it
implies Relational Monotonicity and directed fibers. Conversely, suppose both
hold, and take $i\InputOrd j$ and a finite nonempty $X\subseteq\fiber{i}$. Relational Monotonicity gives each $x\in X$ an \outnoun in $\fiber{j}$, and
since $\fiber{j}$ is directed, those \outnouns have a common \outnoun in
$\fiber{j}$. So Lifted Monotonicity holds exactly when both do. In
powerdomain terms, Relational Monotonicity is monotonicity of
$i\mapsto\fiber{i}$ under the Hoare preorder~\cite{abramsky1994domain}, which
asks only that each output in $A$ have an \outnoun in $B$. The joint
refinement lift asks that every finite set of them have a common one, and the
two agree when $B$ is directed. Unlike the Hoare preorder, the joint
refinement lift is not reflexive: $A\LiftedOrd A$ holds exactly when $A$ is
directed. The transaction fiber of Figure~\ref{fig:ambiguity-frames}(f)
therefore fails it already at $j=i$.

\paragraph{The common-extension condition.}
\textcolor{reviewed}{Lifted Monotonicity is stated for one input and its extensions, but runs may
emit outputs at incomparable inputs, say $o_1$ at $i_1$ and $o_2$ at $i_2$.} \textcolor{reviewed}{Such runs can be reconciled only at a common \indesc $j$ of both, which calls
for a condition on rows at different inputs: a common \outnoun of $o_1$ and
$o_2$ in the fiber at $j$.} \textcolor{reviewed}{The two conditions turn out to be equivalent.}

\begin{definition}[Common-extension condition]
\label{def:common-expansion-condition}
\textcolor{reviewed}{An \spectype satisfies the \emph{common-extension condition} when,
for every finite set of rows $(i_1,o_1),\ldots,(i_k,o_k)$ of $P$ and every
common \indesc $j$ of $i_1,\ldots,i_k$, some $u\in P(j)$ \outrel every
$o_r$.}
\end{definition}

\textcolor{reviewed}{For the empty set of rows the condition asks only that $P(j)$ be nonempty, which
follows from $P$ being total on $I$.}

\begin{proposition}[Common-extension form of Lifted Monotonicity]
\label{prop:common-expansion-form}
\textcolor{reviewed}{An \spectype satisfies Lifted Monotonicity if and only
if it satisfies the common-extension condition.}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
Lifted Monotonicity gives Relational Monotonicity, which carries each $o_r$
to some \outnoun in the fiber at $j$, and that fiber is directed, so those
\outnouns have a common \outnoun there. Conversely, for $i\InputOrd j$, the
rows $(i,x)$ for $x$ in a finite $X\subseteq\fiber{i}$ have $j$ as a common
\indesc, so the condition gives the common \outnoun that Lifted Monotonicity
asks for. Full proof in Appendix~\ref{app:proofs-sec3}.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Lifted output events}
\label{sec:lifted-output-events}

For a state $(i,R)$ and $o\in R(i)$, the output event $\mathsf{output}(o)$
again emits $o$ and keeps exactly the rows whose outputs \outrelbase $o$,
ending at $(i,R[\mathrm{out}\OutputGe o])$ as in Equation~\ref{eq:output-action}. Every later output must \outrelbase $o$, so the event removes the rows whose
outputs do not. Each removed row $(k,x)$ falls into one of two classes:
subsumed or excluded. A row $(k,x)$ is placed in $\Subsumed_o$ if some
$u\in R(k)$ \outrel both $x$ and $o$. Then a later output $u$ reports
everything $x$ would have, and the removal, an output subsumption, loses
nothing. \textcolor{reviewed}{Every other removed row is placed in $\Excluded_o$.} \textcolor{reviewed}{With several
outputs at an input, the canonical \commitment $x_o$ is the removal of the rows
in $\Excluded_o$ only (Definition~\ref{def:canonical-commitment}); the output
event still removes the rows in $\Subsumed_o$, but that removal is not a
\commitment.} \textcolor{reviewed}{If $x_o$ removes every row at $k$, it rules out the input $k$;
otherwise it rules out only the excluded outputs at $k$.}

These are the cases of Section~\ref{sec:ambiguity-story}. \textcolor{reviewed}{Emitting $\bot$
under the equality order of Figure~\ref{fig:nonmonotone-frame} puts the rows
for $aba$ and $abb$ in $\Excluded_o$ and rules out those inputs.} \textcolor{reviewed}{Emitting
$[A,B]$ in Figure~\ref{fig:ambiguity-frames}(f) puts the row for $[B,A]$ in
$\Excluded_o$ and rules out that output at $\{A,B\}$.} The route sets removed in
panel~(g) are in $\Subsumed_o$. In the functional case $\Subsumed_o$ is empty,
so this is the output event of Definition~\ref{def:output-event}. Appendix~\ref{app:lifted-output-events} gives the formal definitions.

With several outputs at one input, legality is more permissive: an output
event may remove rows by output subsumption without applying a \commitment. \textcolor{reviewed}{As before, $o$ is a legal output when $\mathsf{output}(o)$ applies no
\commitment, exactly when $\Excluded_o$ is empty.} \textcolor{reviewed}{Equivalently, at every legal
\indesc $j$ of $i$, $o$ and each remaining output at $j$ have a common
\outnoun at $j$ (Proposition~\ref{prop:exposability}).}

\subsection{The Complete CALM theorem}
\label{sec:complete-calm-theorem}

Specification states, input events, runs, \commitment-free runs, and
consistency are defined as before. The results of Section~\ref{sec:executions}
use output events only through Equation~\ref{eq:output-action}, which lifted
output events still satisfy, so the rows-remaining lemma
(Lemma~\ref{lem:run-residual}) and the Survivor test
(Proposition~\ref{prop:confluence}) apply unchanged. With them, Complete CALM
characterizes consistency by Lifted Monotonicity.

\begin{theorem}[Complete CALM]
\label{thm:complete-calm}
An \spectype is consistent if and only if it satisfies Lifted
Monotonicity, equivalently the common-extension condition.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
\textcolor{reviewed}{If the common-extension condition holds, a common \outnoun $u\in P(j)$
of everything emitted survives in every run.} \textcolor{reviewed}{If outputs emitted at inputs with a common \indesc $j$ are incompatible in
$P(j)$, the runs that emit them share no row at $j$ and can never be
reconciled there.} Full proof in Appendix~\ref{app:proofs-sec3}.
\end{proof}

\textcolor{reviewed}{The condition that characterizes consistency is a property of a spec: Lifted Monotonicity, which for a function is
ordinary monotonicity.}

\textcolor{reviewed}{The same condition decides which outputs need a \commitment.}

\begin{proposition}[No-\commitment test]
\label{prop:exposure-boundary}
\textcolor{reviewed}{An \spectype satisfies Lifted Monotonicity if and only
if every output at every state reachable without \commitment is legal.}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
If the common-extension condition holds, the spec is consistent by Complete
CALM. Take a reachable state $(i,R)$, an output $o\in R(i)$, a legal \indesc
$j$ of $i$, and $x\in R(j)$. Extending a run that reaches $(i,R)$ by
$\mathsf{output}(o)$, or by $\mathsf{input}(j)$ and then $\mathsf{output}(x)$,
gives two runs that are confluent at $j$, so some $u\in R(j)$ \outrel both $o$
and $x$, which makes $o$ legal (Proposition~\ref{prop:exposability}). Conversely, if every such output is legal, applying
Proposition~\ref{prop:exposability} at canonical states, one row at a time,
builds the common \outnoun that the condition asks for. Full proof in
Appendix~\ref{app:proofs-sec3}.
\end{proof}

For functional specs, Lifted Monotonicity and the common-extension condition
are both functional monotonicity, so Complete CALM and
Proposition~\ref{prop:exposure-boundary} reduce to Functional CALM and
Proposition~\ref{prop:functional-exposure-boundary}.

\section{Distributed Execution}
\label{sec:distributed-execution}

\textcolor{reviewed}{This section gives Functional CALM and Complete CALM a distributed reading,
including a convergence guarantee for functional specs
(Corollary~\ref{cor:convergence}).} We then define systems, relate their runs to
canonical runs, and show where \commitments conflict; Complete CAP follows.


\subsection{Distributed histories}
\label{sec:histories}

\textcolor{reviewed}{Until now, runs have been independent: each state changes only through that
run's input and output events.} \textcolor{reviewed}{We now place one such run at each participant in
a finite set $\mathcal N$.} \textcolor{reviewed}{Participant $p$ starts in the canonical state
$\sigma_P(i_p)$ and still changes its state only through its own specification
events.} \textcolor{reviewed}{The new ingredient is communication.} \textcolor{reviewed}{Besides learning input directly
from the environment, a participant can learn cumulative input from another
participant.}

\textcolor{reviewed}{Participants exchange messages by $\mathsf{send}(m)$ and
$\mathsf{receive}(m)$.} A \emph{message} is a pair $m=(j,d)$ of a cumulative
input $j$, or $\varnothing$ if it carries none, and application-specific data $d$, described below. \textcolor{reviewed}{A receive does not change the specification state.} \textcolor{reviewed}{A participant at $(i,R)$ that receives $(j,d)$ with
$j\neq\varnothing$ may incorporate $j$ by its own $\mathsf{input}(k)$ for a
legal common \indesc $k$ of $i$ and $j$, when one exists.} Which $k$ to use is
the system's choice (\S\ref{sec:systems}).

\textcolor{reviewed}{Send and receive events connect the participants' locally ordered runs.} \textcolor{reviewed}{Adding
an edge from each send to its matching receive gives the standard model of a
distributed history; the transitive closure of these edges and the local orders
is happens-before.}

\begin{definition}[Distributed history, history event]
\label{def:distributed-history}
Fix an \spectype, participants $\mathcal N$, and an initial input $i_p$
for each. \textcolor{reviewed}{A \emph{distributed history} $H$ is a finite set of
\emph{history events}, each an occurrence at one participant of a
specification event (Definition~\ref{def:run}), $\mathsf{send}(m)$, or
$\mathsf{receive}(m)$, with:}
\begin{enumerate}
\item
a total local order on each participant's history events;
\item
for each receive, a matching send of the same message at another
        participant, each send matching at most one receive;
\item
for each participant $p$, its specification events, in local order,
        form a defined transition sequence from $\sigma_P(i_p)$: each
        is legal at the state where it occurs; and
\item
no history event happens before itself
        (Definition~\ref{def:happens-before}).
\end{enumerate}
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Happens-before]
\label{def:happens-before}
\emph{Happens-before} is the transitive closure of the local orders of a
distributed history and of each send preceding its matching receive.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Consistent cut]
\label{def:consistent-cut}
A \emph{consistent cut} $C$ is a subset of history events closed under happens-before~\cite{lamport1978time,chandy1985distributed}. \textcolor{reviewed}{The term is standard and unrelated to our notion of consistency.}
\end{definition}

A consistent cut contains a prefix of each participant's history events, so by
item~3
it determines one run per participant.

\begin{definition}[Participant run]
\label{def:participant-run}
At a consistent cut $C$, the specification events of $C$ at participant $p$,
in local order, form its \emph{participant run}, a run
(Definition~\ref{def:run}) whose final state we write $\sigma_p(C)$.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Endpoint]
\label{def:endpoint}
The \emph{endpoint} of a consistent cut $C$ is
$\operatorname{end}(H,C)=(\sigma_p(C))_{p\in\mathcal N}$.
\end{definition}

\subsection{Distributed consistency}
\label{sec:distributed-consistency}

A \emph{continuation} of a history at a consistent cut $C$ is a history $H'$
that extends the history events of $C$ with further history events after
them. All of $H'$ is a consistent cut of $H'$, its \emph{final cut}. Let $j$ be a common \indesc of the participants' current inputs at a consistent cut $C$. The cut $C$ is \emph{confluent at} $j$ when some continuation at $C$ has a final cut with endpoint $\bigl((j,R)\bigr)_{p\in\mathcal N}$ for some $R$, so that every participant ends in the same state $(j,R)$. History events at different participants are independent, so this holds exactly when the participant runs at $C$ are
confluent at $j$ (Definition~\ref{def:consistency}).

\begin{definition}[Distributed consistency]
\label{def:distributed-consistency}
An \spectype has \emph{distributed consistency} when, for every
finite participant set, every consistent cut of every history is confluent
at every common \indesc of the participants' current inputs.
\end{definition}

\begin{proposition}[Consistency and distributed consistency]
\label{prop:distributed-consistency}
An \spectype is consistent if and only if it has distributed
consistency.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
The participant runs at a cut are a family of runs, and any family
of runs can be placed at separate participants that exchange no messages. Full proof in Appendix~\ref{app:proofs-sec4}.
\end{proof}

By Complete CALM, which reduces to Functional CALM with one output per input,
a spec has distributed consistency exactly when it satisfies Lifted
Monotonicity. Under Lifted Monotonicity, this confluence has a concrete witness. For participant runs with common \indesc $j$, the Survivor test (Proposition~\ref{prop:confluence}) supplies a
$u\in\fiber{j}$ that survives in every endpoint; by
Lemma~\ref{lem:run-residual}, $u$ \outrel everything they have emitted. \textcolor{reviewed}{At
each participant, $\mathsf{input}(j)$ followed by $\mathsf{output}(u)$ reaches
the same state without \commitment (Proposition~\ref{prop:exposure-boundary}).} The witness shows only that such a state exists in the transition system. Choosing $u$, communicating it, and scheduling the transitions are up to the
system (\S\ref{sec:systems}).

\textcolor{reviewed}{For functional specs,
Corollary~\ref{cor:state-by-input} also gives convergence:}

\begin{corollary}[Convergence]
\label{cor:convergence}
In any history of a consistent functional specification, every
participant whose current input at a consistent cut is $j$ is in state
$\sigma_P(j)$. Participants with the same current input therefore agree,
regardless of message paths, arrival order, or the outputs they emitted.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
Each participant run is a run, so Corollary~\ref{cor:state-by-input} applies.
\end{proof}

This is the convergence that strong eventual consistency~\cite{shapiro2011crdt} asks of replicated state: replicas that have received the same updates are in equivalent states. CRDTs obtain it from a semilattice merge, which suffices but is not needed, since every consistent functional spec has it. With several outputs per input, convergence fails even for consistent specs. In Figure~\ref{fig:ambiguity-frames}(g), a run that emitted $\{s{\to}\texttt{s1}\}$
has subsumed the route sets that omit it, while a run at the same input that
emitted nothing keeps them. Consistency then guarantees only that such replicas can be reconciled, not that they agree.

\paragraph{Message data.}
The data $d$ of a message is any application state other than input that a
participant chooses to send, such as timestamps, version vectors, gossip, or votes. Data
can influence which history events occur next at the receiver, but it
never directly changes the receiver's specification state, and nothing in the
definitions above depends on it. Section~\ref{sec:systems} calls the
rule that makes these choices a participant's \emph{strategy}. Data can
influence the strategy, but the strategy can decide only within the
spec. Participants may also share emitted outputs in $d$; this does not change
which cuts are confluent (Appendix~\ref{app:exchanging-outputs}).


\subsection{Systems and canonical runs}
\label{sec:systems}

\textcolor{reviewed}{A spec describes which behaviors are permitted, but not how a system chooses
among them.} \textcolor{reviewed}{We now model those choices, and show that a system's inputs and
outputs always remain those of a canonical run.}

For example, serializability can be enforced by any of a number of concurrency
control protocols (Figure~\ref{fig:ambiguity-frames}(f), formalized in
Appendix~\ref{app:cc-bases}). \textcolor{reviewed}{Two-phase locking (2PL) orders transactions
pairwise through lock requests: when $B$ waits for a lock $A$ holds,
$\mathsf{order}(A,B)$ fixes $A$ before $B$ and nothing else.} \textcolor{reviewed}{Multiversion
timestamp ordering (MVTO)~\cite{bernstein1981concurrency} orders them totally
through timestamp assignment: $\mathsf{stamp}(B)$ fixes $B$ before any
transaction not yet stamped.} The
two narrow the spec differently.

The registrar of Section~\ref{sec:election-story} is a similar example. When
it emits $\bot$, it also closes the polls (Figure~\ref{fig:election-frames}(d)),
although emitting $\bot$ does not require that \commitment. Closing the polls is the registrar's
design decision, as fixing orderings is 2PL's.

\textcolor{reviewed}{Sections~\ref{sec:functional-calm}--\ref{sec:complete-calm} used only the
canonical \commitment $x_o$ that an output requires.} Systems may commit
earlier or remove more alternatives, as the examples above do, so we let a
system issue \commitments of its own. A system for a spec fixes a basis of
\commitments that its participants may apply, and each participant's strategy
(Definition~\ref{def:strategy}) decides which to apply and when.

\begin{definition}[\Commitment]
\label{def:commitment}
A \emph{\commitment} is a partial function $e$ on specification states that
removes a nonempty set of rows, none of which is \outrelpast by a row that
remains at the same input. That is, whenever $e(i,R)=(j,R')$, we have
$R'\subsetneq R$ and
\begin{equation}
  (k,u)\in R\setminus R',\ (k,w)\in R'
  \quad\Longrightarrow\quad
  u\not\OutputOrd w.
  \label{eq:commitment-unsubsumed}
\end{equation}
It leaves the current input unchanged: $j=i$.
\end{definition}

The ordering \commitment above meets this definition: $\mathsf{order}(A,B)$ removes the
serial orders with $B$ before $A$, and no surviving order with $A$ before $B$
\outrel any of them. The canonical \commitments of
Section~\ref{sec:lifted-output-events} satisfy this definition too.

A system draws its \commitments from a basis (Definition~\ref{def:system}).

\begin{definition}[System output event]
\label{def:system-output-event}
Let $(i,R)$ be a state and $o\in R(i)$. A system emits $o$ by the output
event $\mathsf{output}(o)$ of Section~\ref{sec:lifted-output-events}, which
ends at $(i,R[\mathrm{out}\OutputGe o])$. When $o$ is not a legal output,
the event applies the canonical \commitment $x_o$, which must then belong to
the system's basis.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[\Commitment event]
\label{def:commitment-event}
A \emph{\commitment event} $\mathsf{apply}(\gamma)$ applies a nonempty
sequence $\gamma=e_1\cdots e_n$ of \commitments, each defined at the state
its predecessors leave, and emits nothing. Since later \commitments only
remove rows, the sequence as a whole is itself a \commitment.
\end{definition}

A system applies its own \commitments only in \commitment events. A \commitment event may precede an output event, which then
has less to remove. The environment observes nothing from a \commitment event. \Commitment events are a third kind of specification event, so a distributed
history may contain them; Definitions~\ref{def:distributed-history} and~\ref{def:participant-run} apply
unchanged.

\begin{definition}[Local history]
\label{def:local-history}
A \emph{local history} of participant $p$ is a finite sequence of $p$'s
history events, including the messages it has received.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Strategy]
\label{def:strategy}
A \emph{strategy} for a participant $p$ is a nondeterministic transition
function: it maps each local history $\lambda$ of $p$ to a set
$\theta_p(\lambda)$ of output, \commitment, and send events of $p$, and $p$ next
performs any one of them, or waits. Input and receive events are not chosen
by the strategy: inputs come from the environment and receives from matching
sends.
\end{definition}

A strategy says how one participant acts; a system packages one basis with
a strategy for every participant.

\begin{definition}[System]
\label{def:system}
A \emph{system} consists of a \emph{\commitment basis} $\mathcal B$, which
is a set of \commitments shared by all participants, and a strategy $\theta_p$
for each participant $p$, whose output and \commitment events apply only
\commitments from $\mathcal B$.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Produced history]
\label{def:produced-history}
A distributed history \emph{produced} by a system consists of input events
supplied by the environment, receives of matching sends, and output,
\commitment, and send events chosen by the participants' strategies.
\end{definition}

\textcolor{reviewed}{Coordination mechanisms live in the system: its strategies describe how
participants use them, and its basis records the \commitments they can enforce.} A system's strategies select the events of an actual execution; they need not
realize every run the spec permits. A deployment can separately restrict which
participants may emit which rows (Appendix~\ref{app:deployment}).

Participants may behave differently; a replicated (SPMD) system is the special
case in which they share one strategy (Appendix~\ref{app:cap-response}). Systems for different specs compose as components
(Section~\ref{sec:proper-coordination}).

Our model places no bound on the set of participants, and a strategy has no way
to enumerate them. In general there is no list of all participants, which
Ameloot et al.\ call the $\mathsf{All}$ relation~\cite{ameloot2013relational}. Such a list lets a participant wait for every other participant, for example to
collect a vote from each at some step of a protocol, and know when it has them
all. Ameloot et al.\ show that this enlarges what a system can compute. A
spec can include this knowledge as a modeling decision, by fixing the list of
all participants as part of the input in every row. The list is then explicit
common knowledge, available to each participant's strategy from its initial
input.

\paragraph{Observable equivalence.}
A \emph{run under a basis} $\mathcal B$ is one whose output and
\commitment events apply only \commitments from $\mathcal B$
(Definition~\ref{def:run-under-basis}). Locks, timestamps, quorums, and
consensus differ in how and when they decide. The next lemma relates runs
under any such basis to canonical runs.

\begin{lemma}[Observable Equivalence]
\label{lem:canonical-equivalent}
\textcolor{reviewed}{Let $\rho$ be a run of a system for a spec $\Spec$, under any \commitment
basis.} \textcolor{reviewed}{Removing the \commitment events from $\rho$ gives a canonical run of
$\Spec$ (Definition~\ref{def:run}) that is \emph{observably equivalent} to
$\rho$: the two accept the same inputs and emit the same outputs, and after each
event the canonical run keeps every row that $\rho$ keeps.}
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
\Commitment events only remove rows, so dropping them can only keep more. An
input or output event applied to a larger table keeps a larger table, and the
canonical run can perform it because the rows of $\rho$ are among its
own. Full proof in
Appendix~\ref{app:bases}.
\end{proof}

To illustrate the lemma, consider 2PL on an input of three transactions
$A$, $B$, $C$ that have each issued their operations and requested commit,
where $B$ waits for a lock that $A$ holds. When $B$ begins to wait, 2PL applies
$\mathsf{order}(A,B)$ in a \commitment event, before any output, keeping only
rows that can be completed without placing $B$ before $A$. Its canonical run
drops that event, so it keeps every row, including the one completed by
$[B,A,C]$. Suppose 2PL later commits all three transactions and orders $A$
before $C$ before $B$ in a \commitment event, and then emits $[A,C,B]$. Every
row whose output \outrel $[A,C,B]$ satisfies 2PL's \commitments, so 2PL
removed none of them, and after the output both runs keep exactly these rows. The tables are equal: the lock made the $A$-before-$B$ decision
earlier than the output required. \textcolor{reviewed}{The \commitments a spec's outputs require therefore do not depend on their
timing or representation in a system's \commitment basis.}

\textcolor{reviewed}{The converse of the lemma is not true because systems can choose to do less
than canonical runs allow (Appendix~\ref{app:bases}).} \textcolor{reviewed}{Systems may constrain their runs arbitrarily, and
their remainder tables may lack rows that their canonical runs keep.} \textcolor{reviewed}{So a spec
can be confluent while a system for it is not.} \textcolor{reviewed}{The registrar is an example:
after closing the polls at $ab\cdot$, it cannot accept the late ballot $aba$.} \textcolor{reviewed}{Under the original output order, a canonical run can emit $\bot$, accept
$aba$, and emit $a$.}

\textcolor{reviewed}{The CALM and CAP criteria are about specs and
their canonical runs.} \textcolor{reviewed}{A system with no \commitment events inherits their
confluence directly, since its runs are canonical runs.} \textcolor{reviewed}{For a system with
\commitments of its own, showing that it inherits that confluence requires an
argument about that system, which is outside the scope of this paper.}

\paragraph{Commitments and conflict.}
The registrar's failure is one case of a general fact. Suppose one run
applies a \commitment that removes a row, and another run emits that row's
output at its input. Then no continuation brings the two runs to a common
state at that input.

\begin{proposition}[Commitment conflict]
\label{prop:commitment-conflict}
Let a run under some \commitment basis from $\sigma_P(i)$ apply a \commitment
that removes the row $(k,x)$, and let a canonical run from $\sigma_P(i)$ issue
$\mathsf{input}(k)$ followed by $\mathsf{output}(x)$. These two runs
\emph{conflict}: they are not confluent at $k$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
Events only remove rows, so $(k,x)$ is present at $\sigma_P(i)$ and the
second run exists. By the Survivor test (Proposition~\ref{prop:confluence}),
confluence at $k$ needs a row at $k$ that survives in both runs. Every row
that survives $\mathsf{output}(x)$ at $k$ \outrel $x$, but the \commitment left
no row at $k$ that \outrel $x$ (Definition~\ref{def:commitment}). Full proof
in Appendix~\ref{app:proofs-sec4}.
\end{proof}

Familiar coordination mechanisms, such as locks and consensus, keep two such
runs from both occurring.

\paragraph{Placement.}
Whether a coordination mechanism must span participants depends on who can make
each choice. A deployment records which outputs each participant may produce. With unrestricted placement, the two conflicting runs can occur at different
participants; ownership can keep them at one. Appendix~\ref{app:deployment}
formalizes this distinction as monotonicity across partitions.

\paragraph{Locks in a shared-nothing database.}
Consider a database partitioned by key, with each key owned and locked by one
participant~\cite{stonebraker1986sharednothing}. Ordering two transactions on
one key makes a \commitment, but ownership keeps both choices at that owner. A
transaction spanning two keys is different: each owner orders its own part, and
the two owners can order the same pair oppositely. They must agree on one
order.

Two-phase commit adds no further \commitment. Each owner's prepare reports the
\commitment it has already made. The coordinator then makes the monotone
decision whether all owners prepared; it detects whether their \commitments
agree and aborts when they do not. It does not make the owners agree. If the
coordinator instead aborts on timeout before the last vote arrives, that abort
applies another \commitment, like closing the polls. One coordinator can make
that choice locally. Replicated coordinators need distributed agreement to
make it, as in Paxos Commit~\cite{gray2006consensus}.

\subsection{The Complete CAP Theorem}
\label{sec:complete-cap}

\textcolor{reviewed}{Brewer conjectured that a distributed system cannot be consistent, available,
and partition tolerant at once~\cite{brewer2000towards}; Gilbert and Lynch
proved it as an impossibility result for a linearizable register~\cite{gilbert2002cap}.} \textcolor{reviewed}{Complete CAP addresses the conjecture more broadly:
which specifications are subject to the CAP
conjecture, and which are not?}

\textcolor{reviewed}{To pose the question properly, note that the outputs that each participant emits---and
needs to keep available---are not enough to judge consistency.} \textcolor{reviewed}{The spec
must also model what an observer (e.g., a client) can eventually conclude by collecting
outputs from multiple participants.}

\textcolor{reviewed}{Like Gilbert and Lynch, we use a replicated register as an example.} \textcolor{reviewed}{A register is a key-value store with a single key, which is enough to reason
about one object.} \textcolor{reviewed}{The register starts at $v_0$, and a read and a write race at
different participants.} \textcolor{reviewed}{Participant $p$ receives write $w(v)$ and emits its
acknowledgment; concurrently, participant $q$ receives the read $r$ and emits
$v_0$.} \textcolor{reviewed}{A client may later collect both responses.} \textcolor{reviewed}{A linearizable register
spec has a row for this situation: its input is the pair of requests, and its
output is the pair of responses.} \textcolor{reviewed}{The spec permits the row because a
linearization may place the concurrent read before the write.} \textcolor{reviewed}{No participant
emits this combined output, and availability does not need it.} \textcolor{reviewed}{Still, it
\outrel both local outputs, which shows that the two are consistent, so the
spec must contain it.} \textcolor{reviewed}{Had the write finished before the read began, no such
combined output would exist; we return to this case at the end of the section.}

\textcolor{reviewed}{We therefore pair a spec with an \emph{explicit possibility relation}
$E\subseteq P$, or explicit relation for short: the rows whose outputs
participants actually emit.} \textcolor{reviewed}{Outputs in
$P\setminus E$ are not emitted by participants;
they remain permitted in $P$ to capture implicit combinations of emitted outputs.}

\textcolor{reviewed}{Both availability and consistency are judged on histories in which a partition
separates the participants.}

\begin{definition}[Partitioned history]
\label{def:partitioned-history}
\textcolor{reviewed}{A \emph{partitioned history} divides the participants into two or more
sides and contains no receive whose matching send lies on another
side.} Participants on the same side may still exchange messages, and every
participant still receives environment input and emits outputs.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Explicit possibility relation]
\label{def:explicit-relation}
\textcolor{reviewed}{An \emph{explicit possibility relation} $E\subseteq P$ is a set of rows whose
outputs participants actually emit.} \textcolor{reviewed}{Write $E(i)$ for its outputs at input
$i$.}
\end{definition}

\textcolor{reviewed}{For example, consider two-phase commit (2PC).} \textcolor{reviewed}{We focus on the spec for the
voters; the coordinator is an observer of that spec.} \textcolor{reviewed}{We're interested in the
fiber at the input \textsf{prepare(tx)}: how each voter responds to that
prepare message.} \textcolor{reviewed}{Each possible explicit response is a row of $E$.} \textcolor{reviewed}{The overall
\emph{set} of responses the coordinator might observe across voters is in
$P\setminus E$.} \textcolor{reviewed}{The spec includes these as implicit outputs, representing the
possible combined outcomes that respect each voter's response.}

\begin{definition}[Emitted rows]
\label{def:emitted-rows}
The \emph{emitted rows} of a run are the pairs $(i,o)$ such that the run
emits $o$ at current input $i$. The emitted rows of a system are the
emitted rows of its participants' runs in the histories it produces.
\end{definition}

An explicit relation is declared with the spec; the emitted rows depend on
the system. Availability for $E$ asks that every row of $E$ at a
participant's state can become an emitted row.


\textcolor{reviewed}{We consider a system whose participants each follow a strategy that depends
only on their local history, and an environment that may send any legal input
to any participant.} \textcolor{reviewed}{Availability asks that, during a partition, each
participant can still emit every explicit output its state permits.}

\begin{definition}[Same-side continuation]
\label{def:local-continuation}
\textcolor{reviewed}{Consider a partitioned history that the system produces, in which participant
$p$ ends in state $(i,R)$.} \textcolor{reviewed}{A \emph{same-side continuation} for $p$ extends that
history with events the system can produce on $p$'s side alone, with no new
input and no message from another side.} \textcolor{reviewed}{Participant $p$ can emit $o$ when some same-side continuation for $p$ emits
$o$.}
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Availability]
\label{def:partition-notions}
The system has \emph{Availability for $E$} when, in every partitioned history
it produces, each participant $p$ can emit every output in $R(i)\cap E(i)$,
where $(i,R)$ is $p$'s final state. \textcolor{reviewed}{\emph{Availability} is shorthand for
Availability for $P$.}
\end{definition}

Partition Consistency asks that, whatever participants emit on separate sides,
their states can still be brought to a common state.

\begin{definition}[Partition Consistency]
\label{def:partition-consistency}
\textcolor{reviewed}{The system has \emph{Partition Consistency} when the final cut of every
partitioned history it produces is confluent at every common \indesc of the
participants' current inputs.}
\end{definition}

\textcolor{reviewed}{The \emph{canonical system for $E$} is the system whose participants follow
canonical runs and emit only outputs of $E$.}

\begin{definition}[Escaping CAP]
\label{def:escapes-cap}
\textcolor{reviewed}{A spec \emph{escapes CAP} for $E$ when its canonical system for $E$ has
Availability for $E$ and Partition Consistency.}
\end{definition}

\textcolor{reviewed}{The canonical system's participants never wait: each can emit any output of
$E$ that its state allows, so it always has Availability for $E$.} \textcolor{reviewed}{The
question is whether their answers stay consistent across sides.} \textcolor{reviewed}{Complete CAP
says they do exactly when outputs of $E$ emitted on different sides have a
common \outnoun in the spec at every common \indesc of their inputs.}

\begin{definition}[Lifted Monotonicity for $E$]
\label{def:lm-for-A}
\textcolor{reviewed}{$\Spec$ satisfies \emph{Lifted Monotonicity for $E$} when every finite set of rows of $E$ with
a common \indesc $j$ has a common \outnoun in $\fiber{j}$.}
\end{definition}

\begin{theorem}[Complete CAP]
\label{thm:complete-cap}
\textcolor{reviewed}{A spec escapes CAP for $E$ if and only if it satisfies Lifted Monotonicity for
$E$.}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
\textcolor{reviewed}{Suppose Lifted Monotonicity for $E$ holds, and let $j$ be a common \indesc of
the participants' current inputs.} \textcolor{reviewed}{The outputs emitted on all sides are outputs
of $E$, so they have a common \outnoun $u$ in $\fiber{j}$.} \textcolor{reviewed}{Each participant
still has the row $(j,u)$, since it makes no \commitments of its own and $u$
\outrel every output it emitted (Lemma~\ref{lem:run-residual}).} \textcolor{reviewed}{So the
participants can all continue to a common state at $j$.} \textcolor{reviewed}{Conversely, take
finitely many rows of $E$ with a common \indesc $j$, and place one participant
per row on its own side.} \textcolor{reviewed}{Availability lets each participant emit its row's
output, and Partition Consistency then gives those outputs a common \outnoun in
$\fiber{j}$.} Full proof in
Appendix~\ref{app:proofs-sec4}.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}
\label{cor:complete-cap-any-system}
\textcolor{reviewed}{If some system for a spec has Availability for $E$ and Partition Consistency,
then so does the spec's canonical system for $E$.}
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
\textcolor{reviewed}{We show that if Lifted Monotonicity for $E$ fails, the system loses Partition
Consistency.} \textcolor{reviewed}{Then some rows of $E$ share a common \indesc $j$, but no output in
$\fiber{j}$ \outrel all of their outputs.} \textcolor{reviewed}{We place each row's participant on
its own side of a partition, and Availability lets each one emit its row's
output.} \textcolor{reviewed}{By Observable Equivalence, a participant keeps no more rows than its
canonical run, so each row it keeps at $j$ \outrel its own output.} \textcolor{reviewed}{A common
state at $j$ would need a row that \outrel every participant's output, and
there is none.} Full proof in Appendix~\ref{app:proofs-sec4}.
\end{proof}

A system might hope to keep more outputs available, without losing Partition
Consistency, by issuing \commitments that coordinate within each side of a
partition. It cannot: Corollary~\ref{cor:complete-cap-any-system} holds for
every system, whether or not it issues \commitments. For
$E=P$, a spec escapes CAP exactly when it satisfies Lifted Monotonicity, and
then the canonical system needs no \commitment at all.

\textcolor{reviewed}{We return to the voters' spec.} \textcolor{reviewed}{Each voter decides whether it can commit and emits
its vote, yes or no.} \textcolor{reviewed}{That decision is a \commitment: a voter that votes yes
rules out every set of votes in which it votes no.} \textcolor{reviewed}{But it is a single-voter
\commitment involving no communication, so the voter can make it and produce
output during a partition.} \textcolor{reviewed}{Each vote is cast by a different voter, so votes
emitted on different sides of a partition never conflict (Appendix~\ref{app:deployment} formalizes this placement as a deployment), and every
set of votes an observer may collect is consistent.}

\textcolor{reviewed}{A partition does not change the intrinsic \commitments a spec needs; rather,
it prevents separated participants from jointly enforcing those \commitments
via coordination.} \textcolor{reviewed}{When Lifted
Monotonicity for $E$ fails, some output of $E$ waits for another side,
sacrificing Availability, or is emitted anyway, sacrificing Partition
Consistency.}

\paragraph{Gilbert and Lynch.}
\textcolor{reviewed}{Gilbert and Lynch's theorem is a case of Complete CAP.} \textcolor{reviewed}{Linearizability
respects real-time order: if one operation finishes before another begins,
every linearization puts the first before the
second~\cite{herlihy1990linearizability}.} \textcolor{reviewed}{In the register example, suppose the
write finishes before the read begins.} \textcolor{reviewed}{Every linearization then puts the write
first, so the read must return $v$.} \textcolor{reviewed}{But each of $p$ and $q$ sees only its own
request, and Availability requires $p$ to acknowledge the write and $q$ to
answer $v_0$.} \textcolor{reviewed}{No output for the two requests together \outrel both answers, so
the spec does not satisfy Lifted Monotonicity for them.} \textcolor{reviewed}{By
Corollary~\ref{cor:complete-cap-any-system}, no system can keep both outputs
available and stay consistent across a partition, which is Gilbert and Lynch's
theorem.}

In this example, the proof of Corollary~\ref{cor:complete-cap-any-system}
uses only weaker forms of its hypotheses, and these weaker forms are Gilbert
and Lynch's, which is why it yields their theorem exactly. We place $p$ and
$q$ on separate sides. Availability is used only to make $p$ acknowledge the
write and $q$ answer the read. Each of their fibers holds a single output, so
requiring each to emit every permitted output is the same as requiring it to
respond, which is Gilbert and Lynch's availability. Partition Consistency is
used only to demand a common \outnoun of the two answers. Such an \outnoun is
a single linearization that explains both answers, which is Gilbert and Lynch's
consistency, and none exists.

In this section the real-time order of the write and the read is part of the
input. Appendix~\ref{app:cap-response} instead takes that order from the
execution itself, extends Complete CAP to systems that choose their responses
when responses are not forced, and derives the theorem in Gilbert and Lynch's
asynchronous model.


\section{Composition and Proper Coordination}
\label{sec:composition}

\textcolor{reviewed}{A replicated key-value store poses a puzzle.} \textcolor{reviewed}{With mutable values, it is
non-monotone and inconsistent~\cite{alvaro2011bloom}.} \textcolor{reviewed}{The pragmatic solution is
to impose a consensus-driven log upstream of the replicas, \`a la
Multi-Paxos~\cite{lamport2001paxos}, so all replicas execute the same commands
in the same order.} \textcolor{reviewed}{But consensus is itself non-monotone: when two commands
arrive concurrently, either may be appended next, and the two alternative orders
have no common \outnoun.} \textcolor{reviewed}{In essence, the standard solution to non-monotonicity
is more non-monotonicity!} \textcolor{reviewed}{Why does it work?}

\textcolor{reviewed}{That puzzle is explained by our framework.} \textcolor{reviewed}{As we'll see, two specs compose by equijoin,
and the composition of two monotone specs is also monotone.} \textcolor{reviewed}{Moreover, for non-monotone specs,
as in our puzzle, we
can reason about which component actually makes \commitments under composition.} \textcolor{reviewed}{When the
upstream component makes every \commitment the composition needs, and the
downstream needs none on the inputs the upstream offers, we say the pipeline's
coordination is \emph{proper}
(\S\ref{sec:proper-coordination}).} Proofs are in
Appendix~\ref{app:composition}.
\label{sec:sequential-composition}

\textcolor{reviewed}{Formally, consider two \spectype[s]
${\Spec_1=(I,\InputOrd,M,\preceq_M,P_1)}$ and
${\Spec_2=(M,\preceq_M,O,\OutputOrd,P_2)}$
in which the output domain of $\Spec_1$ is the input domain of $\Spec_2$.} \textcolor{reviewed}{Their
\emph{sequential composition} is the \spectype from $I$ to $O$ with table}
\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
  P_2\circ P_1
  &=\pi_{I,O}(P_1\Join_M P_2)\\
  &=\{(i,o):\exists m.\ (i,m)\in P_1\land(m,o)\in P_2\}
\end{split}
  \label{eq:spec-composition}
\end{equation}
\textcolor{reviewed}{where $\pi$ and $\Join$ are relational projection and equijoin.} \textcolor{reviewed}{Every $i\in I$
has a nonempty fiber in $P_2\circ P_1$, since $P_1$ is total on $I$ and $P_2$ is
total on $M$ (it has a row at every $m\in M$).} \textcolor{reviewed}{We run the two specs as a pipeline: $\Spec_2$ takes the outputs of $\Spec_1$ as
its inputs, and we say that $\Spec_1$ \emph{guards} $\Spec_2$.} Appendix~\ref{sec:parameterized-composition} gives each spec an input of its
own as well, as when a store serves reads that bypass the log
(Example~\ref{ex:bare-reads}).

\begin{lemma}[Composition]
\label{lem:composition}
\textcolor{reviewed}{If $\Spec_1$ and $\Spec_2$ satisfy Lifted Monotonicity, then so does the \spectype with table $P_2\circ P_1$.}
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
\textcolor{reviewed}{By Proposition~\ref{prop:common-expansion-form}, it suffices to check the
common-extension condition.} \textcolor{reviewed}{Let finitely many rows $(i_r,o_r)$ of
$P_2\circ P_1$ have a common \indesc $j$.} \textcolor{reviewed}{Each row comes from an intermediate
value $m_r$ with $(i_r,m_r)\in P_1$ and $(m_r,o_r)\in P_2$.} \textcolor{reviewed}{The condition for
$\Spec_1$ gives some $m\in P_1(j)$ that \outrel every $m_r$.} \textcolor{reviewed}{So $m$ is a
common \indesc of the inputs $m_r$ of $\Spec_2$, and the condition for
$\Spec_2$ gives some $o\in P_2(m)$ that \outrel every $o_r$.} \textcolor{reviewed}{Then $(j,o)$ is a
row of $P_2\circ P_1$, and $o$ is the common \outnoun.} Full proof
in Appendix~\ref{app:composition}.
\end{proof}

\textcolor{reviewed}{By associativity the lemma extends to finite pipelines.} For a function $f$,
its table $\{(i,f(i))\}$ satisfies Lifted Monotonicity exactly when $f$ is
monotone (\S\ref{sec:lifted-monotonicity}).

\subsection{Proper Coordination}
\label{sec:proper-coordination}

\textcolor{reviewed}{We return to the replicated store of the opening.} \textcolor{reviewed}{The key-value store is not
monotone on its own, but it is monotone on what the log offers: a log fixes
the order of commands, and a longer log only extends the responses.} \textcolor{reviewed}{The log
issues a \commitment to offer only such inputs, freeing the store from making any
\commitments.}

\textcolor{reviewed}{More generally, let $\Spec_1$, which guards $\Spec_2$, be run by a system with any basis, and
let $\Spec_2$ run under a system of its own.} \textcolor{reviewed}{Write $\Spec_2|_{\Spec_1}$ for $\Spec_2$ restricted to the outputs of
$\Spec_1$.} We say that $P_2$ satisfies Lifted Monotonicity \emph{on} a set
$D\subseteq M$ when its restriction to inputs in $D$ does.

\begin{definition}[Proper Coordination]
\label{def:proper-coordination}
\textcolor{reviewed}{A pipeline in which $\Spec_1$ guards $\Spec_2$ is \emph{proper} when
$\Spec_2|_{\Spec_1}$ needs no \commitment: every output at every state it
reaches without \commitment is legal.}
\end{definition}

\begin{theorem}[Proper Coordination]
\label{thm:proper-coordination}
Let $\Spec_1$ guard $\Spec_2$ in a pipeline.
\begin{enumerate}
\item
The pipeline is proper exactly when $\Spec_2$ satisfies Lifted
        Monotonicity on the outputs of $\Spec_1$.
\item
\textcolor{reviewed}{Suppose the pipeline is proper, and fix any system for $\Spec_1$.} \textcolor{reviewed}{Then $\Spec_2|_{\Spec_1}$ can take each output of a run $\rho$ of that
        system as its next input and answer it without \commitment.} \textcolor{reviewed}{Replacing
        each output of $\rho$ by these answers, and dropping the \commitment
        events of $\rho$, gives a canonical run of $P_2\circ P_1$.} Such runs are
        confluent whenever the runs of the system are.
\item
\textcolor{reviewed}{If the pipeline is proper and $P_2\circ P_1$ does not satisfy Lifted
        Monotonicity, some output of $\Spec_1$ needs a \commitment.}
\end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
\textcolor{reviewed}{(1) is Complete CALM and the no-\commitment test
(Theorem~\ref{thm:complete-calm}, Proposition~\ref{prop:exposure-boundary})
applied to $\Spec_2|_{\Spec_1}$.} (2) The outputs of a run of $\Spec_1$ form a
chain, since each output event keeps only rows above its output. They are
therefore a valid input sequence for $\Spec_2|_{\Spec_1}$, which by (1) answers
each without \commitment. The pipeline's run is a run of $P_2\circ P_1$: joining
each answer with the row of $\Spec_1$ it answers gives a row of the composite. For confluence, confluent runs of $\Spec_1$ share a row above every input the
downstream runs have taken, and Lifted Monotonicity of $\Spec_2$ at that row
gives a common answer. (3) The no-\commitment test
of the composite fails at a canonical state, which is the image of a canonical
state of $\Spec_1$. Full proof in Appendix~\ref{app:composition}.
\end{proof}

\textcolor{reviewed}{Longer pipelines nest: the composite $P_2\circ P_1$ of a proper pipeline can
itself guard a third stage, and the theorem applies again
(Corollary~\ref{cor:pipelines}).} \textcolor{reviewed}{Locks in
the key-value store would be pure overhead: by (1), the store restricted to
logs needs no \commitment.}

Theorem~\ref{thm:proper-coordination} follows a pipeline event by event,
with one system per stage. Stages can also be viewed from outside, so that
stages with different specs and participants compose without one shared
history. We call a system for one spec, seen only
through its boundary, a \emph{component}. A component may run as one process
or as a replicated cluster. The stages around it observe only its
\emph{admitted runs}, each pairing an input with an emitted output that its
spec permits. Components with different specs form \emph{typed pipelines}
(Appendix~\ref{app:components}). A typed pipeline hides the intermediate
value and both components' runs, and every input--output pair it shows is a
row of $P_2\circ P_1$. Properness then has two forms. A typed pipeline is
\emph{contract-proper} when the pipeline of its specs is proper
(Definition~\ref{def:proper-coordination}), which concerns the specs alone. \emph{Implementation properness} also depends on the runs that the chosen
downstream component admits: it holds when no downstream run that follows
an upstream output applies a \commitment. A contract-proper downstream component may still take
locks, or admit too few runs to follow its upstream. The maximally
permissive empty-basis component for $\Spec_2|_{\Spec_1}$, which admits every
run under the empty basis, does neither.

\subsection{Composite CALM}
\label{sec:composite-calm}

\textcolor{reviewed}{Proper coordination answers our opening puzzle by showing how an upstream
component can discharge a pipeline's \commitment obligations.} \textcolor{reviewed}{\emph{Composite CALM} asks a stronger question: when does a pipeline need no
\commitment at any stage?} \textcolor{reviewed}{If every stage is monotone, the answer is immediate:
by Lemma~\ref{lem:composition} the composite is monotone too, and no stage needs
a \commitment.} \textcolor{reviewed}{The interesting case has a non-monotone stage, and it raises a
simpler puzzle than our opening one.}

\textcolor{reviewed}{Consider the spec for an incrementing counter that is capped at $9$, and
compose it with a downstream spec for a double-digit threshold test, which
outputs \textsf{false} for values up through $9$ and \textsf{true} from $10$ on.} Under the usual boolean order, as in LVars~\cite{kuper2013lvars}, \textsf{false}
is only a lower bound, and the test is monotone. Suppose instead that every
answer must be final. \textcolor{reviewed}{Then the output order is equality, and the test cannot
emit \textsf{false} without a \commitment, since a later count of $10$ would
contradict it.} \textcolor{reviewed}{Yet when composed after the capped counter, the pair of
components needs no \commitment.} \textcolor{reviewed}{When do composed specs erase the need for
\commitment entirely?}

The composite is itself an \spectype. By Complete CALM, it is consistent
exactly when it satisfies Lifted Monotonicity, and then no output needs a
\commitment (Theorem~\ref{thm:complete-calm},
Proposition~\ref{prop:exposure-boundary}). The orders on $I$ and $O$ are part
of the composite's contract with its environment. The order on $M$ is not:
the composite's table never mentions it, and some order on $M$ may make both
stages monotone. A composite can be jointly monotone, with no stage needing a
\commitment, in three ways.

First, every stage is monotone, as noted above
(Corollary~\ref{cor:pipelines}). The CRDT observations of Section~\ref{sec:crdt-instance} have this shape. A
client query $q$ over the replica state $s$ is the composition $q\circ s$, and
compositions of monotone functions are monotone
(Corollary~\ref{cor:functional-composition}), so monotonicity of $q$ on the
whole state domain suffices.

Second, the upstream can filter out the inputs on which the downstream is not
monotone. \textcolor{reviewed}{In the example above, the test fails monotonicity only when the
count passes from single to double digits, and the capped counter never does
so.} \textcolor{reviewed}{Restricted to the counts it does produce, the test always answers
\textsf{false}; it is constant, and so monotone under any output order,
including equality.} \textcolor{reviewed}{By Theorem~\ref{thm:proper-coordination}(1), the pipeline
is proper, and since the capped counter is itself monotone, neither stage needs
a \commitment (Proposition~\ref{prop:joint-monotone}(2)).} This is proper
coordination in which the upstream itself needs no \commitment.

Third, a different order on $M$ can make two non-monotone stages monotone. We order the booleans with false below true for $I$, $O$, and $M$. In a
double toggle, two stages that each negate a boolean compose to the identity. Neither stage is monotone, but under the reversed order on $M$, both are
(Appendix~\ref{app:composition}).

\begin{proposition}[Jointly monotone composites]
\label{prop:joint-monotone}
The composite $P_2\circ P_1$ satisfies Lifted Monotonicity, and so is
consistent and needs no \commitment, in each of the following cases.
\begin{enumerate}
\item
$\Spec_1$ and $\Spec_2$ satisfy Lifted Monotonicity.
\item
$\Spec_1$ satisfies Lifted Monotonicity; $\Spec_2$ satisfies it on
        $\Spec_1$'s outputs.
\item
$\Spec_1$ and $\Spec_2$ satisfy Lifted Monotonicity via a different order
        on $M$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
(1) is Lemma~\ref{lem:composition}. (2) The proof of the lemma uses only
intermediate values in fibers of $\Spec_1$. (3) The composite joins the two
tables on the intermediate value and never compares two of them, so its table
is the same under a different order. The lemma then applies under that order. Full
proof in Appendix~\ref{app:composition}.
\end{proof}

The three cases do not cover every monotone composite
(Appendix~\ref{app:composition}). When no case applies, some stage may have to issue a \commitment even though the composite is monotone.

Sequential composition, products of independent stages, and glue stages that
copy, merge, or route outputs build arbitrary acyclic graphs. A graph
satisfies Lifted Monotonicity when every node does on the outputs offered to
it, checked node by node much as types are
(Appendix~\ref{app:dataflow-graphs}).

\subsection{Intersection}
\label{sec:intersection}

Sequential composition feeds the outputs of one spec to the inputs of the
next. Specs can also be combined on a shared interface. An application spec,
such as a replicated store that answers reads by a fixed rule, may promise
nothing about isolation, while a consistency property, such as an isolation
level, constrains the same reads. A system that meets both may produce only
the rows that both permit.

\begin{definition}[Intersection]
\label{def:intersection}
Let $\Spec_1=(I,\InputOrd,O,\OutputOrd,P_1)$ and
$\Spec_2=(I,\InputOrd,O,\OutputOrd,P_2)$ share their ordered domains. Their
\emph{intersection} $\Spec_1\cap\Spec_2$ has table $P_1\cap P_2$. It is an
\spectype when $P_1\cap P_2$ is total on $I$: every input has an output that
both permit.
\end{definition}

The shared domains matter. For example, isolation levels are defined on
histories of reads, writes, and commits, so an application spec must describe
its inputs and outputs in those terms before it can be intersected with one.

Intersection does not preserve Lifted Monotonicity. Let $a$ and $b$ be
incomparable outputs, each below both $c$ and $d$, with $c$ and $d$
incomparable, and let both specs have a single input. The fiber $\{a,b,c\}$ is
directed, since $c$ \outrel each of its members, and so is $\{a,b,d\}$. The
intersection of the two fibers, $\{a,b\}$, is not: no output in it \outrel
both $a$ and $b$. Two specs that need no \commitment can thus have an
intersection that does. A
monotone bound rules this out.

\begin{proposition}[Intersection under a monotone bound]
\label{prop:intersection-bound}
Let $g:I\to O$ be monotone. Suppose that every output $\Spec_1$ permits at an
input $j$ lies below $g(j)$, and that $\Spec_1$ and $\Spec_2$ both permit $g(j)$
at every $j$. Then $\Spec_1\cap\Spec_2$ is an \spectype and satisfies Lifted
Monotonicity.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
The intersection permits $g(j)$ at every $j$, so it is total. An output it
permits at $i\InputOrd j$ lies below $g(i)$, and $g(i)\OutputOrd g(j)$, so
$g(j)$ \outrel every output it permits at inputs below $j$. Full proof in
Appendix~\ref{app:intersection}.
\end{proof}

A store whose answers follow a monotone rule therefore stays monotone when
intersected with any property that permits the rule's answers. Section~\ref{sec:hat-instance} uses this construction for isolation levels and
a key-value store.

\section{Related Work and Mappings}
\label{sec:instances}
\label{sec:related}

\textcolor{reviewed}{Complete CALM provides a unified lens for many results across literatures.} \textcolor{reviewed}{For the key related ideas, we provide mappings here to our specification model.} \textcolor{reviewed}{Each setting declares an input, an output, and their orders before
applying either CALM theorem.}

\subsection{Query monotonicity}
\label{sec:query-instance}

The CALM Theorem of Ameloot et al.\ (\S\ref{sec:functional-calm}) is stated in
a particular operational model, relational languages on transducer networks: a
program
can be evaluated without coordination exactly when it is monotone, under that
model's assumptions about data placement and
execution~\cite{ameloot2013relational,ameloot2015weaker}. Functional CALM
does not replace the details of that theorem. It restates the result as a
property of a spec, which can be compared with specs outside Datalog.

Subsequent extensions to the original CALM Theorem become changes to the input
domain and its order, or to the notion of coordination. Zinn et al.\ gave transducers partial knowledge of how data is
placed, and more queries became coordination-free, among them the
non-monotone win-move query~\cite{zinn2012winmove}. Ameloot et al.\
characterized the new classes by weaker forms of
monotonicity~\cite{ameloot2015weaker}, correcting an earlier one-fact
version~\cite{zinn2012weak}. A query is \emph{domain-distinct-monotone} when
$Q(I)\subseteq Q(I\cup J)$ for every $J$ whose facts each contain a value
that does not occur in $I$. It is \emph{domain-disjoint-monotone} when this
holds for every $J$ whose facts use only such values. Each is Functional
CALM under a different input order. Under policy-aware placement, a node can
learn which facts over its known values are absent, so the input it cannot
yet rule out is a domain-distinct $J$. Under domain-guided placement it is a
domain-disjoint $J$. When the order lets $I\cup J$ \inrelbase $I$ only for
such $J$, each condition is functional monotonicity. Functional CALM takes
the order as given.

Li and Lee model replicated objects with write, merge, and query operations,
show that strong eventual consistency holds exactly when merge is associative,
commutative, and idempotent and a write equals merging in its input, and prove
CALM in that model: a problem has an implementation that is confluent and
consistent under partition exactly when it is monotone~\cite{li2025coordinationfree}. Their problems are functions from sets of inputs, ordered by inclusion, to a
partially ordered set of outputs whose order they read as consistency, that is,
the functional specs whose inputs are sets under inclusion.

Baccaert and Ketsman were the first to extend CALM beyond deterministic
queries~\cite{baccaert2023dcbq,baccaert2026spectrum}. Their running example
is leader election, which permits many outputs for one input. They also
changed how data placement is modeled. Ameloot et al.\ require a query to be
computed correctly however its input is distributed across nodes. Baccaert
and Ketsman make placement part of the problem instead: each fact names the
node that holds it, as in Dedalus and declarative
networking~\cite{alvaro2010dedalus,loo2009declarative}, and a
\emph{behavior} maps such located inputs to sets of permitted located outputs.

Their model maps directly to ours. A behavior is an \spectype whose outputs
are ordered by inclusion. Each fact names the participant that holds it. One
input extends another by adding the facts of participants not yet heard from,
never by adding facts of a participant already heard from. A network of
transducers is a system in which every participant runs the same strategy,
sees only its own facts and the contents of the messages it receives, and
broadcasts its messages. Since its own facts name it, a participant knows its
own name and the order on names, which is what their coordination-free
transducers know. It does not know the list of all participants. Under their
placement, the environment gives each participant only its own facts, at the
start, so the facts in a participant's input that name other participants
arrived in messages, and a participant passes on only facts it holds. A
transducer that computes a behavior translates into a system whose runs stay
confluent. Every output that some fair run of the transducer can produce is
emitted by some run of that system.

Their aims differ from ours. They ask which behaviors become computable when
nodes share some piece of system knowledge, such as identifiers, an order on
them, or the list of all nodes. For them, a behavior needs coordination when
it cannot be computed without the list of all nodes. Correctness is judged by
the final output of a run, and the input is fixed when the run starts. We ask
instead when a spec requires a \commitment, judge consistency at every point of
a run, and let input arrive over time. A behavior can therefore be
coordination-free in their sense while still needing a \commitment in ours,
made by one participant, as in their constructions, where a group's smallest
node chooses for the group.

Two sides of a partition cannot know that they are disconnected, so neither can
tell whether it is the whole network, and each must emit. When the partition
heals, the outputs of both sides must have a common \outnoun at any input that
extends what both sides have received. This is what Baccaert and Ketsman call
\emph{node monotonicity}: the common-extension condition for outputs of
participants that have heard from disjoint sets of participants. They show
that a behavior is coordination-free exactly when it contains a node monotone
behavior.

Complete CAP asks for more, because the environment is not constrained from
back-channel sharing of facts across sides. It requires the common-extension
condition even when participants on different sides receive overlapping input. Under Baccaert and Ketsman's placement there is no back channel.

We now show how the back-channel sharing that Complete CAP allows goes beyond
node monotonicity. Consider an unconstrained matching problem over data items
partitioned across participants: any set of disjoint pairs of items is a
permitted output. Under a network partition with Baccaert and Ketsman's
placement, each side can pair its items arbitrarily. Two sides can always
combine their pairings, because no item can be found on both sides. This
changes in our model, where the environment can provide an item to
participants on both sides of the partition. An item $i$ may then be paired
with $j$ on one side and with $k$ on the other, and no matching contains both
pairs. For deterministic behaviors the two conditions coincide.

\subsection{CRDTs: monotone queries over convergent state}
\label{sec:crdt-instance}

A CRDT guarantees that replicas receiving the same updates converge to
equivalent states, whatever the order of delivery and merge. But the Strong
Eventual Consistency property it offers provides no guarantee on what the
environment may observe, and is subject to Lamport's ``anomalous
behavior''~\cite{lamport1978time} (\S\ref{sec:functional-events}). \textcolor{reviewed}{Laddad et al.\
proposed monotonicity as the right test for safe observation from CRDT
state~\cite{laddad2022keep}.} Their example is a two-phase set, whose state
$(\mathit{Add},\mathit{Del})$ merges by componentwise union. The query
$\mathit{Add}\setminus\mathit{Del}$ can expose added items that are subsequently
deleted. We make the proposed test precise with Functional CALM.

Let $(D,\sqsubseteq,\sqcup)$ be the state join-semilattice. The input is the
finite set $U$ of immutable payloads received so far, ordered by inclusion, and
the replica's state is $s(U)=s_0\sqcup\bigsqcup U$. Then $U\subseteq V$
implies $s(U)\sqsubseteq s(V)$, and $s(U_1)\sqcup s(U_2)=s(U_1\cup U_2)$ is the
least state \outrelpart both~\cite{shapiro2011crdt}. A client observation
$q:D\to(Y,\preceq_Y)$ gives the client-visible spec $q\circ s$. By
Functional CALM, $q\circ s$ is consistent exactly when $U\subseteq V$ implies
$q(s(U))\preceq_Y q(s(V))$. Monotonicity of $q$ on all of $D$ suffices, but
$q$ need only be monotone between reachable states. The query $\mathit{Add}\setminus\mathit{Del}$ over a two-phase set fails the test: a deleted item grows $U$ and shrinks the answer. Operation-based CRDTs fit too, with the causally closed set of
delivered operations as
input~\cite{shapiro2011crdt,burckhardt2014replicated,baquero2014operation,hellerstein2025turtles}.

CRDT states form a join-semilattice, but $q$ needs less:
Complete CALM requires only \emph{some} common \outnoun of query outputs
(\S\ref{sec:lifted-monotonicity}), not a least one.

\subsection{Highly available transactions}
\label{sec:hat-instance}

Bailis et al.\ define isolation levels by Adya's phenomena, adding sessions and
per-item dependencies, and give highly available algorithms for the weaker
levels~\cite{bailis2013hat,adya1999weakconsistency}. We take their
assumptions as given. Items are partially replicated, and a client sends each
operation to some replica of its item. A transaction's writes carry a unique
client timestamp, and versions are ordered by timestamp, last writer wins. The system never aborts a transaction on its own. A replica answers a read
with the highest-timestamped committed write of the item that it has received.

The HAT results illustrate the intersection reasoning of
Section~\ref{sec:intersection}. An isolation level and a store are separate
specs on the same histories: the level constrains which read answers may
appear together, and the store answers each read by its read rule. The store
held to a level is their intersection, and we ask which intersections satisfy
Lifted Monotonicity.

\begin{definition}[HAT specification]
\label{def:hat-spec}
An input is a history ordered by happens-before: reads, each tagged with its
replica; commits, each with its client timestamp and write set; and deliveries
of committed writes to replicas. The history preserves
each client's program order and each replica's arrival order, and a
transaction's reads precede its commit, which precedes its deliveries. Inputs are ordered by downward-closed extension. \textcolor{reviewed}{An output is a set of read
answers, ordered by inclusion.} \textcolor{reviewed}{A history is \emph{complete} when it answers
every read and orders versions by timestamp.} \textcolor{reviewed}{For an isolation level $L$, the table $P_L$ of $\Spec_L$ contains
an output at an input when the input extends to a complete history that includes the output and exhibits
none of the phenomena that $L$ proscribes.} \textcolor{reviewed}{The explicit relation $E$
contains, at each input, the output that the read rule above gives.}
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Store specification]
\label{def:store-spec}
The \emph{store specification} $\Spec_{\mathrm{st}}$ has the inputs and outputs
of Definition~\ref{def:hat-spec}. At each input, it permits any subset of the
answers that the read rule gives.
\end{definition}

The store promises its read rule and nothing about isolation. It satisfies
Lifted Monotonicity on its own: a read's answer under the read rule depends
only on the history preceding it, so later input never changes it, and the rule
is a monotone function of the input. Its full answers are the rows of $E$, and
the intersection $\Spec_{\mathrm{st}}\cap\Spec_L$
(Definition~\ref{def:intersection}) is the store held to isolation level $L$. It is total, since both permit the empty set of answers. By
Proposition~\ref{prop:intersection-bound}, the intersection keeps Lifted
Monotonicity when $\Spec_L$ permits the read rule's answers.

\begin{theorem}[HAT levels]
\label{thm:hat-levels}
\textcolor{reviewed}{Suppose each transaction's timestamp exceeds that of every version it reads.} \textcolor{reviewed}{For read uncommitted and read committed, $E\subseteq P_L$ and
$\Spec_{\mathrm{st}}\cap\Spec_L$ satisfies Lifted Monotonicity, so $\Spec_L$
satisfies Lifted Monotonicity for $E$, so some system for $\Spec_L$ has
Availability for $E$ and Partition Consistency.} For any level that proscribes
Lost Update, including snapshot isolation, repeatable read, and
serializability, $\Spec_{\mathrm{st}}\cap\Spec_L$ does not satisfy Lifted
Monotonicity, and $\Spec_L$ does not satisfy Lifted Monotonicity for any
explicit relation $E$ that answers every read; hence no system for $\Spec_L$
has Availability for such an $E$ and Partition Consistency.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
For read uncommitted and read committed, the timestamp condition rules out the
dependency cycles of G0 and G1c, so $\Spec_L$ permits the read rule's answers. Proposition~\ref{prop:intersection-bound}, with the read rule as the bound,
gives Lifted Monotonicity of $\Spec_{\mathrm{st}}\cap\Spec_L$, whose rows
include those of $E$. For the converse, let two transactions at different
replicas each read $x_0$ and write $x$. Each replica's answer is forced, and
their union contains a cycle on $x$ with an anti-dependency, which is Lost
Update. So the store's forced answer at one replica has no extension in
$\Spec_{\mathrm{st}}\cap\Spec_L$ once both transactions are in the input. The store needs no \commitment on its own, so this need comes from the
intersection with $\Spec_L$.
\end{proof}

\textcolor{reviewed}{The timestamp condition is required, correcting an error in the pseudocode of
the extended HAT paper~\cite{bailis2013hatextended}.} \textcolor{reviewed}{That paper's pseudocode
fixes each transaction's timestamp when it begins.} \textcolor{reviewed}{A transaction can then read
a version with a higher timestamp than its own and overwrite it, so its write
is ordered before the version it read: a G1c cycle, which read committed
proscribes.} \textcolor{reviewed}{The algorithm as written therefore does not guarantee read
committed.} \textcolor{reviewed}{Our framework exposed the problem directly as we were writing this
up, since the read rule's output at such an input is not in $P_{\mathrm{RC}}$.} \textcolor{reviewed}{Taking each timestamp at commit, above every version read, repairs the
algorithm.}

Session guarantees, which Bailis et al.\ achieve under sticky availability,
are deployments in our terms (Proposition~\ref{thm:complete-cap-deployment});
we do not treat them here.

Mahajan et al.\ showed that no consistency semantics stronger than real-time
causal consistency can be enforced in an always-available, one-way convergent
system~\cite{mahajan2011consistency}. Their availability, that every
request is eventually answered, is a response obligation in our terms
(Appendix~\ref{app:cap-response}). Their result is a maximality claim:
real-time causal consistency is achievable, and no stronger semantics is. With real-time precedence recorded in the input, we recover both halves
(Appendix~\ref{app:rtc}). The spec is the real-time causal product
interface, in which each replica answers its own reads. Its implementation
uses no coordination mechanism, and no strict strengthening shares that
property, provided that the strengthening, like their implementations, does not
consult real time. One-way convergence, which requires that a node able to
receive from another eventually reflect the updates the other knows, needs no
separate assumption, since the input records which writes each replica has
received.

\subsection{Invariant confluence}
\label{sec:iconfluence-instance}

An invariant is a predicate that every state must satisfy. We follow the model
of Bailis et al.~\cite{bailis2014coordination}. A database state is a set of
versions, and replicas merge states by union. Each transaction $t\in T$ runs
on the state of the replica that executes it and adds the versions it writes,
so $t(s)\supseteq s$. An initial state $s_0$ satisfies the invariant
$\mathcal I$. \textcolor{reviewed}{A state is \emph{reachable} when it can be derived from $s_0$ by
applying transactions and merging states, with every state along the way
satisfying $\mathcal I$.} Every reachable state descends from $s_0$, so $T$ is
\emph{invariant confluent} exactly when $\mathcal I(s_1\cup s_2)$ for all
reachable $s_1$ and $s_2$.

From an instance $(s_0,T,\mathcal I)$ of their model we construct a
spec $\Spec_{\mathcal I}$ in ours as follows (Appendix~\ref{app:iconfluence}). Inputs are \emph{transaction histories}: partial orders of events, each
labeled by a transaction in $T$, where one event precedes another when the
second ran on a state that included the first's writes. \textcolor{reviewed}{One history \inrel
another when it adds events, none of which precedes an event already present.} \textcolor{reviewed}{Legal inputs include only the \emph{locally valid} histories, in which each
transaction preserves $\mathcal I$ on the state it ran on.} \textcolor{reviewed}{Outputs are
database states, ordered by set-containment of versioned data.} \textcolor{reviewed}{The
\emph{state} of a history is $s_0$ together with every version its
transactions write.} \textcolor{reviewed}{Call a sub-history \emph{closed} when it contains every
event that precedes one of its own, and \emph{valid} when the state of each of
its closed sub-histories satisfies $\mathcal I$.} \textcolor{reviewed}{At input $h$, each valid
closed sub-history of $h$ contributes its state as a permitted output.} \textcolor{reviewed}{An
output may therefore leave out some of the input's transactions, but what it
includes must be closed and valid.}

\begin{proposition}[Invariant confluence and Lifted Monotonicity]
\label{prop:iconfluence-lm}
\textcolor{reviewed}{$T$ is invariant confluent if and only if $\Spec_{\mathcal I}$ satisfies Lifted
Monotonicity.}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
See Appendix~\ref{app:iconfluence}.
\end{proof}

The uniqueness constraint of Bailis et al.'s payroll example shows the failure
directly. Two replicas each create an employee and assign ID~7, each running
on a state in which ID~7 is unused. The history in which neither creation ran
on a state that included the other's writes is locally valid and \inrel both one-creation histories, but its state has two
employees with ID~7, so no state it permits \outrel both. The system must
reserve IDs, reject one creation, or weaken the invariant. Concurrency,
not the transactions alone, decides the outcome: a transaction that is harmless
after another can, when it runs on a state without the other's writes, produce a state whose merge
with the other's is invalid.

Invariant preservation and isolation are expressed by different specs: $\Spec_{\mathcal I}$ constrains exposed database states, while an
isolation level constrains exposed read and commit facts. Kaki et
al.~\cite{kaki2018alone} relate the two for a given program: they infer the
weakest isolation level a store offers under which each transaction still
preserves the declared invariants.

\subsection{General operational specification models}
\label{sec:operational-models}
An I/O spec declaratively relates cumulative inputs to permitted
outputs. An I/O automaton instead defines operational states, input, output,
and internal actions, and a transition relation~\cite{lynch1987io,lynch1996distributed}. Projecting its externally visible behavior can yield an I/O spec. Such operational models answer how a declared machine may execute and how one
machine implements another. Our goal is narrower: the possibility relation,
its orders, and its remainder tables expose which observable alternatives
remain, which removals preserve prior information, and which are binding
\commitments. Section~\ref{sec:distributed-execution} provides a projection
from distributed histories, connecting our abstraction back to
participant-local executions and message causality. Thus our specification
model does not replace an operational formalism; it is a semantic projection
designed to make the intrinsic coordination question comparable across such
formalisms.

\paragraph{Compiling a state machine.}
\textcolor{reviewed}{Automata are a traditional way to model operational behavior
abstractly~\cite{lynch1987io,lynch1996distributed}.} \textcolor{reviewed}{Our model keeps only the
inputs and outputs of that behavior and drops the operational details.} \textcolor{reviewed}{The
mapping is straightforward: for each input, record the outputs of the runs that
consume it.} \textcolor{reviewed}{Work that models systems as state machines therefore maps
naturally into our model, where Complete CALM assesses its consistency
directly.} We now make the mapping precise, and then explain how it is used in
practice.

We take the standard I/O-automaton model~\cite{lynch1987io} of a state
machine, its clients, and the channel that carries their state-machine events. The machine
$M$ has states $Q$, an initial state $q_0$, receive transitions for
state-machine events that accept every payload in every state, output transitions that
emit an output to a client and may change state, internal transitions, and an
observation $\mathrm{obs}:Q\to O$. There are finitely many clients. Each may send a state-machine event with
any payload at any time, and receives the machine's outputs. A channel
carries each state-machine event at most once to the machine's input queue,
possibly reordering events on the way, and the machine consumes the queue in
order. In an execution of the
composed automaton, \emph{happens-before} is Lamport's
order~\cite{lamport1978time}: the transitive closure of the local order at the
machine and at each client and of the edge from sending a state-machine event to receiving
it. An output of the machine is an input of the client that receives it, so a
client's later sends happen after everything the machine had received when it
emitted that output.

\begin{definition}[Input of an execution]
\label{def:execution-input}
The \emph{input} of a finite execution is the set of state-machine events the machine has
received, ordered by happens-before between their sends; $h\InputOrd h'$ when
$h\subseteq h'$ and the two orders agree on $h$.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Induced specification]
\label{def:induced-specification}
The specification \emph{induced} by $M$ and its environment pairs each input
(Definition~\ref{def:execution-input}) that occurs with the machine's
observation at the end of every execution with that input.
\end{definition}

This is not a distributed execution in the sense of
Section~\ref{sec:distributed-execution}. The machine is a single
participant; the clients and the channel are its environment, and outputs
flow to the environment as in Section~\ref{sec:election-story}, where the
environment may use an output when choosing later input. Happens-before here
only records which state-machine events each client sent after which
outputs, so the input remembers what the environment's choices depended on. Distributing the machine is then direct: the clients remain the environment,
each client's state-machine events are sent to some participant, and among participants
Section~\ref{sec:distributed-execution} applies, using the induced
spec.

\begin{proposition}[Induced specifications]
\label{prop:induced-specification}
\leavevmode
\begin{enumerate}
\item
The induced specification is an \spectype, and the input
        of an execution only extends as the execution continues.
\item
When there is one client and its state-machine events arrive in the order it sent
        them, every input is a chain, inputs correspond to state-machine event sequences
        ordered by prefix, and the fiber at a sequence collects the
        observations of the machine's runs that receive it in order,
        interleaved with output and internal steps. If output and internal
        steps leave the state unchanged and receiving is deterministic, the
        specification is functional and consistent exactly when
        $\mathrm{obs}(q)\OutputOrd\mathrm{obs}(q')$ for every receive
        transition $q\to q'$ from a reachable state.
\item
Every observation in the fiber at $h$ is reached by a run of the
        machine that receives each state-machine event of $h$ once, interleaved with output
        and internal steps. If the channel may reorder and clients observe no
        outputs, every such run is reached, whatever the order of its
        receives.
\item
The machine's observations are confluent across its executions
        exactly when the induced specification satisfies Lifted Monotonicity. Here confluence means that for executions with inputs
        $h_1,\ldots,h_k$ and any input $h$ that occurs and extends each $h_r$,
        some execution with input $h$ ends in an observation that \outrel
        each of theirs.
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
(1) Inclusion with agreeing orders is a partial order, every input that occurs
has a row, and extending an execution adds received state-machine events without changing
happens-before among earlier sends. (2) A single client's sends are totally
ordered, and they arrive in that order, so an input is determined by its
sequence of payloads, and the machine's steps in an execution form a run of
the machine automaton; in the special case, the claim is monotonicity along
receive transitions, and Functional CALM. (3) The machine's steps in an
execution form such a run; without outputs to clients, happens-before orders
only each client's own sends, so all sends can precede the machine's run. (4) Unfold the fiber in the
common-extension condition and apply Complete CALM
(Theorem~\ref{thm:complete-calm}, Proposition~\ref{prop:common-expansion-form}).
\end{proof}

The input orders state-machine events by their sends, not by their arrivals: the machine
receives state-machine events in one total order, but that order is what differs between
executions. Outputs are where the environment's reactions enter. A client
that sends after observing an output is ordered after everything that output
reflected, and the input records that dependence; what the client saw is
recorded by the run's output events, which keep only rows that \outrelbase
it.

\paragraph{Testing and proof.}
The induced spec is defined by every finite execution of the machine
and its environment. There are infinitely many such executions, so the
induced spec is a semantic object: we can reason about it, but not
list it. Two standard approaches make that reasoning practical.

The first is testing. A simulator or model checker runs the machine and its
environment on bounded inputs~\cite{misra1986des,flanagan2005dpor}; the
input/output pairs it collects form a finite spec in our model,
which can be checked for violations of Lifted Monotonicity. This is routine engineering
practice and has found serious bugs in production
systems~\cite{newcombe2015aws,zhou2021foundationdb}. Testing suits
refutation. If Lifted Monotonicity fails, finitely many executions show it:
executions with inputs $h_1,\ldots,h_k$, and a common \indesc $h$ at which no
execution's observation \outrel all of theirs. A violation found in the
finite spec is such a witness when the exploration covers every
execution with input $h$, so that its fiber there is complete. Testing cannot show that
Lifted Monotonicity holds, since that is a claim about every input.

The second is proof. Instead of computing the induced spec, a
designer writes one down. A \emph{declared} spec $D$ gives, for each
input, a set of outputs by a rule, such as ``the set of ballots received'' or
``the largest timestamp seen''; it does not enumerate executions. The proof
then shows that $D$ describes the machine well enough that Lifted Monotonicity
can be checked on $D$ instead. Two things must be shown, and both are needed,
because neither half alone preserves Lifted Monotonicity: if $D$ omits an
output the machine can produce, it may hide an incompatibility, and if $D$
adds an output the machine cannot produce, it may supply a common \outnoun
that no execution reaches.
\begin{itemize}
\item
\emph{Realizability.}  Every output that $D$ allows at an input is the
        observation of some execution with that input.
\item
\emph{Domination.}  Every execution's final observation is
        \outrelpast by some output that $D$ allows at its input.
\end{itemize}
$D$ need not match the machine exactly: it may leave out observations that
others \outrelbase, such as transient intermediate states.

\begin{proposition}[Declared specifications]
\label{prop:declared-specification}
Let $D$ be an \spectype whose inputs correspond, by an order
isomorphism, to the inputs that occur. If $D$ satisfies realizability and
domination, then $D$ satisfies Lifted Monotonicity exactly when the induced
specification does, and so exactly when the machine's observations are
confluent across its executions.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
We use the common-extension form. Given rows of the induced spec, we
dominate each by a row of $D$, take their common \outnoun in $D$, and realize
it. Conversely, rows of $D$ are rows of the induced spec, and their
common \outnoun there is dominated back into $D$.
\end{proof}

Both obligations are standard in the verification literature. Domination
says that the machine refines $D$: every observation of every execution is
allowed by $D$, up to refinement of outputs. It is proved with an
\emph{invariant}, a property of the machine's state that holds initially, is
preserved by every step, and implies that the observation is dominated; it is
checked one step at a time, never by enumerating executions. This is the core
of refinement proofs for state machines, which Lynch and Vaandrager call a
forward simulation~\cite{lynch1995forward} and Abadi and Lamport a refinement
mapping~\cite{abadi1991refinement}; such proofs are routinely mechanized for
practical distributed systems~\cite{hawblitzel2015ironfleet}. Realizability
is the converse inclusion, that $D$ allows nothing the machine cannot produce. It too is proved once for all inputs, typically by induction on the input,
giving a uniform recipe that builds, for any declared row, an execution that
produces it. The proposition asks for nothing beyond these two facts. It
says that together they let $D$ stand in for the machine: Lifted Monotonicity
is checked on $D$ exactly as everywhere else in this paper, and nothing about
coordination is proved about the machine itself.

\subsection{Scope}
\label{sec:scope}

\textcolor{reviewed}{Our conditions are semantic.} Confluence guarantees that a common \outnoun
exists, not that a deployed system reaches it. A participant may lack the
authority to emit it (Appendix~\ref{app:deployment}), and reaching it takes an
algorithm, message delivery, and a termination argument. Participant runs
likewise set aside delivery and strategy details. Protocols,
liveness, and cost belong to the operational model in which a spec is
realized.


\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\textcolor{reviewed}{The computing literature is rich with coordination mechanisms for managing
concurrency within and across machines.} \textcolor{reviewed}{The CALM Conjecture asked a question
still latent at the time: when are these mechanisms required, and why?} \textcolor{reviewed}{It drew
on declarative networking~\cite{loo2009declarative}, which, in Codd's tradition
of separating specification from implementation~\cite{codd1970relational}, wrote
the desired effects of protocols as queries to be analyzed and optimized into a
variety of mechanisms.} \textcolor{reviewed}{Could a signature property of declarative
languages---monotonicity---be the criterion?}

\textcolor{reviewed}{The intuition was sharpened by a related friction in the literature on parallel
query processing.} \textcolor{reviewed}{The Google MapReduce
paper~\cite{dean2004mapreduce} departed from prior work on parallel query
processing~\cite{wilschut1991dataflow,graefe1990volcano}, conflating data
redistribution (shuffle) with barrier coordination required for a non-monotone
reduce operator.} \textcolor{reviewed}{This coupling of unrelated concerns required monotone operators
like join to block in the MapReduce model unnecessarily.} \textcolor{reviewed}{Cost models that count
rounds, from BSP supersteps to MapReduce rounds, carry the same
conflation~\cite{valiant1990bsp,karloff2010mrc}.}

\textcolor{reviewed}{The CALM Conjecture hypothesized that the database community's more judicious
use of barriers could be generalized: coordination---of any kind---is required
if and only if the program is non-monotone.} \textcolor{reviewed}{Ameloot et al.\
proved it, but only for relational languages, which to date have
still not become mainstream beyond query processing (more on this below).}

This paper moves the declarations one level higher, from queries to
specs: all that matters is a spec's possibility relation over ordered input
and output domains. \textcolor{reviewed}{Eliding the query logic frees the analysis from any particular
language and broadens what can be specified.} \textcolor{reviewed}{Prior work focused on implementing
complex programs
declaratively~\cite{loo2005overlays,loo2009declarative,chu2007declarative,alvaro2011bloom}.} \textcolor{reviewed}{This was interesting ``declarative engineering'' with concrete benefits in
analysis and optimization.} \textcolor{reviewed}{But the higher-level model here also captures abstract
families of guarantees, like CAP and isolation levels, which have no application or
protocol in mind.}

\textcolor{reviewed}{Lifting the abstraction improves the power of analysis as well.} \textcolor{reviewed}{Prior program
analyses became enmeshed in the details of implementations, however
declarative.} \textcolor{reviewed}{Bloom~\cite{alvaro2011bloom} flagged syntactic non-monotonicity in
programs and conservatively injected coordination code in front of it.} \textcolor{reviewed}{Yet
coordination code is itself non-monotone, so adding it to a Bloom program only
adds more non-monotone syntax.} \textcolor{reviewed}{Subsequent work moved the question from syntax to declared contracts and
invariants~\cite{sivaramakrishnan2015quelea,kaki2018alone}.} \textcolor{reviewed}{Their tools pick,
for each operation or transaction, the weakest consistency or isolation level
that the store offers and that still satisfies the declared properties.} \textcolor{reviewed}{We
ask what the spec itself requires, independent of any menu of
levels.} Because the canonical \commitments are universal
(\S\ref{sec:systems}), the answer holds for every mechanism. \textcolor{reviewed}{Our
composition results need no program logic, and the criteria are exact on
specs, not conservative.}

The possibility relation shows where coordination is needed. Growing input can remove possibilities. Emitting an output can remove others: a removed
possibility that some remaining output extension subsumes is an incidental
decision, and one that nothing remaining subsumes is a fateful decision. Only
the fateful removals are \commitments, and coordination mechanisms enforce
them.

\textcolor{reviewed}{A limitation of prior CALM work was that it was constructive on only one side of
its boundary.} \textcolor{reviewed}{Monotone programs need no coordination; for a non-monotone
program, CALM said only that coordination was required somewhere, not what it
had to accomplish or where it belonged.} \textcolor{reviewed}{Complete CALM says more.} \textcolor{reviewed}{A
spec that is not monotone needs the specific \commitments its outputs
require.} \textcolor{reviewed}{Every coordination mechanism, from locks to consensus, is a way of
applying these \commitments.} \textcolor{reviewed}{The same analysis says which outputs need
\commitments and whether conflicting choices can arise at another participant.} \textcolor{reviewed}{That tells an implementation when it needs agreement rather than a local
decision.} \textcolor{reviewed}{Proper coordination
then places the \commitments in a composition: it identifies the stage that
must make them, as the log does in state-machine replication, and certifies
that the stages behind it need none of their own.}

\textcolor{reviewed}{Underlying these issues is a reassessment of
tradition: what is a declarative specification, and how little does it require
to be useful?} \textcolor{reviewed}{The question is timely.} \textcolor{reviewed}{Coding agents increasingly write
imperative code, often rife with bugs, which has renewed interest in formal
specification languages like Lean~\cite{demoura2021lean4}.} \textcolor{reviewed}{A higher-level
language keeps agents out of the incidental complexity of implementations and
pairs them with proofs of correctness: neurosymbolic
computing~\cite{an2025neurosymbolic}.} \textcolor{reviewed}{This is a renewed opportunity for logic,
dataflow, and functional languages.} \textcolor{reviewed}{Today these languages typically specify
protocols and programs in detail, as in declarative networking; this paper
points to specs at a higher level still, opening analyses like ours and
potential program synthesis and optimization.}

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

A spec abstracts execution details into a possibility relation over
ordered input and output domains. The results here reduce to one question: can a participant act on what it knows
without excluding a possibility that another run may still need? Mutexes,
locks, atomic instructions, and agreement protocols prevent such contradictions
by applying \commitments. \textcolor{reviewed}{Functional CALM answers the question for functions: a functional spec
stays confluent as input arrives exactly when it is monotone, and then no
output needs a \commitment.} This generalizes the classic result from logical fact
sets to arbitrary input and output orders.

\textcolor{reviewed}{Complete CALM extends the answer to specs that offer several outputs
for one input.} The test is Lifted Monotonicity, which combines Relational
Monotonicity with the requirement that the spec has directed fibers. \textcolor{reviewed}{When the test fails, some reachable output
requires a \commitment, which rules out an input or some of its outputs.} \textcolor{reviewed}{Because the model makes \commitments explicit, it can also show where a
composition coordinates (\S\ref{sec:composition}).} For example, a totally ordered log
and a key-value store are each non-monotone on their own, yet when the log
guards the store, the log's \commitments are all the composition needs, and
the store needs none. Multi-Paxos is one way to distribute those
\commitments.

Complete CAP is the partition form of the same equivalence. A system can keep
the outputs selected by a spec's explicit relation available, and
consistent, through partitions exactly when they have a common \outnoun at
every common \indesc of their inputs; when the relation selects every output,
this is Lifted Monotonicity.

The generality of our specification model opens a range of new questions
regarding the nondeterminism of specs and their \commitments. \textcolor{reviewed}{One direction
extends the boolean criteria of this paper to metrics: a Determination
Complexity theory that assesses \emph{how many} \commitments are intrinsically
needed to determine a spec.} \textcolor{reviewed}{Another is Determination Provenance: a
given output---or its absence---is determined not only by inputs, but also by
the \commitments made in a run, which brings formal models of provenance to
concurrent systems.} \textcolor{reviewed}{A third direction is to provide general-purpose static
analysis of CALM and proper coordination in a real programming framework.}

\paragraph{Relational notation.}
To describe how execution narrows $P$, we use a compact relational notation. For any relation
$R\subseteq I\times O$, we select rows by writing a predicate on its
columns $\mathrm{in}$ and $\mathrm{out}$ in square brackets: $R[\mathrm{in}\InputGe j]$ is the table of rows
whose input \inrel $j$, $R[\mathrm{out}\OutputGe o]$ is the table of rows
whose output \outrel $o$, and $R[\mathrm{in}\in K]$ is
the table of rows with inputs in $K$. Commas separate conjuncts:
$R[\mathrm{in}\InputGe j,\ \mathrm{out}\OutputGe o]$ is the table of rows
satisfying both predicates. To project onto a single column, we write $\pi_{\mathrm{in}}(R)$ and
$\pi_{\mathrm{out}}(R)$ for the sets of inputs and outputs that appear in $R$. We say that a row $(k,u)$ is \emph{at input} $k$; $R[\mathrm{in}=k]$ is the table of
rows at $k$.

\subsection{Proofs for Section~\ref{sec:functional-calm}}
\label{app:proofs-sec2}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Lemma~\ref{lem:run-residual}]
The run starts at the canonical state $\sigma_P(i_0)$, whose table is
$P[\mathrm{in}\InputGe i_0]$. Each input event $\mathsf{input}(j)$ keeps only
the rows with $\mathrm{in}\InputGe j$ (Equation~\ref{eq:input-event}), and each
output event $\mathsf{output}(o)$ keeps only the rows with
$\mathrm{out}\OutputGe o$ (Equation~\ref{eq:output-action}). The input
conditions reduce to the last one, $\mathrm{in}\InputGe i$, because each later
input is an extension of the earlier ones. One output condition remains for each
emitted output.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Proposition~\ref{prop:confluence}]
A common state with current input $j$ has a row at $j$, and since rows are
never added, that row belongs to every $R_r$. Conversely, let $(j,u)$ belong
to every $R_r$. Then $\mathsf{input}(j)$ is defined at every $(i_r,R_r)$, and
$\mathsf{output}(u)$ is defined at $(j,R_r[\mathrm{in}\InputGe j])$, since
$u\in R_r(j)$. By
Lemma~\ref{lem:run-residual}, $(j,u)\in R_r$ says that $u$ \outrel every output
emitted on run $r$. After $\mathsf{input}(j)$ and $\mathsf{output}(u)$, the
same lemma leaves exactly
$P[\mathrm{in}\InputGe j,\ \mathrm{out}\OutputGe u]$, independent of the
starting run.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:functional-calm}]
Every input has one row, so an output emitted at current input $k$ is $f(k)$. By Lemma~\ref{lem:run-residual}, a run ending at input $i$ keeps the
row $(j,f(j))$ at an \indesc $j$ of $i$ exactly when $f(j)$ \outrel every
output the run has emitted. By Proposition~\ref{prop:confluence}, a finite
family of runs is therefore confluent at a common \indesc $j$ exactly
when $f(j)$ \outrel every output emitted on any of them. If $f$ is monotone,
this holds, since each emitted $f(k)$ has $k\InputOrd j$. \textcolor{reviewed}{If $f$ is not
monotone, some $i\InputOrd k$ has $f(i)\not\OutputOrd f(k)$.} Of two
runs from $\sigma_P(i)$, one that emits $f(i)$ no longer has the row
$(k,f(k))$, while one that accepts $\mathsf{input}(k)$ keeps it; no row at $k$
survives in both, so they are not confluent at $k$.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Corollary~\ref{cor:state-by-input}]
By Functional CALM (Theorem~\ref{thm:functional-calm}), the spec is
monotone. By Lemma~\ref{lem:run-residual}, the run's remainder table is
$P[\mathrm{in}\InputGe i,\ \mathrm{out}\OutputGe o_1,\ \ldots,\
\mathrm{out}\OutputGe o_m]$, where each $o_\ell=f(i_\ell)$ for some
$i_\ell\InputOrd i$. Every row whose input \inrel $i$ has the form $(k,f(k))$ with
$i_\ell\InputOrd k$, so monotonicity gives $o_\ell\OutputOrd f(k)$, and the
output conditions remove nothing.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Proposition~\ref{prop:functional-exposure-boundary}]
Suppose $f$ is monotone, and let a \commitment-free run reach $(i,R)$. By Functional CALM and Corollary~\ref{cor:state-by-input},
$(i,R)=\sigma_P(i)$, so the legal $j\InputGe i$ are exactly the \indescs of
$i$, and monotonicity gives $f(i)\OutputOrd f(j)$ at each, so $f(i)$ is a
legal output (Definition~\ref{def:exposable}). Conversely, if every such output is a legal output, then at $\sigma_P(i)$,
which the empty run reaches, legality of $f(i)$ gives $f(i)\OutputOrd f(j)$
for every \indesc $j$ of $i$, so $f$ is monotone.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Proofs for Section~\ref{sec:complete-calm}}
\label{app:proofs-sec3}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Proposition~\ref{prop:common-expansion-form}]
Suppose the spec satisfies Lifted Monotonicity, and let $(i_1,o_1),\ldots,(i_k,o_k)$ be rows
with common \indesc $j$. If $k=0$, any $u\in\fiber{j}$ will do, and since
$\Spec$ is total, $j$ appears in some row $(j,u)$. Otherwise, since $i_r\InputOrd j$, Relational Monotonicity gives, for each
$r$, a $u_r\in\fiber{j}$ with $o_r\OutputOrd u_r$. The $u_r$ all lie in the
one fiber $\fiber{j}$, which is directed, so some $u\in\fiber{j}$ \outrel
every $u_r$, hence every $o_r$. Conversely, let $i\InputOrd j$ and
$X\subseteq\fiber{i}$ be nonempty and finite. The rows $\{(i,x):x\in X\}$
have common \indesc $j$, so some $u\in\fiber{j}$ \outrel all of $X$; that is
$\fiber{i}\LiftedOrd\fiber{j}$.
\end{proof}

The Survivor test (Proposition~\ref{prop:confluence}) and
Lemma~\ref{lem:run-residual} combine into the form that the later arguments
use.

\begin{lemma}[Reconciliation]
\label{lem:reconciliation}
Let runs end at $(i_r,R_r)$, $r=1,\ldots,m$, let $j$ be a common \indesc of
the $i_r$, and let $X$ be the set of outputs emitted on any of them. The runs
are confluent at $j$ if and only if some $u\in\fiber{j}$ \outrel every output
in $X$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
By Lemma~\ref{lem:run-residual}, $(j,u)\in R_r$ exactly when $u\in\fiber{j}$
\outrel every output emitted on run $r$, and Proposition~\ref{prop:confluence}
gives the claim.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:complete-calm}]
\textcolor{reviewed}{By Proposition~\ref{prop:common-expansion-form} it suffices to treat the
common-extension condition.} ($\Leftarrow$) Let finitely many runs end at
$(i_r,R_r)$ with common \indesc $j$. Each output $o$ emitted on a run was
emitted at some input $i_o\InputOrd j$, so $(i_o,o)$ is a row of $P$; the
condition supplies $u\in\fiber{j}$ \outrelpart all of them, and
Lemma~\ref{lem:reconciliation} gives confluence. ($\Rightarrow$) Given rows $(i_r,o_r)$ with common \indesc $j$, consider
the runs that start at $\sigma_P(i_r)$ and emit $o_r$. Consistency and
Lemma~\ref{lem:reconciliation}, applied to them, give some $u\in\fiber{j}$
\outrelpart every $o_r$. For the empty family, totality gives
$\fiber{j}\neq\varnothing$.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Proposition~\ref{prop:exposure-boundary}]
Suppose the common-extension condition holds; by Theorem~\ref{thm:complete-calm}, the
spec is consistent. Let $(i,R)$ be reached by a run (\commitment-free or not), and let $o\in R(i)$, $j\InputGe i$ legal, and
$x\in R(j)$. We extend the run in two ways: by $\mathsf{output}(o)$, and by
$\mathsf{input}(j)$ followed by $\mathsf{output}(x)$. By consistency the two extended runs are confluent at $j$, so
Proposition~\ref{prop:confluence} supplies a row $(j,u)$ in both tables. Both
tables are contained in $R$, so $u\in R(j)$ \outrel $o$ and $x$, and the legal-output test
(Proposition~\ref{prop:exposability}) makes $o$ a legal output. Conversely, suppose every such output is a legal output, and let rows
$(i_1,o_1),\ldots,(i_k,o_k)$ have common \indesc $j$. By totality there is some
$u_0\in P(j)$. For $r=1,\ldots,k$ in turn, the legal-output test for $o_r$ at
$\sigma_P(i_r)$, applied with $x=u_{r-1}$, gives $u_r\in P(j)$ \outrelpart both
$o_r$ and $u_{r-1}$. Then $u_k$ \outrel every $o_r$.
\end{proof}

\paragraph{Pairs suffice for the common-extension condition.}
\label{app:pairs-suffice}
Suppose every two rows with a common \indesc $j$ have a common \outnoun in
$\fiber{j}$. Then the condition holds for every finite family of rows with
common \indesc $j$. We replace the first two rows of the family by a row
$(j,u)$ whose output $u$ \outrel both, and repeat until one row remains; a
single row, paired with any row at $j$, has an \outnoun in $\fiber{j}$. The
pairwise hypothesis must cover all rows of $P$, including rows at $j$ itself,
since the replacement rows are at $j$. Checking only rows at single-proposal
inputs does not suffice, even with families of up to $k$ rows: in the $k$-set agreement example of
Appendix~\ref{app:additional-applications}, every family of at most $k$ rows
at such inputs is compatible at each common \indesc, but the $k+1$ proposal
rows are not.

\subsection{Proofs for Section~\ref{sec:distributed-execution}}
\label{app:proofs-sec4}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Proposition~\ref{prop:distributed-consistency}]
A cut is confluent at $j$ exactly when its participant runs are
(\S\ref{sec:distributed-consistency}). The participant runs at any cut form a
finite family of runs, so consistency gives distributed consistency. Conversely, every finite family of runs is the family of participant runs, at
the final cut, of a message-free history with one participant per run, so
distributed consistency gives consistency.
\end{proof}

\paragraph{Exchanging outputs.}
\label{app:exchanging-outputs}
Suppose participants share their \emph{outputs} in the data $d$ of messages
(\S\ref{sec:distributed-consistency}). A participant that receives an
output $o$ its sender has emitted has two
options. If $o\in R(i)$ at its own current input $i$, it can emit $o$ itself;
that is an ordinary output event, already covered by
Definition~\ref{def:distributed-history}. Otherwise, or in addition, it can silently honor $o$ by restricting its table
to $R[\mathrm{out}\OutputGe o]$. This restriction is a table, not a
specification state: it may have no row at the participant's current input,
so silent honoring is not a specification event and we reason about it at the level of
tables. Silent honoring cannot change confluence in either direction. It cannot
help, because it only removes rows, and confluence asks for a row that
survives everywhere (Proposition~\ref{prop:confluence}). It cannot hurt,
because a row that survives at a common \indesc already \outrel every output
emitted in the cut (Lemma~\ref{lem:run-residual}), including $o$, which was previously emitted. The confluence of a cut therefore depends on which outputs were emitted,
not on who has heard about them.

\begin{proof}[Proof of Proposition~\ref{prop:commitment-conflict}]
Events only remove rows, so $(k,x)$ is present at $\sigma_P(i)$. Thus $k$
\inrel $i$ and $x\in\fiber{k}$, so $\mathsf{input}(k)$ and then
$\mathsf{output}(x)$ are enabled from $\sigma_P(i)$, and the second run
exists. After $\mathsf{output}(x)$, every row at $k$ \outrel $x$. The
\commitment leaves no such row at $k$
(Equation~\ref{eq:commitment-unsubsumed}); if it removed every row at $k$,
there is none at all. Later events only remove rows, so no row at $k$
survives in both runs, and by Proposition~\ref{prop:confluence} the runs are
not confluent at $k$.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:complete-cap}]
The canonical system for $E$ has the canonical basis and no \commitment
events; its strategies emit, by the canonical output event $\mathsf{output}(o)$
of Section~\ref{sec:complete-calm}, any output $o$ of $E$ in the current fiber. That event applies whatever \commitment $o$ requires, $x_o$ from the
canonical basis (Definition~\ref{def:system-output-event}), so the system has
Availability for $E$ whatever the spec. Suppose $\Spec$ satisfies Lifted
Monotonicity for $E$. Every output the canonical system emits is the output of a row of $E$ whose
input lies below the current one, so at a common \indesc $j$ of the current
inputs the emitted outputs have a common \outnoun in $\fiber{j}$. Since the
system emits only canonical output events, Lemma~\ref{lem:reconciliation}
makes this confluence at $j$, which gives Partition Consistency.

Conversely, suppose some system, such as the canonical system for $E$, has
Availability for $E$ and Partition Consistency. Let $(i_1,o_1),\ldots,(i_k,o_k)$ be rows of $E$ with a common
\indesc $j$; we find some $u\in\fiber{j}$ that \outrel every $o_r$. If $k=0$,
$\Spec$ is total, so $j$ appears in some row $(j,u)$. Otherwise, take one
participant $p_r$ per row, starting at input $i_r$, each on its own side,
and, if $k=1$, a second participant for that row on another side, since a
partitioned history has at least two sides. Each runs without messages. By Availability for $E$, each $p_r$ can emit $o_r$. By Partition Consistency,
they then reach one state $(j,R)$, which has some row $(j,u)$
(Definition~\ref{def:residual-state}); this $u$ is the one we seek. Rows are
never added, so $(j,u)$ was already in each $p_r$'s table just after it
emitted $o_r$. Emitting an output keeps only rows whose output \outrel it
(Definition~\ref{def:lifted-output-event}), so $u$ \outrel every
$o_r$.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Corollary~\ref{cor:complete-cap-any-system}]
The converse argument in the proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:complete-cap} applies
to any system with Availability for $E$ and Partition Consistency, so $\Spec$
satisfies Lifted Monotonicity for $E$. By Theorem~\ref{thm:complete-cap},
$\Spec$ escapes CAP for $E$.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Invariant confluence (\S\ref{sec:iconfluence-instance})}
\label{app:iconfluence}

The input records which transactions ran and whose writes each one ran on. A \emph{transaction history} $h$ (here simply \emph{history}) is a finite
partial order of transaction events, each labeled by a transaction in $T$;
$e'<e$ means that $e$ ran on a state that included the writes of $e'$. A
sub-history is \emph{closed} when it contains every event that precedes one of
its own, that is, when it is a downward-closed sub-order. History $h'$
\inrel $h$ when $h$ is a closed sub-history of $h'$, so the events $h'$ adds
precede no event of $h$, and each event of $h$ keeps exactly the predecessors
it had. The state of a history is computed
event by event. An event $e$, labeled by the transaction $t_e$, runs on
$\mathrm{pre}(e)=s_0\cup\bigcup_{e'<e}\mathrm{post}(e')$ and produces
$\mathrm{post}(e)=t_e(\mathrm{pre}(e))$, and the state of $h$ is
$\mathrm{eval}(h)=s_0\cup\bigcup_{e\in h}\mathrm{post}(e)$. Because $h$ is a
partial order and transactions are inflationary ($t(s)\supseteq s$),
$\mathrm{pre}(e)$ is the state of the sub-history strictly below $e$, and
$\mathrm{post}(e)$ is the state of the sub-history at or below $e$. A
closed sub-history keeps each event's predecessors, and so its
$\mathrm{pre}$ and $\mathrm{post}$; hence the state of a union of
closed sub-histories is the union of their states. A history is
\emph{locally valid} when every $\mathrm{pre}(e)$ and $\mathrm{post}(e)$
satisfies $\mathcal I$, as when replicas abort transactions that would violate
the invariant on their own state. It is \emph{valid} when the
state of each of its closed sub-histories satisfies $\mathcal I$:
$\mathcal I(\mathrm{eval}(c))$ for every closed sub-history $c$.

The spec $\Spec_{\mathcal I}$ has the locally valid histories as inputs;
a closed sub-history of a locally valid history is locally valid, so the
inputs are closed under the order. An output is a state, and $u$ \outrel $s$ when $s\subseteq u$. \textcolor{reviewed}{At input $h$,
each valid closed sub-history of $h$ contributes its state as a permitted
output:}
\[
  P_{\mathcal I}(h)=\{\mathrm{eval}(g):g\text{ a valid closed
  sub-history of }h\}.
\]
\textcolor{reviewed}{An output may therefore leave out some of the input's transactions, but what
it includes must be closed and valid.} Taking $g$ empty gives
$s_0$, so the spec is total.

\begin{proof}[Proof of Proposition~\ref{prop:iconfluence-lm}]
We use the common-extension form of Lifted Monotonicity
(Proposition~\ref{prop:common-expansion-form}). First, the
state of a valid history $g$ is reachable, by induction on $g$: if $e$ is
maximal in $g$, then $g\setminus\{e\}$ and the sub-history strictly below $e$
are closed, hence valid, and their states $\mathrm{eval}(g\setminus\{e\})$
and $\mathrm{pre}(e)$ are reachable by induction; $\mathrm{post}(e)$ is a valid
transaction step from $\mathrm{pre}(e)$, and
$\mathrm{eval}(g)=\mathrm{eval}(g\setminus\{e\})\cup\mathrm{post}(e)$ is a
valid merge.

($\Rightarrow$) Let rows $(h_r,\mathrm{eval}(g_r))$, $r=1,\ldots,k$, have a
common \indesc $j$, so each $g_r$ is a closed sub-history of $j$. Let
$g=g_1\cup\cdots\cup g_k$. Every closed sub-history $c$ of $g$ is the
union of the pieces $c\cap g_r$, each a closed sub-history of the valid
$g_r$, so by the first step $\mathrm{eval}(c)$ is a union of reachable states. Invariant
confluence makes each successive union valid, and a valid merge of reachable
states is reachable, so $\mathcal I(\mathrm{eval}(c))$. Thus $g$ is valid, and
$\mathrm{eval}(g)\in P_{\mathcal I}(j)$ \outrel every $\mathrm{eval}(g_r)$. For $k=0$, take $g$ empty.

($\Leftarrow$) Only the property of having directed fibers is needed. Let $g_1$ and $g_2$ be valid
histories, and let $j$ be their disjoint union, with events renamed apart and
no order between the two parts. Each event keeps its predecessors, and its
$\mathrm{pre}$ and $\mathrm{post}$ are states of closed sub-histories
of a valid $g_r$, so $j$ is locally valid and $\mathrm{eval}(g_1),\mathrm{eval}(g_2)\in P_{\mathcal I}(j)$. Because the spec has directed fibers, there is a valid sub-history $g$ of $j$ with
$\mathrm{eval}(g)\supseteq\mathrm{eval}(g_1)\cup\mathrm{eval}(g_2)=\mathrm{eval}(j)\supseteq\mathrm{eval}(g)$,
so $\mathcal I(\mathrm{eval}(g_1)\cup\mathrm{eval}(g_2))$. Every
closed $c\subseteq j$ is the disjoint union of $c\cap g_1$ and $c\cap
g_2$, both valid, so the same argument gives $\mathcal I(\mathrm{eval}(c))$ and
$j$ is valid. Valid histories
are therefore closed under disjoint union, and under adding an event above all
others when its transaction step is valid. By induction on derivations, every
reachable state is the state of a valid history, and the pairwise argument
gives $\mathcal I(s_1\cup s_2)$ for all reachable $s_1,s_2$.
\end{proof}

\section{Output events and \commitment bases}
\label{app:bases}

This appendix first gives the formal definition of the lifted output events of
Section~\ref{sec:lifted-output-events}. It then supplies the details behind
Section~\ref{sec:systems}: the canonical \commitments of
Sections~\ref{sec:functional-calm}--\ref{sec:complete-calm}
are \commitments (Definition~\ref{def:commitment}) and form a canonical basis,
and every run under any basis
has an observably equivalent canonical run that retains at least as many rows
after each event. Because \commitments are partial functions, a basis may
offer a \commitment only at the states where its own precondition holds.

\subsection{Lifted output events}
\label{app:lifted-output-events}

Fix a state $(i,R)$ and an output $o\in R(i)$. A row is \emph{ruled out by}
$o$ when its output does not \outrelbase $o$. The ruled-out rows fall into two
disjoint sets:
\begin{align}
  \Subsumed_o&=\{(k,x)\in R\setminus R[\mathrm{out}\OutputGe o]:\notag\\
    &\qquad\quad x\OutputOrd u\text{ for some }u\in R[\mathrm{out}\OutputGe o](k)\},
      \label{eq:output-subsumptions}\\
  \Excluded_o&=(R\setminus R[\mathrm{out}\OutputGe o])\setminus\Subsumed_o.
      \label{eq:commitment-removals}
\end{align}

\begin{definition}[Lifted output event, output subsumption]
\label{def:lifted-output-event}
Let $(i,R)$ be a state and $o\in R(i)$. The \emph{output event}
$\mathsf{output}(o)$ keeps exactly the rows whose outputs \outrelbase $o$ and
emits $o$, atomically, ending at $(i,R[\mathrm{out}\OutputGe o])$ as in
Equation~\ref{eq:output-action}. It removes each row $(k,x)$ in
$\Subsumed_o$, for which some $u\in R(k)$ \outrel both $x$ and $o$, by
\emph{output subsumption}, which is not a \commitment.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Canonical \commitment]
\label{def:canonical-commitment}
When $\Excluded_o\neq\varnothing$, the output event applies the \emph{canonical
\commitment} $x_o$:
\begin{equation}
  x_o(i,R)=\bigl(i,\ R\setminus\Excluded_o\bigr).
  \label{eq:canonical-commitment-relational}
\end{equation}
Thus $x_o$ removes exactly the rows in $\Excluded_o$. At an input $k$, if
  $\Excluded_o(k)=R(k)$, it rules out $k$; otherwise it rules out only the
  outputs in $\Excluded_o(k)$. A row in $\Subsumed_o(k)$ has a kept \outnoun
  at $k$. The output event removes that row separately by output subsumption,
  and its kept \outnoun prevents the event from ruling out $k$.
\end{definition}

In Figure~\ref{fig:ambiguity-frames}(f), emitting $[A,B]$ places the row for
$[B,A]$ in $\Excluded_o$, so the event rules out that output at $\{A,B\}$. In panel~(g), the two removed route sets are in $\Subsumed_o$, each
\outrelpast by a retained route set, so the event subsumes them without
\commitment. With one row per input, $\Subsumed_o$ is empty, and $x_o$ rules
out each input whose row is removed; this is the functional output event of
Definition~\ref{def:output-event}. Apart from output subsumption and
\commitments, a row leaves the remainder table only by input loss
(\S\ref{sec:functional-events}).

Legality lifts with the event: $o$ is a legal output when $\mathsf{output}(o)$
applies no \commitment, exactly when $\Excluded_o$ is empty, though the event may
still subsume the rows in $\Subsumed_o$. The legal-output test lifts as well:

\begin{proposition}[Legal-output test]
\label{prop:exposability}
An output $o\in R(i)$ is legal at $(i,R)$ if and only if, for every legal
$j\InputGe i$ and $x\in R(j)$, some $u\in R(j)$ \outrel both:
\begin{equation}
  o\OutputOrd u
  \qquad\text{and}\qquad
  x\OutputOrd u.
  \label{eq:safe-exposure}
\end{equation}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
The event $\mathsf{output}(o)$ applies no \commitment exactly when every
ruled-out row $(j,x)$ lies in $\Subsumed_o$, that is, is \outrelpast by some row
$(j,u)$ \outrelpart $o$. Rows that already \outrelbase $o$ satisfy the stated
test with $u=x$, so this is exactly the stated test.
\end{proof}

This test marks the \commitment boundary: if it fails, emitting $o$ requires an
\commitment.

\subsection{\Commitment bases}
\label{app:commitment-bases}

\begin{definition}[Canonical basis]
\label{def:canonical-commitment-basis}
The \emph{canonical \commitment basis} collects the canonical \commitments of
Definition~\ref{def:canonical-commitment},
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal B_P
    =\{x_o:o\in O,\ \operatorname{dom}(x_o)\neq\varnothing\},
  \label{eq:canonical-commitment-basis-relational}
\end{equation}
where $x_o$ is defined at the states with $o\in R(i)$ and $\Excluded_o\neq\varnothing$.
\end{definition}

\paragraph{The canonical \commitment is a \commitment.}
Definition~\ref{def:commitment} asks that $x_o$ keep the current input, which
it does, remove a nonempty set of rows, which $\Excluded_o\neq\varnothing$
ensures, and remove no row that a kept row at the same input \outrel
(Equation~\ref{eq:commitment-unsubsumed}). Only the last needs an argument. Suppose a removed row $(k,x)\in\Excluded_o$ were \outrelpast by a row $(k,y)$
that $x_o$ leaves. Either $y$ \outrel $o$, or $y\in\Subsumed_o$ and is
\outrelpast by a row \outrelpart $o$. By transitivity, $x$ is then
\outrelpast by a row \outrelpart $o$, so $x\in\Subsumed_o$, a contradiction.

\begin{definition}[Run under a basis]
\label{def:run-under-basis}
Fix a \commitment basis $\mathcal B$. A \emph{run under $\mathcal B$} is a
finite transition sequence from a canonical state $\sigma_P(i)$ of input
events, output events (Definition~\ref{def:system-output-event}), and
\commitment events (Definition~\ref{def:commitment-event}) that use only
\commitments from $\mathcal B$.
\end{definition}

Canonical runs (Definition~\ref{def:run}) are exactly the runs under the
canonical basis $\mathcal B_P$ of Definition~\ref{def:canonical-commitment-basis}
with no \commitment events.

Two runs are \emph{observably equivalent} when they start at the same canonical
state and agree once \commitment events are dropped. They then accept the same
inputs and emit the same outputs, in the same order, which is all the
environment observes, although their remainder tables may differ.

\begin{proof}[Proof of Lemma~\ref{lem:canonical-equivalent}]
We drop the \commitment events from a run under $\mathcal B$. By induction on
the events, the result is a canonical run whose table $\widehat R$ contains
the original's table $R$ after each event; both start at the same canonical
state. A \commitment event keeps the current input and only removes rows, so
dropping it preserves $R\subseteq\widehat R$. An input event
$\mathsf{input}(j)$ legal at $R$ is legal at $\widehat R$, and keeps
$R[\mathrm{in}\InputGe j]\subseteq\widehat R[\mathrm{in}\InputGe j]$. An
output event $\mathsf{output}(o)$ at $R$ can be issued at $\widehat R$, since
$o\in R(i)\subseteq\widehat R(i)$, with $x_o$ from the canonical basis if it
applies one, and keeps
$R[\mathrm{out}\OutputGe o]\subseteq\widehat R[\mathrm{out}\OutputGe o]$.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[Reconciliation under any basis]
\label{cor:reconciliation-any-basis}
Let runs under any \commitment bases end at $(i_r,R_r)$, and let $j$ be a
common \indesc of the $i_r$. If some continuations, whose output and \commitment events
may apply any \commitments, bring them to a common state with current input $j$,
then some $u\in\fiber{j}$ \outrel every output emitted on every run.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
The common state has a row $(j,u)$, and events never add rows, so
$(j,u)\in R_r$. By Lemma~\ref{lem:canonical-equivalent}, $R_r$ is contained in
the table of a canonical run with the same inputs and outputs, and
Lemma~\ref{lem:run-residual} makes $u$ \outrel the outputs of run $r$.
\end{proof}

The converse of Lemma~\ref{lem:canonical-equivalent} fails: a canonical run
need not be observably equivalent to any run under a particular system's
basis. That basis may lack the \commitment that an output event requires,
which disables that output. Such a system still chooses only behaviors that
$P$ permits, and the spec still permits the disabled canonical run. Looking
only at the runs that one system produces could therefore hide
non-monotonicity, and hide a canonical run that conflicts with a \commitment. We instead judge consistency, and the effect of \commitments, against the
canonical runs of the declared spec. A behavioral restriction narrows that
comparison only when it is part of the declared contract.

\paragraph{Application-specific \commitments.}
Locks and timestamps are examples: two-phase locking applies a \commitment
that fixes an order among some transactions, and timestamp assignment places
one transaction relative to all others. Section~\ref{sec:systems} illustrates both, and
Appendix~\ref{app:cc-bases} writes the two bases down.

\section{Proofs and Details for Composition}
\label{app:composition}

This appendix proves the results of Section~\ref{sec:composition}, defines
composition with independent inputs and its curried evaluation, and works out
the replicated store with bare reads.

\subsection{Sequential composition}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Lemma~\ref{lem:composition}]
We verify the common-extension condition
(Proposition~\ref{prop:common-expansion-form}). Let rows $(i_r,o_r)$ of
$P_2\circ P_1$, finitely many, have common \indesc $j$, and choose $m_r$ with
$(i_r,m_r)\in P_1$ and $(m_r,o_r)\in P_2$. \textcolor{reviewed}{The condition for $\Spec_1$ gives
$m\in P_1(j)$ \outrelpart every $m_r$.} Then $m$ is a common \indesc of the
inputs of the rows $(m_r,o_r)$, and the condition for $\Spec_2$ gives
$u\in P_2(m)$ \outrelpart every $o_r$. So $(j,u)\in P_2\circ P_1$ is the
required common \outnoun.
\end{proof}

Tables of functions are closed under $\circ$, so Lemma~\ref{lem:composition}
specializes to functions.

\begin{corollary}[Functional composition]
\label{cor:functional-composition}
If $f:(I,\InputOrd)\to(M,\preceq_M)$ and
$g:(M,\preceq_M)\to(O,\OutputOrd)$ are monotone, so is $g\circ f$.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
The tables of $f$ and $g$ satisfy Lifted Monotonicity and compose to the
table of $g\circ f$, so Lemma~\ref{lem:composition} applies.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Composition with independent inputs}
\label{sec:parameterized-composition}

Sequential composition cannot express a downstream component with input of
its own, such as a replica that serves bare reads
(\S\ref{sec:composition}), so we give each component a separate
input. Let
\begin{align*}
  \Spec_F&=\bigl(I\times A,\preceq_I\times\preceq_A,B,\preceq_B,F\bigr),\\
  \Spec_G&=\bigl(J\times B,\preceq_J\times\preceq_B,C,\preceq_C,G\bigr)
\end{align*}
be \spectype[s] in which the output domain $(B,\preceq_B)$ of $\Spec_F$ is
part of the input domain of $\Spec_G$; it carries values from $F$ to $G$. Here $i\in I$ is input
that only $F$ receives, and $j\in J$ is input that only $G$ receives. We
split $F$'s input as $(i,a)$ so that $F$ restricted to a fixed $i$ is a table
from $A$ to $B$. The curried evaluation below uses this split to evaluate
the composition one pair $(i,j)$ at a time. The \emph{independently
parameterized composition} of $\Spec_F$ and $\Spec_G$ is
\begin{align}
  G\star F
  &=\pi_{I,J,A,C}\bigl(F\Join_{B}G\bigr) \notag\\
  &=\{((i,j,a),c):\exists b.\ ((i,a),b)\in F
                         \land ((j,b),c)\in G\},
  \label{eq:parameterized-composition}
\end{align}
with the product order on $I\times J\times A$. Since $F$ is total on
$I\times A$ and $G$ is total on $J\times B$, every composed input has a
nonempty fiber.

\begin{lemma}[Independently parameterized composition]
\label{lem:parameterized-composition}
If $\Spec_F$ and $\Spec_G$ satisfy Lifted Monotonicity, then the specification
with table $G\star F$ satisfies Lifted Monotonicity.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Let rows $((i_r,j_r,a_r),c_r)$, finitely many, have common \indesc $(i,j,a)$,
with witnesses $b_r$ from Equation~\ref{eq:parameterized-composition}. The
common-extension condition for $\Spec_F$ at $(i,a)$ gives $b\in F(i,a)$
\outrelpart every $b_r$. Then $(j,b)$ is a common \indesc of the
$(j_r,b_r)$, and the condition for $\Spec_G$ gives $c\in G(j,b)$ \outrelpart
every $c_r$. So $((i,j,a),c)\in G\star F$ is the required common \outnoun.
\end{proof}

\paragraph{Curried evaluation.}
For fixed parameters let
\begin{equation}
  F_i=\pi_{A,B}(F[I=i]),
  \qquad
  G_j=\pi_{B,C}(G[J=j]),
  \label{eq:curried-fibers}
\end{equation}
with the selection of Section~\ref{sec:functional-specifications}.

\begin{proposition}[Curried equijoin plan]
\label{prop:curried-query-plan}
The table in Equation~\ref{eq:parameterized-composition} equals
\begin{equation}
  \bigcup_{(i,j)\in I\times J}
  \left\{((i,j,a),c):
    (a,c)\in\pi_{A,C}\bigl(F_i\Join_B G_j\bigr)\right\}.
  \label{eq:curried-query-plan}
\end{equation}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
By Equation~\ref{eq:curried-fibers}, $((i,a),b)\in F$ and $((j,b),c)\in G$
iff $(a,b)\in F_i$ and $(b,c)\in G_j$. Quantifying over $b$ gives the same
condition on $((i,j,a),c)$ in both expressions.
\end{proof}

For monotone $f:I\times A\to B$ and $g:J\times B\to C$, the instance of
Lemma~\ref{lem:parameterized-composition} for their tables says that
\begin{equation}
  h(i,j,a)=g\bigl(j,f(i,a)\bigr)
  \label{eq:curried-composition}
\end{equation}
\textcolor{reviewed}{is monotone.} Write $f^\sharp(i)$ for the function $a\mapsto f(i,a)$, and
similarly $g^\sharp(j)$ and $h^\sharp(i,j)$. In this curried form
$h^\sharp(i,j)=g^\sharp(j)\circ f^\sharp(i)$, which is the plan of
Proposition~\ref{prop:curried-query-plan} on tables of functions.

\subsection{Jointly monotone composites}

Suppose every stage satisfies Lifted Monotonicity and $\Spec_1$ runs under
the empty basis. Then the pipeline emits only what $P_2\circ P_1$ emits under
the empty basis. The pipeline's runs are runs of a spec that satisfies Lifted
Monotonicity, and at their states every output is legal
(Proposition~\ref{prop:exposure-boundary}). So the next stage again has an
empty-basis guard, and by induction no stage of the pipeline issues a
\commitment.

\begin{proof}[Proof of Proposition~\ref{prop:joint-monotone}]
(1) is Lemma~\ref{lem:composition}, and also the case $D=M$ of (2).

(2) Let $D$ contain the outputs of $\Spec_1$. Take rows $(i_r,o_r)$ of $P_2\circ
P_1$ with a common \indesc $j$, and intermediate values $m_r$ with
$(i_r,m_r)\in P_1$ and $(m_r,o_r)\in P_2$. \textcolor{reviewed}{The common-extension condition for
$\Spec_1$ gives $m\in P_1(j)$ that \outrel every $m_r$.} All of $m_r$ and $m$
lie in $D$, so the condition for $\Spec_2$ on $D$ gives $o\in P_2(m)$ that
\outrel every $o_r$, and $(j,o)\in P_2\circ P_1$. No closure property of $D$
is used.

(3) The table $P_2\circ P_1$ of Equation~\ref{eq:spec-composition} mentions
no order on $M$, and Lifted Monotonicity of the composite uses only the orders
on $I$ and $O$. So the composite under $\preceq_M$ is the composite under the
other order, which satisfies Lifted Monotonicity by (1). The same holds after
re-encoding the intermediate values. A re-encoding is a map $e:M\to M'$
together with a decoding $e':M'\to M$ that recovers every value,
$e'(e(m))=m$. Replace $P_1$ by $\{(i,e(m)):(i,m)\in P_1\}$ and $P_2$ by
$\{(m',o):(e'(m'),o)\in P_2\}$. A join through $e(m)$ is a join through
$e'(e(m))=m$, so the composite table is unchanged, whatever the orders on $M$
and $M'$.
\end{proof}

\paragraph{The reach of case (3).}
Let $f:I\to M$ and $g:M\to O$ be
functions with $g(f(\cdot))$ monotone. One candidate order on $M$
places $a$ below $b$ when $g(a)\OutputOrd g(b)$. This relation is reflexive
and transitive. It makes $g$ monotone by construction, and $f$ monotone
because $g(f(\cdot))$ is. It can place two distinct values each below the
other, so it is an order only when no such pair exists, in particular when $g$
maps distinct values to distinct values. Then case (3) applies. In general case (3) need not apply. Let $I$ be the chain $x\InputOrd
y\InputOrd z$, $M=\{p,q\}$, $f(x)=f(z)=p$, $f(y)=q$, and $g$ constant. The
composite is constant, hence monotone. Under any order on $M$, monotonicity
of $f$ gives $p\preceq_M q\preceq_M p$, so $p=q$, a contradiction. The
upstream therefore cannot emit under the empty basis at one of the canonical
states of $x$ and $y$: by the legal-output test for tables of functions,
$f(i)$ is a legal output at $\sigma(i)$ exactly when $f(i)\preceq_M f(k)$ for
every $k\InputGe i$. The stages can be repaired only by merging $p$ and $q$
into one intermediate value.

For the capped counter of Section~\ref{sec:composite-calm}, let $\mathcal S_1$
be the functional spec induced by $c(n)=\min(n,9)$ on
$(\mathbb N,\le)$, and $\mathcal S_2$ that induced by the test $t(n)=[10\le n]$,
whose two outputs are ordered by equality. $\mathcal S_2$
violates Lifted Monotonicity, since $[10\le 9]$ and $[10\le 10]$ differ, but it
satisfies it on $\{n\le 9\}$, so the composite satisfies Lifted Monotonicity by case (2). At
$\sigma_{\mathcal S_2}(m)$ with $m\le 9$, emitting $[10\le m]$ must remove the row
$(10,\mathit{true})$ by \commitment, so $\mathcal S_2$ cannot emit under the empty basis. The outputs of $\mathcal S_1$ lie in $\{n\le 9\}$, so the pipeline is proper
(Theorem~\ref{thm:proper-coordination}(1)). Under the empty basis,
$\mathcal S_2|_{\mathcal S_1}$ answers every count that $\mathcal S_1$ emits.

For the boolean toggle, let $I$, $M$, and $O$ be the booleans with
$\mathit{false}$ below $\mathit{true}$, and let both stages be negation. The
table of a function $f$ has $f(i)$ a legal output at $\sigma(i)$ exactly when
$f(i)\preceq_M f(k)$ for all $k\InputGe i$. At $i=\mathit{false}$ the first
stage would emit $\mathit{true}$, which is not below its output
$\mathit{false}$ at the larger input, so under the empty basis it cannot emit
there. Under the reversed order on $M$, negation is monotone in both stages,
and by case (3) the composite is the identity in either order.

Let $\mathcal S_1$ have one input $*$ and possibility relation
$\{(*,x),(*,y)\}$ with $x,y$ incomparable, and let $\mathcal S_2$ be the
functional spec induced by a function $f$ that maps
both to one value. The composite has one row, so it satisfies Lifted
Monotonicity. At the canonical state of $\mathcal S_1$ neither $x$ nor $y$ is
a legal output. Under the empty basis an output event emits only a legal
output (Definition~\ref{def:system-output-event}), and no \commitment event is
possible, since such an event applies a nonempty sequence of \commitments from
the basis. Input events only remove rows at other inputs, and there are none. So every run of $\mathcal S_1$ under the empty basis stays at the canonical
state and emits nothing. Then $\mathcal S_2$ receives nothing. Renaming $x,y$ to
$\mathit{lo}\preceq\mathit{hi}$ makes $\mathcal S_1$ satisfy Lifted Monotonicity, since
$\mathit{hi}$ subsumes $\mathit{lo}$, and by case (3) the composite is
unchanged.

\subsection{Proper Coordination}

Throughout, $\Spec_1$ guards $\Spec_2$, and $\mathcal B$ is the basis of the
system for $\Spec_1$. The inputs of $\Spec_2|_{\Spec_1}$ are the outputs of
$\Spec_1$, ordered by $\preceq_M$, and its rows are the rows of $P_2$ at those
inputs. For tables $R$ of $\Spec_1$ and $T$ of $\Spec_2$ we write $R\Join
T=\{(k,x):\exists m.\ (k,m)\in R\land(m,x)\in T\}$. The \emph{image} of a
state $\sigma=(i,R)$ of $\Spec_1$ is the state $(i,R\Join P_2)$ of
$P_2\circ P_1$. The image of $\sigma_{\Spec_1}(i)$ is
$\sigma_{P_2\circ P_1}(i)$, since $P_1[\mathrm{in}\InputGe i]\Join P_2$ is the
composite table restricted to the \indescs of $i$.

A run of the pipeline is a run of $\Spec_1$ under $\mathcal B$, together with
a run of $\Spec_2|_{\Spec_1}$ from $\sigma(m_1)$, where $m_1$ is the first
output of $\Spec_1$. Each later output of $\Spec_1$ is an input event of
$\Spec_2|_{\Spec_1}$. Between its inputs, $\Spec_2|_{\Spec_1}$ emits any
number of outputs, possibly none. The environment observes $\mathsf{input}(j)$
and the outputs of $\Spec_2|_{\Spec_1}$.

The proofs below rest on three facts about a proper pipeline. The first two
concern a state $\tau$ that $\Spec_2|_{\Spec_1}$ reaches without \commitment. First, every input $j\succeq_M\tau.\mathrm{cur}$ has a row in $\tau$: the
common-extension condition gives $u\in P_2(j)$ that \outrel an output $v$ at
the current input, and $(j,u)$ survives, since every output emitted so far is
\outrelpast by $v$. Second, every output at the current input of $\tau$ is a
legal output. The third fact concerns $\Spec_1$: if an output $m$ at the
current input of a state $\sigma$ of $\Spec_1$ is a legal output, then every
$o\in P_2(m)$ is a legal output at the image of $\sigma$. To see this, let
$(k,x)$ be a row of the image not \outrelpast by $o$, coming from
$(k,m')\in R_\sigma$ and $(m',x)\in P_2$. If $m\preceq_M m'$, the
common-extension condition on the outputs of $\Spec_1$, for the rows $(m,o)$ and
$(m',x)$ at $m'$, gives $u\in P_2(m')$ with $o,x\OutputOrd u$. Otherwise
legality gives $(k,w)\in R_\sigma$ with $m,m'\preceq_M w$, and the
condition at $w$ gives $u\in P_2(w)$. Either way $(k,u)$ is a row of the
image that subsumes $(k,x)$.

\begin{proof}[Proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:proper-coordination}]
(1) By Theorem~\ref{thm:complete-calm} and
Proposition~\ref{prop:exposure-boundary}, $\Spec_2|_{\Spec_1}$ needs no
\commitment exactly when it satisfies Lifted Monotonicity. Its rows have
inputs among the outputs of $\Spec_1$, and so do its \indescs. So this is
Lifted Monotonicity of $\Spec_2$ on the outputs of $\Spec_1$.

(2) After $\mathsf{output}(m)$ every row of $\Spec_1$ \outrel $m$
(Definition~\ref{def:lifted-output-event}), and later events only remove rows, so
the outputs
$m_1\preceq_M m_2\preceq_M\cdots$ of a run of $\Spec_1$ form a chain. $\Spec_2|_{\Spec_1}$ starts at $\sigma(m_1)$, which it reaches without
\commitment. By the first two facts it can receive each $m_{r+1}$ and answer
it by a legal output, so it follows the run under the empty basis.

Consider any run of the pipeline in which $\Spec_2|_{\Spec_1}$ runs under the
empty basis, and replace each output $m_r$ of $\Spec_1$ by the answers to it. Let $\sigma$ be the state of $\Spec_1$, $T$ the table of
$\Spec_2|_{\Spec_1}$, and $\rho$ the composite's state. Before the first
output of $\Spec_1$ we maintain $\rho.\mathrm{cur}=\sigma.\mathrm{cur}$ and
$R_\sigma\Join P_2\subseteq R_\rho$. Afterwards we maintain $R_\sigma\Join
T\subseteq R_\rho$, and every row $(k,m)$ of $R_\sigma$ has $m$ above the
current input of $\Spec_2|_{\Spec_1}$. Both hold at the start, where the
image of $\sigma_{\Spec_1}(i)$ is $\sigma_{P_2\circ P_1}(i)$. For
$\mathsf{input}(j)$ of $\Spec_1$, a row $(j,m)$ of $R_\sigma$ and, by the first
fact, a row $(m,x)$ of $T$ give $(j,x)\in R_\rho$, so the input is legal, and
both events keep the rows at \indescs of $j$. A \commitment event of
$\Spec_1$ keeps the current input and only removes rows of $R_\sigma$, so both
invariants survive it. For $\mathsf{output}(m)$ at input $i$, output
containment gives $(i,m)\in R_\sigma$ and the resulting table
$R_\sigma[\mathrm{out}\succeq_M m]$. $\Spec_2|_{\Spec_1}$ receives $m$
and keeps $T[\mathrm{in}\succeq_M m]$. Each answer $o$ has $(m,o)\in T$, so
$(i,o)\in R_\rho$. The composite's $\mathsf{output}(o)$ keeps
$R_\rho[\mathrm{out}\OutputGe o]$, and $\Spec_2|_{\Spec_1}$ keeps
$T[\mathrm{out}\OutputGe o]$, so the join stays inside the composite's table.

For confluence, let the runs of $\Spec_1$ end at
$\sigma_1,\ldots,\sigma_n$, the runs of $\Spec_2|_{\Spec_1}$ at
$\tau_1,\ldots,\tau_n$, and the composite runs at $\rho_1,\ldots,\rho_n$, and
let each $\sigma_r$ continue under some basis to a common state with a row
$(j,m)$. Events never add rows, so $(j,m)\in R_{\sigma_r}$, and $m$ is above
the current input of each $\tau_r$. Let $v_r$ be an output at the current
input of $\tau_r$. The common-extension condition gives $u\in P_2(m)$ that
\outrel every $v_r$, and $(m,u)$ survives in every $\tau_r$. So $(j,u)\in
R_{\rho_r}$ for every $r$, and the composite runs are confluent at $j$
(Proposition~\ref{prop:confluence}).

(3) The proof of Proposition~\ref{prop:exposure-boundary} derives the
common-extension condition from legality at canonical states alone. If
Lifted Monotonicity fails for $P_2\circ P_1$, some $o$ with $(i,m)\in P_1$ and
$o\in P_2(m)$ is therefore not a legal output at $\sigma_{P_2\circ P_1}(i)$, the
image of $\sigma_{\Spec_1}(i)$. By the third fact, no $m'\in P_1(i)$ with
$o\in P_2(m')$ is a legal output at $\sigma_{\Spec_1}(i)$.
\end{proof}

Properness is needed in (3). With $\Spec_1$ the identity on the booleans and
$\Spec_2$ negation, the composite is negation, which does not satisfy Lifted
Monotonicity, yet $\Spec_1$ needs no \commitment.

The \emph{\commitment-erased replacement} of a system has the empty basis and
the same strategy, with its \commitment events dropped.

\begin{corollary}[Empty store]
\label{cor:empty-store}
In a proper pipeline, fix any system for $\Spec_2|_{\Spec_1}$. For every run
of the pipeline, the replacement has one with the same run of $\Spec_1$ and
the same inputs and outputs of $\Spec_2|_{\Spec_1}$. After each event, the
replacement's table contains the original's.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
The replacement's run drops the original's \commitment events. As in the
proof of Lemma~\ref{lem:canonical-equivalent}, it then has the same inputs and
outputs, and after each event its table contains the original's. Each output
the original emits is a legal output for the replacement by the second fact,
so the empty basis suffices, and the replacement's strategy allows each event,
since the original's did.
\end{proof}

The converse fails: the early lock below loses a run that its replacement has.

\begin{corollary}[Pipelines]
\label{cor:pipelines}
In a proper pipeline, with any systems for $\Spec_1$ and
$\Spec_2|_{\Spec_1}$, the outputs of every run of the pipeline are emitted by
a run of $P_2\circ P_1$ under its canonical basis. So the pipeline guards a
further stage $\Spec_3$, and Theorem~\ref{thm:proper-coordination} applies
with $P_2\circ P_1$ in place of $\Spec_1$.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
By Corollary~\ref{cor:empty-store}, every run of the pipeline has one with the
same outputs in which $\Spec_2|_{\Spec_1}$ runs under the empty basis. Theorem~\ref{thm:proper-coordination}(2), applied to it and a prefix of the
run of $\Spec_1$, gives a run of $P_2\circ P_1$ with these outputs, and the
canonical basis supplies the canonical \commitment of each output event that
needs one (Definition~\ref{def:system-output-event}), so it is a run under the
canonical basis. By induction the same holds along a pipeline
$\Spec_n\circ\cdots\circ\Spec_1$ in which each prefix properly guards the next
stage. Composition is associative, so the bracketing does not matter.
\end{proof}

Consider locks in the store behind the log. Let $\Spec_1$ be the log
spec of Example~\ref{ex:committed-log-composition}, under its
canonical basis with a strategy that allows every event, and $\Spec_2$ the
table of any monotone function $f$ of the log. Every log is an output of
$\Spec_1$, so $\Spec_2|_{\Spec_1}$ is $\Spec_2$. For a set $Q$ of logs,
removing every row whose input is in $Q$ is a \commitment of $\Spec_2$ at any
state whose current input is not in $Q$ and that has such a row. Such a
\commitment removes whole fibers, so it empties them. Consider two writes $a$
and $b$ to one key, and a lock granted to $a$, which empties the fibers of the
logs that start with $b$. If the store takes the lock only once its current
log is nonempty, every log it can still receive has the same first command, so
the lock removes no row and is not a \commitment. If instead the store takes
the lock at the empty log, ahead of the log, the lock empties logs that
$\Spec_1$ can still offer, although the store's output $f([\,])$ was a legal
output without the lock. At
$\sigma_{\Spec_1}([b])$, $\Spec_1$ can emit $[\,]$ and then $[b]$. The
replacement emits $f([\,])$, receives $[b]$, and emits $f([b])$. The locking
store must take the lock at $[\,]$, which empties $[b]$, so it cannot receive
$[b]$.

For a function store, some \commitments of $\Spec_1$ are invisible to the
composite. Let $\Spec_2$ be the functional spec induced by a
monotone function $f$, and write
$f(\sigma)$ for the image of a state $\sigma$ of $\Spec_1$.

\begin{proposition}[Invisible coordination]
\label{prop:invisible-coordination}
Let $f$ be monotone, and let $\Spec_1$ apply $\gamma$ at $\sigma$ in a
\commitment event and then emit $m$ by $\mathsf{output}(m)$, a legal output
after $\gamma$. If every row that $\gamma$ removes has a row with the same
input and the same image that $\gamma$ keeps, then $f(m)$ is a legal output at
$f(\sigma)$.
\end{proposition}

The proof shows that the \commitments of $\Spec_1$ cover those of the
composite. For a table $R$ of $\Spec_1$ write $f(R)=\{(k,f(m)):(k,m)\in R\}$. Let
$\gamma$ take $\sigma$ to $\sigma_c$, at which $m$ is a legal output, and let
$\rho$ be a composite state with $f(R_\sigma)\subseteq R_\rho$. We show that no row
$(k,f(m'))$ with $(k,m')\in R_{\sigma_c}$ is in $\Excluded_{f(m)}$ at
$\rho$. If $m\preceq_M m'$ then $f(m)\OutputOrd f(m')$. Otherwise legality gives $(k,u)\in R_{\sigma_c}$ with $m\preceq_M u$ and
$m'\preceq_M u$, and $(k,f(u))\in R_\rho$ subsumes $(k,f(m'))$. So every row
that the composite's $\mathsf{output}(f(m))$ removes by \commitment has had
every preimage in $R_\sigma$ removed by $\gamma$.

\begin{proof}[Proof of Proposition~\ref{prop:invisible-coordination}]
The hypothesis gives $f(R_{\sigma_c})=f(R_\sigma)$. By the covering
argument with $\rho=f(\sigma)$, no row of $f(\sigma)$ is in
$\Excluded_{f(m)}$, so $f(m)$ is a legal output at $f(\sigma)$.
\end{proof}

Consider a store that answers each key from that key's commands, in log
order. Let $\Spec_1$ have one input with fiber $\{[a,b],[b,a]\}$, for
commands $a$ and $b$ on different keys, and let $f$ send a log to the family
of its subsequences on each key. The two logs are incomparable with no common
\outnoun, so $[a,b]$ is not a legal output, and $\Spec_1$ must order them. But
$f([a,b])=f([b,a])$, the composite has one row, and $f([a,b])$ is a legal
output. The order of $a$ and $b$ is a \commitment inside $\Spec_1$ that conflicts
with a canonical run, and the composite does not see it.

The image under $f$ of a \commitment of $\Spec_1$ need not be a \commitment
of the composite. Let $\Spec_1$
have one input with fiber $\{x,y\}$, $x$ and $y$ incomparable, and
$f(x)=\mathit{lo}\OutputOrd\mathit{hi}=f(y)$. The \commitment $e_y$ removes $x$
and keeps $y$. Its image would remove $\mathit{lo}$ and keep $\mathit{hi}$,
violating Equation~\ref{eq:commitment-unsubsumed}, so no \commitment of the
composite has that effect. In the composite, $\mathit{hi}$ is a legal output
at the canonical state, since it subsumes $\mathit{lo}$.

Monotonicity of $f$ is needed. Let $\Spec_1$ be the functional spec
induced by the identity on
$M$, which needs no \commitment. For $m\preceq_M m'$, its run
$\mathsf{output}(m)$, $\mathsf{input}(m')$, $\mathsf{output}(m')$ maps to a run
of the composite exactly when $f(m)\OutputOrd f(m')$. Nor can a downstream stage that needs a \commitment of its own delegate it
upstream. If $\Spec_1$ is the identity on one input, no \commitment of
$\Spec_1$ exists. If $\Spec_2$ relates that input
to two incomparable outputs, every output event of the composite at its
canonical state applies a \commitment, which $\Spec_1$ cannot make.

\begin{example}[Replicated store]
\label{ex:committed-log-composition}
Consider again state-machine replication, with every put and get passing
through the log. Let $I$ be the proposal streams and $M$ the logs, both ordered by prefix,
and $O$ the cumulative response histories, ordered by extension. Let $\Spec_1$ relate each proposal stream to the logs of its commands, and let
the store $\Spec_2$ be the functional spec induced by the function
$f$ that executes a log on a
deterministic state machine, which is monotone in the prefix order. Then
$\Spec_1$ is non-monotone, since distinct next entries are incompatible
outputs in one fiber. So is the key-value store $P_2\circ P_1$, since
concurrent commands ordered oppositely give incompatible responses. Multi-Paxos runs $\Spec_1$ under its own basis, deciding one log slot at a
time~\cite{schneider1990smr,lamport2001paxos}. Since $f$ is monotone, the
pipeline is proper, and by Theorem~\ref{thm:proper-coordination}(2) the store
under the empty basis follows every run of the log. By
Theorem~\ref{thm:proper-coordination}(3), the log's \commitments cannot be
dropped. By Corollary~\ref{cor:empty-store}, replicas whose store takes locks
of its own can run it with no \commitments at all.
\end{example}

\begin{example}[Bare reads]
\label{ex:bare-reads}
We add bare reads to Example~\ref{ex:committed-log-composition}. Let $I$
and $B$ be the proposal streams and logs of that example, $A$ a one-point set,
and $J$ the histories of bare reads received by a replica, ordered by prefix. Let $C$ be the pairs of a response history for logged commands, ordered by
extension, and a set of bare-read responses, ordered by inclusion. Let $F$ be
the log spec $\Spec_1$ of that example, with the one-point $A$
added to its input. Let $G$ relate a bare-read history $j$ and a log $b$ to
the responses obtained by executing $b$, together with at least one response to
each bare read in $j$. Responses are facts, so a set of them may answer one
read more than once. As in Example~\ref{ex:committed-log-composition}, $F$
and $G\star F$ are non-monotone.

Suppose first that each bare-read response records the log position it read
from, as in ``$x=5$ as of entry 17.''  A longer log never contradicts such a
response, so $G$ is monotone. By Complete CALM and the no-\commitment test,
every output of $G$ at every state reachable without \commitment is
legal, so bare reads need no \commitment of their own. Suppose instead
that the spec demands the latest value, so that a read of $x$ must
return the value of $x$ after all of $b$. Then a bare read can be
contradicted by an entry it has not seen, and $G$ is not monotone. By the
no-\commitment test some output of $G$ then needs a \commitment of the store's
own. Implementations typically enforce it by routing reads through the log.
\end{example}

\subsection{Intersection}
\label{app:intersection}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Proposition~\ref{prop:intersection-bound}]
Since $g(j)\in P_1(j)\cap P_2(j)$ for every $j$, the table $P_1\cap P_2$ is
total. Let $i\InputOrd j$ and let $X\subseteq P_1(i)\cap P_2(i)$ be finite and
nonempty. Every $x\in X$ is in $P_1(i)$, so $x\OutputOrd g(i)$, and
$g(i)\OutputOrd g(j)$ since $g$ is monotone. So $g(j)\in P_1(j)\cap P_2(j)$
\outrel every member of $X$, which is Lifted Monotonicity.
\end{proof}

In the example of Section~\ref{sec:intersection}, both specs satisfy Lifted
Monotonicity: with a single input, Lifted Monotonicity is directedness of the
one fiber, and $c$ and $d$ bound their fibers. The intersection is total, since
$a$ is in both fibers, and its fiber $\{a,b\}$ is not directed. By
Proposition~\ref{prop:exposure-boundary}, some output of the intersection at a
state reachable without \commitment is not legal.

\subsection{Components and typed pipelines}
\label{app:components}

Theorem~\ref{thm:proper-coordination} fixes a system for each stage and
follows the pipeline event by event. Here each stage is seen from outside,
through a typed boundary, so that stages with different specs,
bases, and participants compose without one shared history.

\begin{definition}[Component, admitted run]
\label{def:component}
A \emph{component} for a specification $\Spec$ consists of a \commitment basis
$\mathcal B$ and a set of \emph{admitted runs}. Each admitted run is a run
under $\mathcal B$ from a canonical state $\sigma(i)$
(Definition~\ref{def:run-under-basis}), together with an output $o$ that it
emits, such that $(i,o)\in P$.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Implementation form]
\label{def:implementation-form}
A component may carry an \emph{implementation form}, $\mathsf{Process}$,
$\mathsf{Cluster}$, or $\mathsf{Composite}$, which records how it is
realized. The implementation form plays no role in typed composition; it
records which internal system model and coordination mechanisms justify the
component's admitted runs.
\end{definition}

A system for $\Spec$ (Definition~\ref{def:system}) induces a component once
we choose its boundary observations. An admitted boundary run is a
participant run of a produced history, together with an output $o$ that the
run emits such that $(i,o)$ is a row of $P$, where $i$ is the run's initial
input. A run that emits no such output induces no observation. The system's messages,
partitions, and \commitments stay inside the component. Restricted to
one-participant histories, the induced component is a process, and a
replicated system induces a cluster. The two-phase commit decision discussed
after Proposition~\ref{prop:commitment-conflict} can take either form. Run by the coordinator alone, it is a process, so no conflicting
choice is placed at another participant. Replicated, it is a cluster, and a
conflicting choice can be placed at another participant. In both cases the
timeout has the same semantic effect: it applies a \commitment that conflicts
with a canonical run. The \emph{maximally permissive} component under $\mathcal B$
admits every such run under $\mathcal B$.

Let $C_1$ be a component for $\Spec_1$ and $C_2$ a component for $\Spec_2$.

\begin{definition}[Feed, typed pipeline, external observation]
\label{def:typed-pipeline}
A \emph{feed} pairs an admitted run of $C_1$, with input $i$ and output $m$,
with an admitted run of $C_2$ from $\sigma(m)$, with output $o$. The
\emph{typed pipeline} of $C_1$ and $C_2$ shows the \emph{external
observation} $(i,o)$ of each feed; the value $m$, both runs, and their
\commitments stay hidden.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Contract-proper, implementation-proper]
\label{def:proper-pipeline}
A typed pipeline is \emph{contract-proper} when the pipeline of $\Spec_1$
and $\Spec_2$ is proper (Definition~\ref{def:proper-coordination}), and
\emph{implementation-proper} when no downstream run of a feed applies a
\commitment.
\end{definition}

The value $m$ is the downstream run's initial input, so receiving it is not
a \commitment of $C_2$. Glue stages that copy, merge, route, or adapt values
(\S\ref{app:dataflow-graphs}) are components with specs of
their own, and they may issue \commitments of their own.

\begin{proposition}[Typed pipelines]
\label{prop:typed-pipelines}
\leavevmode
\begin{enumerate}
\item
Every external observation admitted by a typed pipeline is a row of
      $P_2\circ P_1$.
\item
If a pipeline is implementation-proper, the downstream run of every
      feed, with its \commitments erased, is a \commitment-free run of
      $\Spec_2$, in which every output is legal.
\item
Contract properness does not imply implementation properness: behind a
      log, the early-lock store is a contract-proper downstream
      implementation that still issues \commitments.
\item
Contract properness does not imply feed availability for an
      arbitrarily typed downstream component: behind the capped counter, the
      maximally permissive empty-basis component for the unrestricted
      threshold specification admits no feed at an offered count.
\item
If, in a contract-proper pipeline, the downstream is instead the
      maximally permissive empty-basis component for $\Spec_2|_{\Spec_1}$,
      every admitted upstream boundary run has a feed, and the pipeline is
      implementation-proper.
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
(1) The two runs of a feed certify $(i,m)\in P_1$ and $(m,o)\in P_2$.

(2) A run that applies no \commitment has no \commitment event, and each of its
output events $\mathsf{output}(o)$ applies no \commitment, which holds exactly
when $o$ is a legal output.

(3) The early-lock store above, fed by a log, is contract-proper, since the
store's table satisfies Lifted Monotonicity on logs. From the empty log it
admits a run whose \commitment event takes the lock.

(4) Consider the capped counter of Section~\ref{sec:composite-calm} feeding
the double-digit threshold test. The pipeline is
contract-proper, since the test satisfies Lifted Monotonicity on the counts
the counter offers. An admitted run of the test from an offered count
$m\le 9$ must emit $\mathit{false}$, its answer at $m$. While its current count is below
$10$, its table keeps the row $(10,\mathit{true})$, so $\mathit{false}$ is not a legal output;
once the count reaches $10$, only $\mathit{true}$ remains. So no feed exists.

(5) Let $m$ be the output of an admitted run of $C_1$. By
Theorem~\ref{thm:proper-coordination}(1), $\Spec_2|_{\Spec_1}$ satisfies
Lifted Monotonicity, so every $o\in P_2(m)$ is a legal output at $\sigma(m)$
(Proposition~\ref{prop:exposure-boundary}). The one-event run
$\mathsf{output}(o)$ from $\sigma(m)$ is then an admitted run of $C_2$. Under the empty basis no run applies a \commitment.
\end{proof}

Item (5) covers one boundary output. Theorem~\ref{thm:proper-coordination}(2) follows the entire emitted stream
and gives the composite-run invariant and confluence, and (3) of that
theorem supplies the necessity result. Item (4) is why (5) uses a component
for $\Spec_2|_{\Spec_1}$ rather than for $\Spec_2$: its canonical states keep
only rows at inputs the upstream offers.

Hydro's \texttt{Process} and \texttt{Cluster} location
types~\cite{hydro} provide the implementation analogy. A \texttt{Process}
location corresponds to a one-participant component, while a
\texttt{Cluster} location corresponds to a replicated component. Both
connect to the surrounding dataflow through typed edges. This is an
interface analogy; the results do not model Hydro's scheduler, placement, or
failure semantics. A typed pipeline is binary, and finite acyclic graphs
follow by associativity of composition and explicit glue components. We do
not treat cyclic dataflows, scheduling a graph of components, physical
placement, co-location, or failure domains, and we do not merge the
participants of different components into one history.

\subsection{Dataflow graphs}
\label{app:dataflow-graphs}

Sequential composition builds pipelines. Two more ingredients build
arbitrary acyclic graphs of stages. The \emph{product}
$\Spec_a\otimes\Spec_b$ runs two stages side by side on independent inputs. Its inputs are pairs $(i_a,i_b)$, ordered componentwise, and each of its rows
pairs a row of $\Spec_a$ with a row of $\Spec_b$. \emph{Glue} stages are
tables of monotone functions. A \emph{tee} $m\mapsto(m,m)$ copies one output
to two consumers, a \emph{merge} joins two inputs, for instance by set union,
a \emph{split} routes each element of a set by a fixed predicate, and a
projection discards a component. A graph is an expression built from
$\circ$, $\otimes$, and glue.

The product behaves componentwise. It satisfies Lifted Monotonicity exactly
when both factors do, an output is a legal output exactly when both components
are, and it needs no \commitment exactly when neither factor does. The
converse directions need nonempty input domains. A stage that merges the
outputs of two guards is proper exactly when it satisfies Lifted Monotonicity
on the product of their outputs, and a merge by join is always proper. Composition with independent inputs (\S\ref{sec:parameterized-composition})
is the product of $\Spec_1$ with the identity on $J$, followed by $\Spec_2$, so
Lemma~\ref{lem:parameterized-composition} follows from the product and
composition lemmas.

Monotonicity of a graph can be checked node by node, much as a type checker
proceeds. Each node is offered the outputs that everything upstream of it
can produce. The node passes when it satisfies Lifted Monotonicity on what it
is offered, and its outputs become what the next node is offered. If every
node passes, the graph satisfies Lifted Monotonicity. A node's test is
exactly properness of the pipeline from its upstream, so a graph in which
every node is properly guarded is consistent. The test is sufficient but not
necessary: a composite that is monotone only under another order on an
interface (Proposition~\ref{prop:joint-monotone}(3)) fails it.

What a node is offered depends on the shape of the graph at fan-in. Independent sources offer the product of their outputs, while a tee offers
only matching pairs. So teeing a stage's output differs from running the
stage twice. Both are monotone when the stage is, but two runs of a
nondeterministic stage may disagree. Consider a stage with one input and
outputs $\mathit{lo}\preceq\mathit{hi}$, followed by a test of whether its two
copies agree. The pipeline is proper after a tee, and not after two runs. Teeing and running twice coincide exactly when the stage is a function.

We leave feedback loops to future work. Networks of monotone functions have
least-fixpoint meanings~\cite{kahn1974semantics}, but for nondeterministic
stages, input--output relations do not compose under
feedback~\cite{brock1981scenarios}.


\section{Deployments: One Explicit Relation per Participant}
\label{app:deployment}

Theorem~\ref{thm:complete-cap} fixes one explicit relation $E$ for all
participants. A deployment fixes one for each participant: the rows that
participant may emit. It models a system that assigns authority, such as a
key owned by one participant or a replica that answers only its own reads. This appendix states Availability for a deployment and characterizes when the
choices authorized at different participants remain compatible across a
partition. Assigning rows to participants removes no row from the spec, and
\commitments are still compared with all of the spec's canonical runs.

\begin{definition}[Deployment, emitted rows, partition]
\label{def:deployment}
A \emph{deployment} over a finite participant set $\mathcal N$ assigns to each $p\in\mathcal N$
an explicit relation $E_p\subseteq P$, the rows $p$ may emit: $p$ performs an output event
$\mathsf{output}(o)$ (Definition~\ref{def:system-output-event}) at current input
$i$ only if $(i,o)\in E_p$. Emitted rows are as in Definition~\ref{def:emitted-rows}. A \emph{partition} $\Pi$ of $\mathcal N$ divides $\mathcal N$ into
two or more nonempty sides, as in a partitioned history
(Definition~\ref{def:partitioned-history}).
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Participant run family, silent participant, separable run family]
\label{def:participant-run-family}
A \emph{participant run family} assigns to each of finitely many participants
$p\in\mathcal N$ an initial input and a canonical run whose emitted rows lie in $E_p$. A participant whose run emits no output is \emph{silent}. The family is
\emph{separable under $\Pi$} when either participants on at least two
sides emit, or exactly one distinct row is emitted and some silent
participant lies on another side.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Monotone across partitions]
\label{def:monotone-across-partitions}
Relative to a deployment, $\Spec$ is \emph{monotone across $\Pi$} when, for
every participant run family separable under $\Pi$ and every common \indesc $j$
of its participants' current inputs, some $u\in\fiber{j}$ \outrel every
emitted output. It is \emph{monotone across partitions} when it is monotone
across every partition of $\mathcal N$.
\end{definition}

Monotonicity across partitions plays the role of Lifted Monotonicity for $E$
(Definition~\ref{def:lm-for-A}), restricted to the families that a partition
can separate. If $\Spec$ satisfies Lifted Monotonicity for a relation that
contains every $E_p$, it is monotone across partitions, since the emitted rows
of a family are rows of that relation at inputs below $j$. So Lifted
Monotonicity for $E$ suffices for the uniform deployment $E_p=E$, and Lifted
Monotonicity suffices for every deployment.

The converse can fail, because a deployment can keep conflicting rows at one
participant. Rows authorized only to $p$ can be emitted only by $p$'s run, and
the outputs that run emits form a chain, since each output event keeps only
rows whose outputs extend it. So a family whose emitted rows are all
authorized only to $p$ is separable only when it emits a single row. For
example, let $\mathcal N=\{p,q\}$, let every fiber be $\{a,b\}$ ordered by
equality, and let $E_p=P$ and $E_q=\varnothing$, so that $p$ alone names a
leader. A run of $p$ emits at most one distinct output, and $q$ never emits. Every separable family therefore consists of one row $(i,a)$ or $(i,b)$ beside
a silent $q$, and that output lies in $\fiber{j}$ for every \indesc $j$ of $i$. So $\Spec$ is monotone across partitions, yet $(i,a)$ and $(i,b)$ have no
common \outnoun, and Lifted Monotonicity fails. The same happens whenever two
conditions hold: each authorized row has an \outnoun at every \indesc of its
input, and every set of authorized rows that lacks a common \outnoun at some
common \indesc is authorized only to a single participant. The causal register
of Appendix~\ref{app:registers} is a further example of monotonicity across
partitions without Lifted Monotonicity, with no single authority. It need not
satisfy these conditions, since a set of rows lacking a common \outnoun may also
contain rows of other participants. The converse does hold when every
participant may emit every row. If $|\mathcal N|\ge2$ and $E_p=P$ for every
$p$, every pair of rows can be split across a partition, and every single row
can be paired with a silent participant at the common \indesc. Pairs of rows
suffice for the common-extension condition (Appendix~\ref{app:pairs-suffice}),
so monotonicity across partitions is then Lifted Monotonicity.

\begin{definition}[Deployment Availability]
\label{def:deployment-availability}
Relative to a deployment, a system has \emph{Deployment Availability} when, in
every partitioned history over $\mathcal N$ it produces, each participant $p$
can emit every output in $R(i)\cap E_p(i)$, where $(i,R)$ is $p$'s final state
(Definition~\ref{def:local-continuation}).
\end{definition}

This is Availability for $E_p$, one relation per participant. For the uniform
deployment $E_p=E$ it is Availability for $E$ on histories over $\mathcal N$, and for
$E_p=P$ it is Availability.

A deployment is \emph{single-authority} when $E_q=\varnothing$ for every
participant $q$ but one, as in the leader example above. Every separable family
is then one row emitted by the single authority beside a silent participant, and
monotonicity across partitions says only that each such row has an \outnoun at
every \indesc of its input. Placement-sensitive models such as
relational-transducer CALM~\cite{ameloot2013relational,ameloot2015weaker} also
cover this case. The deployment keeps the conflicting choice from being made
at another participant, but it does not change the semantic conflict: a
canonical run still conflicts with the authority's \commitment
(Proposition~\ref{prop:commitment-conflict}). For example, another canonical
run of the spec conflicts with a lock manager's choice even when only the
manager is authorized to emit the result. A deployment therefore says whether
conflicting choices can occur at different participants; it does not remove
the conflict or the \commitment. Comparing \commitments with fewer canonical
runs is appropriate only when the restriction is part of the declared
contract. A deployment also does not restrict which inputs a participant
receives: the environment may deliver any input to any participant.

\begin{proposition}[Partition compatibility under a deployment]
\label{thm:complete-cap-deployment}
Fix a deployment. The following are equivalent.
\begin{enumerate}
\item
For every partition $\Pi$, every participant run family separable
        under $\Pi$ is confluent at every common \indesc $j$ of its
        participants' current inputs.
\item
$\Spec$ is monotone across partitions.
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Fix $\Pi$, a separable family, and $j$, and let $X$ be the set of emitted
outputs. By Lemma~\ref{lem:reconciliation}, the family is confluent at $j$ if
and only if some $u\in\fiber{j}$ \outrel every output in $X$. The two
conditions thus agree family by family.
\end{proof}

The proposition concerns canonical runs and does not use Deployment
Availability. It also applies to the runs of a system: by
Lemma~\ref{lem:canonical-equivalent}, each such run is observably equivalent to
a canonical run with the same emitted rows, which therefore lie in $E_p$. So if
$\Spec$ is monotone across partitions, the outputs that a separable family of
system runs emits in a partitioned history, as Deployment Availability
permits, have a common \outnoun $u\in\fiber{j}$ at every common \indesc $j$. It does not follow that the deployment authorizes any participant to emit $u$. Confluence through authorized output events would also require that authority,
a condition we do not impose. With $|\mathcal N|\ge2$ and $E_p=P$ for every
$p$, item~(2) is Lifted Monotonicity. The proposition is then the step behind
Theorem~\ref{thm:complete-cap} with $E=P$: separable families are confluent at
every common \indesc exactly when Lifted Monotonicity holds.

\subsection{Registers}
\label{app:registers}

Section~\ref{sec:complete-cap} derives the Gilbert--Lynch theorem from a
replica that is cut off from a completed write and must still answer a read. The example here differs: two concurrent writes reach both replicas before the
partition.

\paragraph{The causal register.}
This register records causal precedence rather than real-time precedence, and
its outputs record the write that supplies each read rather than the value
returned; it is otherwise the register of Appendix~\ref{app:cap-response}. An
input is a finite operation history $h$: a set of reads and writes of one
register $x$ at replicas, with a strict partial order $\to$ of causal
precedence (program order at each replica, and from each write to the
operations a replica invokes after receiving it, closed transitively). A set
of operations of $h'$ is \emph{causally closed} when it contains every
operation that causally precedes one of its members. We set $h\InputOrd h'$
when the operations of $h$ form a causally closed subset of $h'$ and $\to$
restricted to them is the order of $h$. Thus an \indesc never adds an
operation to the causal past of an existing one.

An output is a \emph{source map} $\rho$: a partial map assigning to some reads
of $h$ a write of $h$ or the initial value $v_0$, ordered by extension. A
\emph{serialization} for replica $p$ is a total order of $p$'s operations and
all writes of $h$ that extends $\to$; it explains a read of $p$ with source
$w$ when $w$ is the last write before the read, or $v_0$ if there is none. Under causal consistency~\cite{lamport1978time,ahamad1995causal},
$\rho\in\fiber{h}$ when each replica has a serialization explaining every read
of that replica in the domain of $\rho$. Under linearizability, a single
total order of all operations of $h$ extending $\to$ must explain every read
in the domain~\cite{herlihy1990linearizability}; real-time edges beyond $\to$
play no role in the example below.

Two replicas start empty. Participant $p$ completes $w_p(x,\dog)$ while $q$
concurrently completes $w_q(x,\catv)$; the replicas exchange both writes, a
partition separates them, and each serves a local read, $r_p$ at $p$ and $r_q$
at $q$. Both writes causally precede both reads, and the writes are
concurrent.

Under linearizability, a source map that sends $r_p$ to $w_q(x,\catv)$ needs a
linearization with $w_p(x,\dog)$ before $w_q(x,\catv)$, and one that sends
$r_q$ to $w_p(x,\dog)$ needs the opposite order. No source map \outrel both, so the register is not monotone across the
partition $\{\{p\},\{q\}\}$ under any deployment authorizing these two reads.

Under causal consistency the two replicas may serialize the concurrent writes
differently, so one source map sends $r_p$ to $w_q(x,\catv)$ and $r_q$ to
$w_p(x,\dog)$. More generally, consider the deployment in which each replica
answers only its own reads ($E_p$ holds the rows whose source map is defined
only on reads of $p$). Under it the causal register is monotone across
partitions, for every history. For each participant $p$ whose run emits, let
$\rho_p$ be the last source map it emits, at input $i_p$; $\rho_p$ \outrel every
output $p$ emitted. Let $j$ be a common \indesc. We take a serialization
$L_p$ of $i_p$ explaining $\rho_p$, and extend it to $j$ by appending the new
operations of $p$ and the new writes in any order extending $\to$. Since no
new operation causally precedes an old one, the result extends $\to$ and
explains the same reads. With these serializations for the replicas that
emitted and arbitrary ones for the others, the union of the $\rho_p$ lies in
$\fiber{j}$ and \outrel every emitted output. Separability is
not used. Two answers to the same read remain incompatible, but only the
reading replica may emit either.

\subsection{Real-time causal consistency}
\label{app:rtc}

\textcolor{reviewed}{Mahajan et al.\ show that a system can provide real-time causal consistency,
but no stronger consistency, while staying always available (every read and
write completes whatever messages are lost) and one-way convergent (a node
able to receive from another eventually reflects the updates the other
knows)~\cite{mahajan2011consistency}.} We recover both parts with real-time
precedence recorded in the input. The argument rests on a property of
products of independent views.

\paragraph{Independent products.}
In the real-time causal register below, each replica exposes a view of its
own reads, and the replicas choose their views independently. We first state
the facts we need for any such product of independent views. Fix participants
$\mathcal N$ with $|\mathcal N|\ge2$. For each $p$ let $(W_p,\le_p)$ be a poset
and, for each input $i$, let $V_p(i)\subseteq W_p$ be a nonempty antichain, the
\emph{views} $p$ may expose at $i$. A \emph{partial tuple} $x$ assigns to each
$p$ a value $x_p$ in $W_p$ or $\bot$, ordered coordinate-wise with $\bot$ below
every value; $x$ is a \emph{restriction} of a tuple $y$ when each coordinate
$x_p$ is $\bot$ or $y_p$. The \emph{product interface} has a row
$(i,x)$ for every restriction $x$ of every $y\in\prod_pV_p(i)$. A
\emph{strengthening} $T$ selects at each input a set of tuples in
$\prod_pV_p(i)$, and its rows are their restrictions. It is \emph{strict} when
it omits a row of the product interface, and \emph{locally conservative} when
every $v\in V_p(i)$ is the $p$-coordinate of a tuple it selects at $i$. Under
the \emph{owner deployment}, $p$ may emit exactly the rows whose tuple is
$\bot$ outside coordinate $p$.

\begin{theorem}[Independent products]
\label{thm:product-frontier}
\leavevmode
\begin{enumerate}
\item
If views continue (for $i\InputOrd j$ and $v\in V_p(i)$ some $w\in
        V_p(j)$ has $v\le_p w$), the product interface is monotone across
        partitions under the owner deployment.
\item
No strict, locally conservative strengthening is monotone across
        partitions under its owner deployment.
\end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
(1) Fix a participant run family and a common \indesc $j$. If $p$ belongs to
the family, the remainder table of $p$'s run has a row $(k_p,u)$, and $u$ \outrel every output $p$ emitted
(Lemma~\ref{lem:run-residual}). As $u$ is a restriction of some
$y\in\prod_qV_q(k_p)$, continuation gives $w_p\in V_p(j)$ with $y_p\le_p w_p$. Otherwise pick any $w_p\in V_p(j)$. Each output of $p$ is $\bot$ outside
coordinate $p$, so $w$ \outrel every emitted output. (2) Strictness gives $y\in\prod_pV_p(i)$ that $T$ does not select at $i$. By
local conservativity, the tuple $e_p$ that is $y_p$ at $p$ and $\bot$
elsewhere is a row of $T$ at $i$, authorized for $p$. Let every participant
emit $e_p$ at $i$; every partition separates two of them. A common \outnoun
at $i$ is a restriction of a selected $z$ with $y_p\le_p z_p$ for every $p$, so
$y=z$ by the antichain property, a contradiction.
\end{proof}

\paragraph{Real-time causal views.}
Consider the histories of Appendix~\ref{app:registers} with a second strict
partial order $\prec$: $a\prec b$ when $a$ ended before $b$ began. We assume
that $a\to b$ implies $a\prec b$, so replicas propagate only completed writes,
and that $\to$ totally orders the operations of each replica. One input
\inrel another when it does so as a history and $\prec$ agrees on the old
operations. A write $w$ is \emph{visible} to a read $r$ when $w\to r$. A
\emph{real-time causal view} of replica $p$ assigns to every read $r$ of $p$ a
source $s(r)$ such that
\begin{enumerate}
\item
$s(r)$ is a write visible to $r$, or $v_0$ if none is;
\item
$s(r)\not\prec w$ for every write $w$ visible to $r$;
\item
if $r'\to r$ is a read of $p$ to which $s(r)$ is visible, then
        $s(r')=s(r)$.
\end{enumerate}
Intuitively, these are Mahajan et al.'s checks for the order that $p$'s reads expose, in
which each visible write precedes the read and each overwritten write
precedes the source. Item~(1) says a replica returns only writes it has
received, (2) is their real-time check ``time doesn't travel backward'', and
(3) keeps one replica's overwrites consistent. Their reads return all
concurrent latest writes; ours return one.

Views exist: as each read's source we can take the visible write that comes
latest in a linear extension of $\prec$. Views also continue. An \indesc
changes neither a read's causal past nor $\prec$ on it, so an old read keeps its
source. A new read takes as source its latest visible write in a linear
extension of $\prec$ together with the edges $x\prec s(r)$, one for each old
read $r$ and each write $x\ne s(r)$ visible to $r$. All views of $p$ at an
input are defined on the same reads, so no view extends a different one, and
they form an antichain. By Theorem~\ref{thm:product-frontier}(1), the real-time causal
product interface, in which each replica answers its own reads, is monotone
across partitions under the owner deployment.

\paragraph{Maximality.}
A strengthening is \emph{real-time blind} when a tuple it selects at an input
stays selected at the same history with fewer real-time edges, provided its
views are still real-time causal there. Mahajan et al.'s implementations do
not observe real time, so they return the same answers at both inputs.

\begin{proposition}[Real-time causal maximality]
\label{prop:rtc-maximal}
No strict, real-time blind strengthening of the real-time causal product
interface is monotone across partitions under its owner deployment.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
By Theorem~\ref{thm:product-frontier}(2) it suffices to show local
conservativity. Let $v$ be a view of $p$ at $(h,\prec)$. Let $a\prec_v b$
when $a\prec b$, or when $a=x$ or $a\prec x$, and $s(r)=b$ or $s(r)\prec b$, for a
read $r$ of $p$ and a write $x\ne s(r)$ visible to $r$. Items~(2) and~(3) and program order
make $\prec_v$ a strict partial order, and it contains $\to$. At $(h,\prec_v)$,
$v$ is $p$'s only view: by~(1) a source is a visible write, and a visible
$x\ne s(r)$ has $x\prec_v s(r)$, which~(2) forbids. Every input has a row of the
strengthening, and rows are restrictions of selected tuples, so it selects
some tuple at $(h,\prec_v)$, and that tuple's $p$-coordinate is $v$. Real-time edges
only exclude views, so its views are real-time causal at $(h,\prec)$, and
blindness selects it there.
\end{proof}

Each hypothesis has a counterpart in Mahajan et al.'s theorem. Their always
availability corresponds to two conditions here: $T$ selects a tuple at every
input, so every replica can answer every read, and the system has Deployment
Availability. One-way convergence needs no separate hypothesis: the input
records which writes each replica has received, the environment supplies that
input, and views must respect $\to$. Real-time blindness is their assumption
that an implementation does not observe real time, which their proof uses to
swap the real-time order of concurrent writes. The proof of
Proposition~\ref{prop:rtc-maximal} needs it too, since a strengthening may read
real time from the input, for instance to break ties between writes that
overlap in real time.

\section{Choosing the Explicit Relation}
\label{app:cap-response}

Theorem~\ref{thm:complete-cap} fixes the explicit relation $E$ in advance. Gilbert and Lynch ask only that every request receive some
response~\cite{gilbert2002cap}, and leave the system to choose which. This
appendix lets the system choose its explicit relation among declared
acceptable responses, shows that the result is Theorem~\ref{thm:complete-cap}
applied to the chosen relation, and derives the Gilbert--Lynch theorem with
precedence taken from real time. Throughout, we fix an \spectype $\Spec$ and the partitioned histories of
Section~\ref{sec:complete-cap}; the environment supplies any input to any
participant and resolves the strategies' nondeterministic choices. Emitted
rows are as in Definition~\ref{def:emitted-rows}.

\subsection{Obligations, availability, and locality}

\begin{definition}[Response obligation, answering]
\label{def:response-obligation}
A \emph{response obligation} assigns to each participant $p$ a relation
$Q_p\subseteq P$ that is total on $I$ and upward closed within fibers: if
$(i,o)\in Q_p$, $(i,u)\in P$, and $o\OutputOrd u$, then $(i,u)\in Q_p$. An
output in $Q_p(i)$ \emph{answers} at $i$ for $p$.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Uniform obligation]
\label{def:uniform-obligation}
A response obligation is \emph{uniform} when every $Q_p$ is the same
relation $Q$.
\end{definition}

The obligation is part of the declared spec. Response Availability is only
as strong as $Q_p$: if a single output answers at every input, every spec
meets condition~(1) of Theorem~\ref{thm:complete-cap-response}.

A continuation of a partitioned history is \emph{within $p$'s side} when
the system produces it, it adds only history events of $p$'s side, and none of its
receives takes a message sent from another side. A same-side continuation
for $p$ (Definition~\ref{def:local-continuation}) is one within $p$'s side
that adds no input event.

\begin{definition}[Response Availability]
\label{def:response-availability}
A system has \emph{Response Availability} for $(Q_p)_{p\in\mathcal N}$ when, in every
partitioned history it produces, each participant $p$, ending with current
input $i$, satisfies two conditions.
\begin{enumerate}
\item
\emph{Answering.}  In some continuation within $p$'s side that
        adds no input event at $p$, $p$ emits some output in $Q_p(i)$.
\item
\emph{Input acceptance.}  For every $j\InputGe i$, in some
        continuation within $p$'s side, $p$ records $\mathsf{input}(j)$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{definition}

Input acceptance says that $p$ never rules out an \indesc of its current
input. It excludes specs whose answers require ruling out such an input, such
as the election under the equality order (Figure~\ref{fig:nonmonotone-frame}),
and it matches Gilbert and Lynch's requirement that nodes accept every
request.

\begin{definition}[Response Partition Consistency]
\label{def:response-partition-consistency}
A system has \emph{Response Partition Consistency} when, in every partitioned
history it produces and at every common \indesc $j$ of the participants'
current inputs, some $u\in\fiber{j}$ \outrel every output emitted in the
history.
\end{definition}

This is the part of Partition Consistency that the converse of
Theorem~\ref{thm:complete-cap} and Corollary~\ref{cor:complete-cap-any-system}
use, so both also hold with Response
Partition Consistency in place of Partition Consistency. Confluence at $j$
implies a common \outnoun at $j$ (Corollary~\ref{cor:reconciliation-any-basis}). The two coincide for a system with basis $\mathcal B_P$ and no \commitment
events, whose participant runs are therefore canonical runs, by
Lemma~\ref{lem:reconciliation} applied to those runs
(\S\ref{sec:distributed-consistency}). With other \commitments the
converse can fail: participants may prune their states so that they cannot
be reconciled while their emitted outputs still have a common \outnoun.

A system is \emph{replicated} (SPMD) when its obligation is uniform and every
participant runs the same strategy, which depends only on the participant's
initial input and local history, not on its identity or the number of
participants.

\begin{lemma}[Locality]
\label{lem:locality}
For each participant $p$ in some set of participants, let $\ell_p$ be a local
history that $p$'s
strategy can produce from initial input $i_p$ and that contains no receive. Placing each $\ell_p$ on its own side, with at least two sides and
any further participants
idle, gives a partitioned history that the system produces. If the system is
replicated, a receive-free local history that the strategy can produce for one participant
it can produce for any participant with the same initial input, in any
participant set.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Whether a history is produced is checked one history event at a time against each
participant's own local prefix, as fixed before
Definition~\ref{def:local-continuation},
and there are no receives to match. Replication makes that check independent of the participant.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Response interfaces}

\begin{definition}[Response interface, self-continuing]
\label{def:response-interface}
For a uniform obligation $Q$, a \emph{response interface} is an explicit
relation $T\subseteq Q$ that is total on $I$ and \emph{self-continuing}: if
$(i,o)\in T$ and $i\InputOrd j$, then some $u\in T(j)$ \outrel $o$.
\end{definition}

Lifted Monotonicity for $T$ is that of Definition~\ref{def:lm-for-A}, and
the reconciling output may lie outside $T$. With $T=P$ it is the
common-extension condition of Proposition~\ref{prop:common-expansion-form}, and
by Lemma~\ref{lem:reconciliation} it is confluence of the canonical runs whose
emitted rows lie in $T$.

A response interface is an explicit relation that a system can always
serve. Consider the canonical system for $T$ (Section~\ref{sec:complete-cap}),
which emits by the canonical output event any output of $T$ in the
current fiber. Each participant's emitted outputs form a chain
$o_1\OutputOrd\cdots\OutputOrd o_m$, so by Lemma~\ref{lem:run-residual} its
table is $P[\mathrm{in}\InputGe i,\ \mathrm{out}\OutputGe o_m]$, or
$P[\mathrm{in}\InputGe i]$ before any emission. Self-continuation applied to
the row of $o_m$, or totality of $T$, gives some $u\in T(i)$ in that table. So every participant's fiber intersects $T$, and Availability for $T$ gives
Answering. The same argument at an input $k\InputGe i$ leaves a row at $k$,
which gives Input acceptance.

\begin{theorem}[Complete CAP with chosen responses]
\label{thm:complete-cap-response}
For an \spectype $\Spec$ and a uniform obligation $Q$,
the following are equivalent:
\begin{enumerate}
\item
some replicated system has Response Availability for $Q$ and
        Response Partition Consistency;
\item
$\Spec$ satisfies Lifted Monotonicity for some response interface $T\subseteq Q$;
\item
for some response interface $T\subseteq Q$, some system has
        Availability for $T$ and Partition Consistency.
\end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
(2$\Leftrightarrow$3) is Theorem~\ref{thm:complete-cap} and
Corollary~\ref{cor:complete-cap-any-system} with $E=T$.

(2$\Rightarrow$1.)  We take the canonical system for $T$. It is replicated, and by the
paragraph above it has Answering and Input acceptance. The outputs emitted in
a history are rows of $T$ at inputs that every common \indesc $j$ of the
current inputs \inrel, so Lifted Monotonicity for $T$ gives Response Partition
Consistency.

(1$\Rightarrow$2.)  We call an emission \emph{isolated} when its participant has
received no message before it, and let $T$ be the set of rows $(i,o)$ with
$o\in Q(i)$ emitted in an isolated emission in some partitioned history of the
system; such a history and participant are a \emph{witness} for $(i,o)$. Then $T\subseteq Q$. When a participant is alone on its side, every
continuation that Response Availability supplies adds only its own history
events and, since the history is partitioned, no receive, so the emission it
yields is isolated. By
Lemma~\ref{lem:locality}, a witness's local history up to the emission can be
replayed by a participant alone on its side, with an idle participant
forming a second side. \emph{Totality:} for each $i$, Answering in the empty history, for a participant
with initial input $i$ alone on its side, gives a row of $T$ at $i$. \emph{Self-continuation:} given $(i,o)\in T$ and $j\InputGe i$, we replay the
witness, record $\mathsf{input}(j)$ by Input acceptance (truncating just after
it), and answer by Answering with some $u\in Q(j)$. By
Lemmas~\ref{lem:canonical-equivalent} and~\ref{lem:run-residual} the table
before that emission is contained in $P[\mathrm{in}\InputGe j,\
\mathrm{out}\OutputGe o]$, so $o\OutputOrd u$ and $(j,u)\in T$. \emph{Lifted Monotonicity for $T$:} given rows $(i_r,o_r)$ of $T$ with common \indesc
$j$, we replay each witness on its own participant, truncated after the
emission, and add a participant with initial input $j$. By
Lemma~\ref{lem:locality} this is a partitioned history of the system, and
Response Partition Consistency, applied to that history, gives $u\in\fiber{j}$
\outrelpart every $o_r$. If there are no rows, totality of $P$ gives some
$u\in\fiber{j}$.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[Without replication]
For a system that is not replicated, fix the participant set. The same proof,
with $T_p$ the rows $p$ emits in histories where it is alone on its side, gives one response interface
$T_p\subseteq Q_p$ per participant. Each strategy sees only its own participant's initial input, and each
participant is replayed once, so consistency is required only for families
that draw on each $T_p$ at most once.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Choosing versus requiring every output}

Theorem~\ref{thm:complete-cap} with $E=P$ requires every permitted output to
stay available; Theorem~\ref{thm:complete-cap-response} lets the system choose
which outputs to serve. Lifted Monotonicity implies the conditions of the
latter theorem for every uniform obligation, with $T=Q$. To see that $Q$ is
self-continuing, let $(i,o)\in Q$ and $i\InputOrd j$. Relational Monotonicity
gives $u\in\fiber{j}$ with $o\OutputOrd u$, and totality gives some $q\in
Q(j)$. Because the spec has directed fibers, some $w\in\fiber{j}$
\outrelpart $u$ and $q$, and upward closure puts $w$ in $Q(j)$. The
common-extension condition gives Lifted Monotonicity for $Q$.

The converse fails. Let $I=\{0,1\}$ with $0\InputOrd 1$, $\bot\OutputOrd a$ and
$\bot\OutputOrd b$ with $a,b$ incomparable, $\fiber{0}=\{\bot,a\}$,
$\fiber{1}=\{b\}$, and $Q=P$. Lifted Monotonicity fails at $(0,a)$, but $\Spec$
satisfies Lifted Monotonicity for the response interface $\{(0,\bot),(1,b)\}$: a system that
answers $\bot$ at $0$ never emits the output that conflicts with $b$.

For a functional spec the two coincide: a total $T\subseteq Q\subseteq
P$ equals $P$, and both self-continuation and Lifted Monotonicity for $P$ are
monotonicity.

If a total $T\subseteq Q$ satisfies Lifted Monotonicity as a spec in its own
right, then $T$ is a response interface for which $\Spec$ satisfies Lifted
Monotonicity. The converse fails: in the following example a response
interface works, but no sub-specification of $Q$ satisfies Lifted
Monotonicity. Let $I=\{i_1,i_2,j,k_1,k_2\}$ with $i_1,i_2\InputOrd j\InputOrd
k_1,k_2$ and no other relations, so $k_1$ and $k_2$ have no common \indesc. Let $\fiber{i_1}=\{a\}$, $\fiber{i_2}=\{b\}$, $\fiber{j}=\{c_1,c_2,d_1,d_2\}$,
$\fiber{k_1}=\{e_1\}$, $\fiber{k_2}=\{e_2\}$, and $Q=P$, where $\OutputOrd$ is
the least order with $a\OutputOrd c_1$, $b\OutputOrd c_2$, $c_1,c_2\OutputOrd
d_1,d_2$, $c_1,c_2,d_1\OutputOrd e_1$, and $c_1,c_2,d_2\OutputOrd e_2$. Then
$T=\{(i_1,a),(i_2,b),(j,c_1),(j,c_2),(k_1,e_1),(k_2,e_2)\}$ is a response
interface for which $\Spec$ satisfies Lifted Monotonicity: $d_1$ reconciles families at $j$, and
$e_1$ or $e_2$ those at $k_1$ or $k_2$. A sub-specification with Lifted
Monotonicity must contain $(i_1,a)$ and $(i_2,b)$, hence $d_1$ or $d_2$ at $j$,
which has no \outnoun at $k_2$ or $k_1$ respectively. Reconciling outputs
need not lie in $T$: a participant that emits $d_1$ or $d_2$ rules out $k_2$ or $k_1$.

\subsection{Gilbert--Lynch with real-time precedence}

Section~\ref{sec:complete-cap} derives the Gilbert--Lynch theorem from
Corollary~\ref{cor:complete-cap-any-system}, with real-time precedence supplied
as input. Here precedence comes from real time, and consistency is required
only at the precedence an execution realizes.

\begin{lemma}[Forced answer]
\label{lem:forced-answer}
Let $i\InputOrd j$, and suppose no output in $Q_q(i)$ has an \outnoun in
$\fiber{j}$. In a system with Answering for $(Q_p)$
(Definition~\ref{def:response-availability}(1)), consider a partitioned
history that the system produces, in which $q$ is alone on its side,
$q$'s current input is $i$, and $j$ \inrel every participant's current input. Then some continuation within $q$'s side that adds only history events of $q$,
and no input event, has emitted outputs with no common \outnoun in
$\fiber{j}$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Answering gives such a continuation in which $q$ emits some $o\in Q_q(i)$. No
current input changes, so $j$ still \inrel all of them, and no output of
$\fiber{j}$ \outrel $o$.
\end{proof}

\paragraph{The register.}
An input is a finite operation history: a set of read and write invocations at
replicas with a strict partial order of real-time precedence. One history
\inrel another when it contains all of its operations and precedence edges. An output is a response map assigning values to reads and acknowledgments to
writes, ordered by extension. The fiber $\fiber{h}$ contains the maps
explained by some linearization of $h$, a total order of its operations
extending precedence~\cite{herlihy1990linearizability} in which each answered
read returns the last preceding write, or the initial value $v_0$. The
obligation $Q_p(h)$ consists of the maps in $\fiber{h}$ that answer every read
and acknowledge every write invoked at $p$. Every finite history has a
linearization, so $Q_p$ is total, and it is upward closed within fibers. Real-time precedence belongs to the input: the environment invokes $b$ after
seeing $a$'s response, so the edge from $a$ to $b$ is information it supplies
(\S\ref{sec:election-story}).

Real time is the order of history events in an execution: an \emph{interleaving}
$\lambda$ of a distributed history is a linear order of its history events consistent
with happens-before, and no strategy observes it. The \emph{invocation} of an
operation is the first input event at its replica whose input contains it,
and its \emph{response} is the first output event at that replica whose map
answers it. For a prefix $C$ of $\lambda$ (a consistent cut), the
\emph{realized history} $h_C$ contains the operations invoked in $C$, with an
edge from $a$ to $b$ when $a$'s response precedes $b$'s invocation in
$\lambda$. If $C'\subseteq C$ is also a prefix, $h_C$ \inrel $h_{C'}$. The
realized history need not be a common \indesc of the participants' current
inputs: an input event may introduce two operations with an edge between them
before either responds.

A \emph{client history} has two participants with empty initial inputs, and
each of its input events adds one operation, invoked at its participant after
all of that participant's earlier operations are answered.

\begin{corollary}[Gilbert--Lynch]
\label{cor:gilbert-lynch}
No system for the linearizable register has Answering for $(Q_p)$ together
with the following, both required only of client histories: for every
partitioned client history it produces, interleaving $\lambda$, and prefix $C$
of $\lambda$ such that $h_C$ is a common \indesc of the current inputs at $C$,
the outputs emitted in $C$ have a common \outnoun in $\fiber{h_C}$.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
Let replicas $p$ and $q$ start with empty inputs on separate sides, and let
the environment invoke a write of $\dog\neq v_0$ at $p$. Answering gives a
continuation in which $p$ acknowledges it. The environment then invokes a read
$r_q$ at $q$, after the acknowledgment in $\lambda$. The history is a client history. No message
crosses the partition, so $q$'s current input is $i_q=\{r_q\}$, and $j=h_C$
contains the write, $r_q$, and an edge from the write to $r_q$; it is a common
\indesc of both current inputs. Every map in $Q_q(i_q)$ answers $r_q$ with
$v_0$, and every linearization of $j$ places the write first, so no output in
$Q_q(i_q)$ has an \outnoun in $\fiber{j}$. Lemma~\ref{lem:forced-answer}
gives a continuation adding no input event, so the realized history stays
$j$, whose emitted outputs have no common \outnoun in $\fiber{j}$.
\end{proof}

A system with Response Availability, and with a common \outnoun at $h_C$ for
every prefix $C$ of every partitioned history, satisfies these hypotheses, so
the corollary excludes it too. The register's obligation differs by replica, so
Theorem~\ref{thm:complete-cap-response} does not apply as stated. The
corollary yields Gilbert and Lynch's theorem in their asynchronous network
model~\cite{gilbert2002cap}.

\begin{proposition}[Gilbert--Lynch, asynchronous model]
\label{prop:gl-async}
In the asynchronous network model with at least two nodes, no algorithm for a
read/write register is both available (a request pending at a node is
answered in some extension of the execution that adds no request and loses
every message) and atomic (every execution with well-formed clients has a
linearization).
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
We translate the algorithm into a system with the canonical basis. Participant
$p$ runs node $p$. An invocation becomes the input event whose input is $p$'s
view: the operations invoked at $p$, with no precedence. A response becomes an
output event emitting $p$'s response map, and $p$ may also re-emit its current
map. With every message lost, $p$'s history is node $p$'s run in the
execution where it is alone, and atomicity of that execution puts the map in
the fiber at the view, so the system can perform its output events under the
canonical basis. Availability, applied
to each unanswered operation of the view, gives Answering. An interleaving of
a client history is an execution of the algorithm whose real-time order is the
precedence of $h_C$, so atomicity gives a common \outnoun in $\fiber{h_C}$. The corollary excludes the algorithm.
\end{proof}

Gilbert and Lynch require availability and atomicity in all fair executions,
including those in which messages are lost, in Lynch's asynchronous network
model~\cite[Theorem~1]{gilbert2002cap}\cite[Chapter~8]{lynch1996distributed}. Both imply the forms in the proposition. In their model any message may be
lost and client requests are inputs, so a finite execution extends to a fair
execution with no new request in which every message is lost; their own
proofs extend executions fairly in this way. Availability answers the pending
request at some finite point of that extension, and the prefix up to that
response is the extension the proposition requires. Atomicity of the fair
extension gives atomicity of the finite execution, since atomicity is a safety
property and so holds of every prefix of an atomic
execution~\cite[Chapter~13]{lynch1996distributed}.

\section{\Commitment Bases for Two-Phase Locking and Multiversion Timestamp Ordering}
\label{app:cc-bases}

This appendix gives the serializability spec of
Sections~\ref{sec:ambiguity-story} and~\ref{sec:systems} in full, and the
\commitment bases of two-phase locking (2PL) and multiversion timestamp ordering
(MVTO). Schedulers act as operations arrive, and they report results while
transactions are still running, such as a read result before its reader
commits, so an output may leave transactions undecided.

\paragraph{Specification.}
An input is a finite set of transactions, each given by the sequence of
operations that its client has issued so far: reads and writes of keys,
possibly followed by a request to commit. One input precedes another when
every transaction of the first is in the second and its sequence in the first
is a prefix of its sequence in the second; once a transaction has requested
commit, its sequence no longer grows. An input does not order the operations
of different transactions. The order in which they arrive is the order of
input events in a run, which the environment controls.

An output has three parts: the committed transactions, in a serial order; the
aborted transactions; and the read results reported so far. A read result
says which version the $k$-th operation of a transaction, a read of a key $x$,
returned: the write of $x$ by some transaction, or the initial version. The
write of $x$ by a transaction is its last write of $x$. A transaction that is
neither committed nor aborted is \emph{undecided}, and its reads may already be
reported. One output \outrel another when it extends all three parts.

An output is \emph{complete} at an input when it commits or aborts every
transaction of the input, and not both, and commits only transactions whose
commit request is in the input. A complete output is permitted when it meets two
conditions. First, each reported read result is for a read in the input and
returns the initial version or the write of its key by a transaction of the
input. Second, each read of a key $x$ by a committed transaction $T$ returns
$T$'s own write of $x$ if $T$ writes $x$ earlier in its sequence. Otherwise it
returns the write of $x$ by the last transaction before $T$ in the serial order
that writes $x$, or the initial version if none does. Reads by aborted
transactions are otherwise unconstrained. Any output is permitted when some permitted complete output
\outrel it, that is, when it can still be completed. The figures of
Sections~\ref{sec:ambiguity-story}--\ref{sec:systems} show only complete
outputs without aborts or read results, as serial orders, at inputs in which
every transaction has requested commit.

We write an output as $(L,X,\rho)$: $X$ is the set of aborted transactions, $L$
is the serial order, a total order on the set $\mathrm{dom}\,L$ of committed
ones, and $\rho$ is the set of reported read results. Then
\[
  \begin{aligned}
  (L,X,\rho)\OutputOrd(L',X',\rho')
  \iff{}& X\subseteq X',\ \ \mathrm{dom}\,L\subseteq\mathrm{dom}\,L',\\
         & L'|_{\mathrm{dom}\,L}=L,\ \ \rho\subseteq\rho'.
  \end{aligned}
\]
Every input has a permitted row: the one that aborts every transaction and
reports nothing. Let $\{A,B\}$ be an input in which $A$ and $B$ have each
requested commit. The rows $(\{A,B\},([A,B],\varnothing,\varnothing))$ and
$(\{A,B\},([B,A],\varnothing,\varnothing))$ of
Figure~\ref{fig:ambiguity-frames}(f) have no common \outnoun, which would order
$A$ both before and after $B$, so the spec is not monotone. The
\emph{abort-free} spec permits a row when it can be completed with
$X=\varnothing$, where a complete output instead commits exactly the transactions
whose commit request is in the input and leaves the others undecided; the same
two rows show that it is not monotone either.

\paragraph{\Commitments.}
Each \commitment is a condition on complete outputs. Applied at a state
$(i,R)$, it keeps the rows of $R$ that can still be completed, within $R$, by a
complete row satisfying the condition, and removes the rest. It is defined
when it keeps some row at $i$ and removes some row.
\begin{itemize}
\item
$\mathsf{order}(A,B)$, for distinct $A,B\in i$: $B$ is not committed
        before $A$, that is, not $B<_L A$.
\item
$\mathsf{TxCommit}(A)$, for $A$ whose commit request is in $i$:
        $A\notin X$.\quad
        $\mathsf{abort}(A)$, for $A\in i$: $A\notin\mathrm{dom}\,L$.
\item
$\mathsf{stamp}(A)$, for $A\in i\setminus Z$, given the set $Z\subseteq
        i$ of transactions stamped before $A$: no committed transaction outside
        $Z\cup\{A\}$ precedes $A$ in $L$, and no committed transaction of $Z$
        follows $A$.
\item
$\mathsf{read}(A,k,v)$, for $A\in i$ whose $k$-th operation in $i$ is a
        read: the output reports that this read returned the version $v$.
\end{itemize}
Each is a \commitment (Definition~\ref{def:commitment}). If a kept row \outrel
another row at the same input, the complete row that keeps it also
\outrel the other, which is therefore kept; so no removed row is \outrelpast by
a kept one, as Equation~\ref{eq:commitment-unsubsumed} requires. The first four
conditions concern only $L$ and $X$, and for them a complete row is kept
exactly when it satisfies the condition, since two permitted complete outputs
at one input, one \outrelpart the other, have the same serial order and abort
set.

The bases are
$\mathcal B_{\mathrm{2PL}}=\{\mathsf{order},\mathsf{TxCommit},\mathsf{abort},\mathsf{read}\}$
and
$\mathcal B_{\mathrm{MVTO}}=\{\mathsf{stamp},\mathsf{TxCommit},\mathsf{abort},\mathsf{read}\}$,
over all transactions, positions, and versions. They share
$\mathsf{TxCommit}$, $\mathsf{abort}$, and $\mathsf{read}$, and differ in how
they order. Let $\{A,B,C\}$ be an input in which $A$, $B$, and $C$ have each
requested commit, after any operations. At its canonical state, no \commitment
of $\mathcal B_{\mathrm{MVTO}}$ has the effect of $\mathsf{order}(A,B)$, and no
\commitment of $\mathcal B_{\mathrm{2PL}}$ has the effect of $\mathsf{stamp}(B)$
with $Z=\varnothing$.

A scheduler applies its \commitments in \commitment events
$\mathsf{apply}(\gamma)$ (Definition~\ref{def:commitment-event}) and reports
results by output events $\mathsf{output}(o)$
(Definition~\ref{def:system-output-event}). Each protocol step below responds to
the arrival of an operation, that is, to an input event, and says which
\commitments it applies. Every state that a run under either basis reaches
has two properties: each row has a complete \outnoun in the table at its
input, and any output between two rows of the table at one input is itself a
row of the table. Because of these properties, a scheduler reports a read by
first applying $\mathsf{read}(A,k,v)$ in a \commitment event; emitting the
result is then a legal output.

\paragraph{2PL.}
When an operation of $B$ arrives, 2PL requests a lock on its key, shared for a
read and exclusive for a write. If $A$ holds that lock in a conflicting mode,
$B$ waits, and the scheduler applies $\mathsf{order}(A,B)$ in a \commitment
event, reporting nothing. When every remaining complete row that commits
both already places $A$ before $B$, $\mathsf{order}(A,B)$ would remove
nothing, so it is undefined and the scheduler applies nothing. This happens when an earlier wait has applied
$\mathsf{order}(A,B)$, or when a reported read result fixes the order, as when
$A$ has read the initial version of the key that $B$ writes. On complete
rows without aborts,
$\mathsf{order}(A,B)$ keeps exactly those with $A$ before $B$
(Figure~\ref{fig:cc-commitments}(h)). At the canonical state of $\{A,B,C\}$ it
removes $[B,A,C]$ and keeps $[A,B,C]$ and $[C,A,B]$, so it is a \commitment, and it
is not the canonical \commitment $x_o$ of any output $o$. When a read is granted its lock,
the scheduler applies $\mathsf{read}$ and reports the result. When a commit
request arrives, the scheduler applies $\mathsf{TxCommit}$ and releases the
transaction's locks.

A deadlock between $A$ and $B$ arises when $B$ waits for a lock that $A$ holds
and an operation of $A$ then waits for a lock that $B$ holds: the scheduler has
applied $\mathsf{order}(A,B)$ and later $\mathsf{order}(B,A)$. Then no
remaining row, at any input, commits both $A$ and $B$, so every remaining
complete row aborts one of them, and resolving the deadlock means aborting one. Once the scheduler applies $\mathsf{TxCommit}(A)$, every remaining complete row
aborts $B$, and $\mathsf{TxCommit}(B)$ is never again defined. Under the
abort-free spec, once both orderings have been applied, no remaining
row is at an input in which both $A$ and $B$ have requested commit. The system
never accepts both requests, so at least one of $A$ and $B$ waits forever. Whether a deadlock can be resolved thus depends on which of the two
specs the system implements.

\paragraph{MVTO.}
We follow Bernstein and Goodman~\cite{bernstein1981concurrency}. When the
first operation of a transaction $A$ arrives, $A$ receives a timestamp above
all issued ones, and the scheduler applies $\mathsf{stamp}(A)$, with $Z$ the
transactions stamped before $A$, in a \commitment event. On complete rows
without aborts, $\mathsf{stamp}(B)$ with $Z=\varnothing$ keeps exactly those
with $B$ first (Figure~\ref{fig:cc-commitments}(i)); at the canonical state of
$\{A,B,C\}$ it is a \commitment, and it is not the canonical \commitment $x_o$ of any output. When
a read of $x$ by $A$ arrives, it returns $A$'s own write of $x$ if $A$ has
written $x$, and otherwise the committed version of $x$ with the largest
timestamp below $A$'s; the scheduler applies $\mathsf{read}$ and reports the
result. When a write of $x$ by $A$ arrives, it is rejected, and $A$ aborted,
if a transaction stamped after $A$ has already read a version of $x$ stamped
before $A$; the scheduler then applies $\mathsf{abort}(A)$ and reports the
abort. When the commit request of $A$ arrives, the scheduler applies
$\mathsf{TxCommit}(A)$.

\emph{What reporting a read excludes.}  Suppose $B$ is stamped after $A$, and
$B$'s read of $x$ returned the initial version or the write of $x$ by a
transaction stamped before $A$. At an input in which $A$ writes $x$, a
complete row that satisfies both stamps and commits both $A$ and $B$ places $A$
between that version and $B$. In that row the read must return $A$'s write
or a later one, so the row does not permit the reported read. Hence, after the
two stamps, no row that reports the read and is at an input in which $A$
writes $x$ commits both $A$ and $B$. Once $\mathsf{read}$ has been applied, no
remaining row at such an input commits both, whether it reports the read or
not. Reporting the read therefore rules out one possibility: that $A$ writes
$x$ and both $A$ and $B$ commit. MVTO reports the read when it arrives,
whether or not $A$'s write has arrived. The condition on the version, that
$B$ read a version older than $A$, is needed. At an input of three
transactions $A$, $W$, $B$, stamped in that order, each having requested
commit, with $A$ and $W$ writing $x$ and $B$ only reading it, the complete row
that commits all three in stamp order and reports that $B$ read $W$'s write is
permitted and satisfies every stamp. There $B$ read a version written after
$A$, and all three commit.

\emph{When the abort is entailed.}  Which of $A$ and $B$ aborts depends on
whether $B$ is decided. Suppose $A$'s write of $x$ has arrived. If every
remaining complete row commits $B$, for instance after $\mathsf{TxCommit}(B)$,
then every remaining complete row aborts $A$. An output that reports the read
and that every remaining row \outrel, with $A$'s abort added, is then a legal
output, so the spec entails the abort. While $B$ is undecided, the spec
does not entail it. Consider a run in which $A$'s first operation, a read of $x$,
arrives, then $B$'s read of $x$, and then $A$'s write of $x$, with further
transactions free to arrive. The scheduler applies $\mathsf{stamp}(A)$ and
$\mathsf{stamp}(B)$ in \commitment events and reports that $B$ read the initial
version of $x$. Reporting the read is not a legal output on its own: at an
input in which $A$ writes $x$, the row that commits $A$ and then $B$ cannot be
completed with it. Once the scheduler applies $\mathsf{read}$ in a
\commitment event, reporting the read is a legal output. After $A$'s write
arrives, two completions remain at the input in which both then request
commit: one commits $B$ and aborts $A$, and the other commits $A$ and aborts
$B$. Emitting $A$'s abort is then not a legal output, and $\mathsf{abort}(A)$ and
$\mathsf{abort}(B)$ are both defined and keep different completions. MVTO's
write rule aborts $A$ at once: the scheduler applies $\mathsf{abort}(A)$ in a
\commitment event, after which reporting the abort is a legal output. That abort is the scheduler's choice between two
outcomes the spec still permits. If the scheduler instead waits until
$B$'s commit request arrives and applies $\mathsf{TxCommit}(B)$, $A$'s abort
becomes a legal output.

\paragraph{Comparison.}
As operations arrive, 2PL applies \commitments that fix the relative order of
conflicting transactions only. MVTO, when a transaction's first operation
arrives, applies a \commitment that fixes its position against all present and
future transactions. Both report reads through $\mathsf{read}$. Under MVTO,
suppose $B$ reads a version of a key older than $A$, which is stamped before
$B$. Reporting the read is then a \commitment: if $A$ writes that key, $A$
and $B$ do not both commit. The write rule settles this by aborting $A$ when
its write arrives, and the spec forces that abort only after $B$ commits.

\section{Additional Applications}
\label{app:additional-applications}

Each example below identifies an obstruction in a spec. None
replaces model-specific results about failures, computability, or
termination.

\subsection{Committed authority, then monotone evidence}

Suppose a \commitment fixes an initial authority and a rule that determines
each certified successor. Inputs are finite sets of protocol messages (proposals,
votes, certificates), ordered by inclusion. An output is a triple $(E,V,D)$:
a certified epoch chain $E$, which is a finite sequence of epochs, each
certified under the authority of its predecessor; a set $V$ of votes or certificates; and a set
$D$ of decisions, at most one value per epoch and slot, each certified for
that epoch and slot. Outputs are
ordered by
\begin{equation}
  (E,V,D)\OutputOrd(E',V',D')
  \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
  E\preceq_{\rm pref}E',\quad V\subseteq V',\quad D\subseteq D',
  \label{eq:authority-refinement}
\end{equation}
where $E\preceq_{\rm pref}E'$ means that $E$ is a prefix of $E'$. Protocol safety guarantees that every output permitted at the current or any
later input \outrel the certified evidence already emitted. So an authority
change or a certified decision is a legal output and needs no further
\commitment. This covers following the succession rule, not choosing
values: competing proposals for an undecided slot have incompatible extensions
of $D$, so certifying one is a \commitment. If the succession rule were not
fixed, choosing among competing next epochs would require a \commitment as
well.

\subsection{$k$-set agreement: no $k$-wise test suffices}

Fix $k\geq1$, distinct participants $p_1,\ldots,p_{k+1}$ in the participant set $\mathcal N$
(possibly among others), and distinct values $v_1,\ldots,v_{k+1}$ in a value
set $\mathit{Val}$. An input is a finite set of proposals $(p,v)$, at most one
per participant, ordered by inclusion; the \emph{proposal input} of $p_r$ is
$\{(p_r,v_r)\}$. Outputs are partial decision maps
$m:\mathcal N\rightharpoonup\mathit{Val}$ ordered by extension. At input $I$, $m$ is
permitted when it decides only participants that propose in $I$, every value
in its range is proposed in $I$ (validity), and its range has at most $k$
values. At $p_r$'s proposal input, $\{p_r\mapsto v_r\}$ is permitted, and the
union of the proposal inputs is a common \indesc. Any $k$ of these $k+1$
rows have a common \outnoun there; all $k+1$ do not, since it would have
$k+1$ values. So the common-extension condition fails and the spec
is not monotone (Proposition~\ref{prop:common-expansion-form}); a partition
separating the $p_r$ gives the negative side of Complete CAP. Consensus is
$k=1$. Classical impossibility results additionally depend on the
computation, failure, and progress model~\cite{saks2000set}.

\subsection{Tight renaming}

Inputs are finite sets $N$ of participants, ordered by inclusion. Outputs are
finite partial naming maps ordered by extension, and the outputs at $N$ are
the injective maps
\begin{equation}
  m:N\longrightarrow\{1,\ldots,|N|\}.
  \label{eq:tight-renaming}
\end{equation}
At $\{p\}$ the only output is $p\mapsto1$, and at $\{q\}$ it is $q\mapsto1$. Each has an \outnoun at $\{p,q\}$, so Relational Monotonicity does not fail
here, but no injective map extends both. The spec also lacks directed
fibers at
$\{p,q\}$: $\{p\mapsto1,q\mapsto2\}$ and $\{p\mapsto2,q\mapsto1\}$ have no
common \outnoun. The spec therefore fails Lifted Monotonicity, and
by Theorem~\ref{thm:complete-cap} with $E=P$ and Corollary~\ref{cor:complete-cap-any-system} a partition separating $p$ and $q$ rules out
Availability with Partition Consistency. The renaming hierarchy adds
wait-free and topological
conclusions~\cite{herlihy1991waitfree,herlihy1999topological}.

\subsection{Snapshots}

Inputs are finite causal histories, that is, finite sets of environment events with a
causal partial order; $h\InputOrd h'$ when $h$ is a downward-closed subset of
$h'$ carrying the restricted order. The \emph{prefix} spec permits
at $h$ any downward-closed subset of $h$, with outputs ordered by inclusion. Old prefixes stay legal as history grows, and finite unions of prefixes are
prefixes, so it satisfies Lifted Monotonicity; it guarantees neither freshness
nor completeness. A spec that requires the complete history, with
output $h$ at $h$ and outputs ordered by equality, is functional and not
monotone: its required output changes whenever an environment event arrives. By
Proposition~\ref{prop:functional-exposure-boundary}, some output can then be
emitted only after a \commitment that rules out larger inputs.
