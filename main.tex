\title{Complete CALM: A Unified Criterion for Coordination}

\author{Joseph M. Hellerstein}
\affiliation{%
  \institution{UC Berkeley \& Amazon Web Services}
  \city{Berkeley, CA}
  \country{USA}
}

\begin{abstract}
When does a distributed system intrinsically need coordination?  Too little
coordination produces heisenbugs; too much adds latency and sacrifices
availability.  CALM, CAP, and CRDTs each answer the question for one class of
programs, consistency models, or design patterns, in formalisms that cannot be
compared or combined, which leaves developers guessing.

We put them on common ground, in a formalism that clarifies the literature and
offers new results.  A minimalist \spectype is a possibility table listing, for
each input, every output a system may produce, with partial orders on inputs and
outputs.  It is \emph{consistent} when all its runs are confluent.
\emph{Functional CALM} generalizes the CALM Theorem from Datalog to any function
between ordered domains: consistency is monotonicity.  \emph{Complete CALM}
extends this to nondeterministic specifications, where the test is
\emph{Lifted Monotonicity}, and \emph{Complete CAP} follows as a corollary.
Highly Available Transactions, Invariant Confluence, and later refinements of
CALM map into the same framework.

Prior CALM results stop at the monotone boundary: for non-monotone
specifications, they say only that coordination is needed somewhere.  Our
unified framework offers constructive results on both sides: it says exactly
how much coordination a specification needs, and where.  A non-monotone
specification's outputs determine the \commitments required for consistency,
enforceable by coordination mechanisms like locks, atomics, or consensus.
\emph{Proper Coordination} identifies which stage of a composition must make the
\commitments and certifies that later stages need none, validating designs such
as consensus logs in front of a replicated store.
\end{abstract}
