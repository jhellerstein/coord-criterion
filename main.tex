\title{Complete CALM: A Unified Criterion for Coordination}

\author{Joseph M. Hellerstein}
\affiliation{%
  \institution{UC Berkeley \& Amazon Web Services}
  \city{Berkeley, CA}
  \country{USA}
}

\begin{abstract}
When does a system inherently require coordination?  CAP, CALM, CRDTs, Highly
Available Transactions, and invariant confluence each draw a coordination
boundary in a different mathematical language, and conventional wisdom has
drifted from what the literature proves.  We give one semantic framework for
all of them: a specification is a table of admissible input--output pairs with
ordered columns, and the state of a computation is the \emph{residual} of that
table---the rows still admissible.
% Residuals are provably the semantic
% quotient of Lamport histories.
\emph{Functional CALM} generalizes the
original CALM Theorem, replacing Datalog queries with arbitrary functions
between partially ordered input and output domains: monotonicity
characterizes exactly when output can be exposed consistently as distributed
input arrives.
\emph{Complete CALM} extends this to \emph{ambiguous} specifications that admit several outputs
for one input, as leader election and transaction ordering do.  Complete CALM
shows that the Lamport histories of a system implementing such a
specification are consistent, in the sense of confluence---participants that
have exposed outputs on different inputs can always be brought to a common
state---exactly when the specification satisfies \emph{Lifted Monotonicity},
monotonicity under an order lifted from outputs to sets of outputs.  Where Lifted Monotonicity
fails, progress requires an irrevocable \emph{commitment}, and only
commitments require coordination.  Complete CALM yields a Complete CAP
Theorem that extends classical CAP from a one-way impossibility about
linearizability to a dichotomy parameterized by the declared specification
and the deployment.  The remaining sections place CRDTs, Highly Available
Transactions, invariant confluence, and classical distributed  agreement problems in this
framework as instances of the two theorems.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Coordination occurs when one participant must wait for decisions or agreement
from another before acting.  The wait adds latency and can destroy availability
when the needed communication crosses a network partition.  Avoiding
unnecessary coordination is therefore a central goal in distributed systems
and databases.  This paper asks which specifications intrinsically require it.

The CALM Conjecture proposed a crisp, bidirectional answer: consistency can be
achieved without coordination exactly when the computation is
monotone~\cite{hellerstein2010declarative}.  Ameloot et al.\ proved it, in
both directions, for Datalog queries evaluated by networks of relational
transducers~\cite{ameloot2013relational,hellerstein2020keeping}.  The theorem
is therefore tied to one formalism. The intuition behind the CALM Theorem is often
invoked in broader contexts, leaving a gap between folklore and what was proven.

Several other traditions draw closely related boundaries, each in its own
formalism.  The CAP Theorem proves an impossibility for available linearizable registers
under partition~\cite{brewer2000towards,gilbert2002cap}.  CRDTs give
a strong sufficient discipline for coordination-free convergence using
join-semilattices and inflationary updates~\cite{shapiro2011crdt}.  Highly
Available Transactions (HATs) classify transaction guarantees that survive
partitions~\cite{bailis2013hat}.  Invariant confluence gives a necessary and
sufficient condition for independently executed transactions to merge without
breaking an application invariant~\cite{bailis2014coordination}.

The results plainly share an idea, but their mathematical frameworks do not
compose: logic programs and transducers, register executions, semilattices,
transaction histories, and invariant-preserving database states rest on
different objects and assumptions.  This makes simple cross-cutting questions
awkward.  Does CALM encompass CAP?  Are CRDT join laws necessary for
coordination freedom, or one strong sufficient discipline?  Does convergent
storage provide highly available transactions?  Do convergence and transaction
availability preserve application invariants?  Section~\ref{sec:cap-corollary}
answers the first question; the CRDT, HAT, and invariant-confluence parts of
Section~\ref{sec:instances} answer the others.

We begin by introducing a general notion of specification, agnostic to any
language or implementation details.  It is consistent with state-machine-style
specifications and can be compiled from them
(Section~\ref{sec:complete-calm}); what it elides is the incidental detail of
execution order, keeping only what the specification can ask about.  A
\emph{functional semantic specification}
(\S\ref{sec:functional-specifications}) is an arbitrary function
\(f:(I,\InputOrd)\to(O,\OutputOrd)\) between a partially ordered input domain
and a separately partially ordered output domain, monotone in the standard
sense when \(i\InputOrd j\) implies \(f(i)\OutputOrd f(j)\).  Distributed
implementations make input arrival uncertain: participants hold different
input refinements, received in different orders and batches.  Monotonicity
says that output exposed from any one of them remains valid as more input
arrives; without it, exposing an output first requires sealing off the inputs
that could contradict it.

Our first step, \emph{Functional CALM} (\S\ref{sec:functional-calm-theorem}),
generalizes the CALM result from relational logic languages and fact inclusion
to these arbitrary functions and refinement partial orders, giving a common
language for coordination-freedom results over functional specifications,
including query evaluation and the usual CRDT state interface.  ``Functional''
means only that each semantic input has one specified output; execution may
be as nondeterministic as it likes.

Our second step addresses a case those interfaces do not raise: the semantic
specification itself may be \emph{ambiguous}---formally
allowing several distinguishable outputs for one input.  Leader election may
allow any participant to be eligible; a database may permit several serial
schedules.  The specification is then a relation \(S\subseteq I\times O\)
(\S\ref{sec:relational-specifications}), and the \emph{fiber}
\(\fiber{i}=\{o:(i,o)\in S\}\) is the set of outputs admitted at input \(i\).

Moving from a function to a relation, the question is still monotonicity, now
of the sets of admitted outputs: \(\fiber{i}\) refines to \(\fiber{j}\) when
every finite set of outputs admitted at \(i\) lies below a single output
admitted at \(j\).  Monotonicity of the fiber map under this lift is
\emph{Lifted Monotonicity} (\S\ref{sec:complete-calm-criterion}).  It asks
both that outputs keep growing as input grows and that the alternatives
admitted at one input can coexist.  Where it fails, progress must rule out an
alternative, a step we call a \emph{commitment}
(Definition~\ref{def:transition-system}), and participants who could commit
incompatibly must coordinate first.  Complete CALM shows that Lifted
Monotonicity is exactly confluence of the system's Lamport histories: whatever
outputs participants expose, in whatever order input arrives, they can always be
brought to a common state at any input combining theirs
(\S\ref{sec:complete-calm-theorem}).

We take this as what consistency should mean for a distributed system: not a
particular consistency model, but confluence of whatever the specification
admits.  The acronym then reads \emph{Consistency As Lifted Monotonicity}, and
the original CALM Theorem is its functional case over relations and queries.

Complete CALM also yields a Complete CAP Theorem
(\S\ref{sec:cap-corollary}).  Classical CAP gives an
impossibility for linearizable registers under partition.
The Complete CAP Theorem extends from impossibility to dichotomy, characterizing
both sides of the boundary: specifications that are subject to CAP tradeoffs and those that are not.
Linearizability becomes a special case on the tradeoff side.

This paper makes four contributions:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item A semantic framework for distributed computation: specifications as
        relations with ordered inputs and outputs, \emph{residual states}
        recording what remains admissible, and three transitions: input events,
        output events, and \emph{commitments}.  Residuals are provably the semantic
        quotient of Lamport histories, and Section~\ref{sec:instances} shows
        that CRDTs, highly available transactions, and invariant confluence are
        instances of the framework.
  \item Functional CALM generalizes and proves the CALM Conjecture in the context of
        arbitrary functions, represented as semantic input/output specifications
        with separate input- and output-refinement partial orders.
  \item Complete CALM extends the model to semantic output choice, formalizes
        output events and commitments, and proves that three conditions coincide:
        Lifted Monotonicity of the specification, confluence of the runs of
        the framework, and confluence of every Lamport history of the system.
        A monotone specification therefore needs no coordination, since
        exposing outputs alone keeps its histories confluent; where monotonicity
        fails, progress requires a commitment, and commitments are shown to
        require coordination.  In partition form
        this is the Complete CAP Theorem,
        which extends the classical impossibility to a bidirectional dichotomy
        parameterized by the declared specification and the
        deployment.
  \item Two frontier theorems (Appendix~\ref{sec:frontiers}) characterize how
        far an interface can be strengthened before coordination becomes
        unavoidable: which earlier outputs a monotone interface must preserve,
        and when constraints across independently acting participants create a
        CAP tradeoff.
\end{enumerate}

Section~\ref{sec:functional-calm} introduces the framework on an election
and develops Functional CALM.  Section~\ref{sec:complete-calm} introduces
semantic choice on two further examples and develops commitment, Complete
CALM, and Complete CAP.  Section~\ref{sec:instances} applies the
results to familiar systems, and Section~\ref{sec:discussion} draws out the
design lessons and scope limits.  Appendix~\ref{sec:frontiers} derives
maximal coordination-free interfaces, and
Appendix~\ref{app:additional-applications} gives further classical
applications.

\section{Functional CALM}
\label{sec:functional-calm}

The CALM Theorem was proved for queries in a logic programming language,
evaluated by relational transducers, and its applicability outside that
setting was never made precise.  Here we use a simpler model that applies to
any specification.  A semantic specification describes allowed input/output
behavior without committing to a language, placement, protocol, or message
schedule.  This section addresses the case that prior work on both CALM and
CRDTs shared: specifications that are functions, in which each
input has a single specified output.  Section~\ref{sec:election-story} walks
through the framework informally on an election.
Section~\ref{sec:functional-specifications} defines functional specifications
and monotonicity, Section~\ref{sec:determination} defines the transition
system that tracks what a run has settled, and
Section~\ref{sec:functional-calm-theorem} proves Functional CALM.

\subsection{The framework on an election}
\label{sec:election-story}

We begin with an informal, example-driven treatment.
Terms introduced in italics are defined formally in the rest of this section
and in Section~\ref{sec:complete-calm}; italics also mark the occasional
standard term borrowed from database theory.  Nothing here is needed for the
proofs, but every notion the Functional CALM theorem rests on---residual
states, input events, retirement, commitments, upper fibers, determination,
output events, runs, and consistency---is exercised on the example.

Two mathematical objects are in play.  The first is a \emph{semantic
specification} $\Spec=(I,\InputOrd,O,\OutputOrd,S)$: a table
$S\subseteq I\times O$ of admissible input--output pairs, with a partial order
on each column.  The specification stands alone, and the theorems are about
it.  The second is needed to model many systems faithfully: a
\emph{commitment basis} $\Phi=\{p_1,p_2,\ldots\}$ of partial functions on
the states defined below, the irrevocable acts the system is permitted to
take on its own authority.  The election introduces the specification first;
the commitment basis first appears when we want to allow the registrar to close the polls.

\paragraph{A specification is a table.}
A specification is a table of input--output pairs, listing the outputs
admissible for each input; when the input column determines the output
column, as for squaring with rows $(2,4)$, $(3,9)$, $(4,16),\ldots$, the
specification is \emph{functional}.  Everything that follows is a matter of
removing rows from a specification, and for now ``the system'' is a single
actor; who does what in a distributed implementation comes in
Section~\ref{sec:complete-calm}.

Figure~\ref{fig:election-frames}(a) is the specification of a small election.
Three voters $v_1,v_2,v_3$ each cast at most one immutable ballot for $a$ or
$b$; the result is whichever candidate has two ballots, and $\bot$ (no result
yet) until one does.  An input represents the
ballots seen so far, written as a three-letter string whose $k$th position holds
$v_k$'s ballot or a dot if it has not arrived; only rows above $a\cdot\cdot$,
where $v_1$ has voted $a$, are shown.  The first row says that with $v_1$'s
ballot alone the result is $\bot$; row $aa\cdot$ says that once $v_2$ also votes
$a$ the result is $a$; row $abb$ says that if $v_2$ and $v_3$ both vote $b$ the
result is $b$.

The rows are only part of the specification.  Each column also carries a
partial order, and both orders are design decisions.  The \emph{input order}
says what counts as more input: here, more ballots, so $ab\cdot$ \emph{refines}
$a\cdot\cdot$, while $aa\cdot$ and $ab\cdot$ are incomparable.  The \emph{output
order} says when a later output keeps every promise an earlier one made: here
$\bot$ lies below both $a$ and $b$, so ``no result yet'' can eventually refine
to a winner, but $a$ and $b$ are incomparable, so neither winner refines the
other.  A
different design could make all outputs incomparable, so that every result is
final; we return to that choice below.  Given these orders, one can follow any input
upward through its refinements, say $a\cdot\cdot$ to $ab\cdot$ to $aba$: the
output climbs too, from $\bot$ to $a$, and never steps down or sideways.  That
is what it means for the \emph{specification} to be \emph{monotone}, a property
of the rows together with the orders.

\paragraph{Input arrives.}
Now $v_2$'s ballot arrives, for $b$; panel (b) shows the result.  The
\emph{current input}, the ballots seen so far, has advanced from $a\cdot\cdot$
to $ab\cdot$, shown in bold.  What an input event does to the specification
is governed by one rule: only the rows at the current input and its
refinements remain, and every other row is \emph{retired}.  The specification
with the retired rows removed is the \emph{residual specification}. A \emph{residual state}
is a pair containing a residual specification and the current input: the account of
what is still possible and of how far input has come.  Each panel shows one
residual state, the residual specification as its rows and the current input
in bold.  The inputs that still have rows are the \emph{valid} ones.
The environment produces input events; the system does not choose them, and
the residual state depends only on the input reached, not on the order or
grouping in which the ballots arrived.  In panel (b) the input has grown
from $a\cdot\cdot$ to $ab\cdot$, so the row at $a\cdot\cdot$ is retired; the
rows at $aa\cdot$, $aaa$, and $aab$ are retired because no input refines both
$aa\cdot$ and $ab\cdot$.  The retired output $\bot$ at $a\cdot\cdot$ is
refined by the $\bot$ at $ab\cdot$, so growing the input lost nothing the
smaller input's output claimed; this is a consequence of the monotonicity
observed above.

\paragraph{What could still happen.}
While input may still grow, what matters is not only the current row but what
any input that could still arrive might require.  The future of the election
is its current input together with its valid input refinements, and the outputs of
those rows form the current input's \emph{upper fiber}.  In
panel (b) those rows are the current row and the two refinements $aba$ and
$abb$ in which $v_3$ votes, so the upper fiber, shaded, is $\{\bot,a,b\}$:
either candidate could still win.

Panel (c) shows the alternative in which $v_2$ voted $a$.  The rows at or
above $aa\cdot$ all say $a$, so the upper fiber is the singleton $\{a\}$ and
the current input is \emph{determined}: it fixes the output regardless of what
ballot $v_3$ could cast.  Narrowing the upper fiber until a single output
remains is what we call \emph{determination}.  Here input arrival determined the residual
on its own; below we meet the case where the system must act.

\paragraph{Commitments.}
Return to panel (b) and let the registrar close the polls: $v_3$'s ballot,
should it arrive, will not be counted.  Panel (d) shows the result.  The rows
at $aba$ and $abb$ are removed although no ballot arrived and the current
input has not changed: those inputs have been rendered invalid.  This is a
\emph{commitment}, specifically an \emph{input-sealing commitment}, and it is
an act of the system rather than of the environment.  It
irrevocably disallows refinements the original specification admitted.
Nothing in the specification says whether the registrar may do this.  That is
part of the second mathematical object mentioned above: the commitment basis.
A faithful model of an election whose polls can close
includes, alongside $\Spec$, a commitment basis $\Phi$ containing this act,
and a model of one whose polls cannot close omits it.  Only the system may
perform a commitment, and only one in $\Phi$.  Afterward no refinement of
$ab\cdot$ remains valid, and its upper fiber has collapsed to $\{\bot\}$, so
it too is determined.
For a functional specification, input-sealing commitments like this one are the only kind
of commitment there is.  The next section considers additional commitments that arise
when an input can be associated with many outputs.

\paragraph{Output events.}
An \emph{output event} \emph{exposes} a result $o$ to a client, a different
act from making a commitment.  It promises that the final result will refine
$o$, and removes every row whose output does not refine $o$.  The output $o$
is \emph{exposable} when every row the event removes is refined by a
remaining one: nothing claimed is lost.  For a functional specification, where
each input has one row, that means the event removes no row at all, which is
the case exactly when $o$ is smaller than everything in the upper fiber: every
input that may still arrive then has an output larger than $o$.  Read the footers of Figure~\ref{fig:election-frames}:
$\bot$ is exposable in every panel, because every shaded output refines it,
and $a$ is exposable in panel (c).

\paragraph{When monotonicity fails.}
Now change one thing: make all outputs incomparable, dropping
$\bot\OutputOrd a,b$, so that an output refines only itself; this is the
\emph{equality order}.  The rows are the same, but in panels (a) and (b)
nothing is exposable: $\bot$ no longer lies below the $a$ and $b$ in the upper
fiber, so exposing it would remove every row whose output is $a$ or $b$, and
with them every input at which a result is reached.  To expose anything, the
registrar must first make an input-sealing commitment.
Figure~\ref{fig:nonmonotone-frame} shows the result of closing the polls at
$a\cdot\cdot$: every refinement is removed by commitment, the upper fiber
collapses to $\{\bot\}$, and only now is $\bot$ exposable.  That contrast is
Functional CALM (Section~\ref{sec:functional-calm}): a monotone functional
specification can expose outputs as input arrives; a non-monotone one must
first make a commitment.

\paragraph{A semantic transition system.}
We now have all the moves.  Residual states are the states of a transition
system, and its transitions are of three kinds: input events, made by the
environment, and output events and commitments, made by the system.  Every panel
of Figures~\ref{fig:election-frames} and~\ref{fig:nonmonotone-frame} is a
state, and panels (b) through (e) are reached from (a) by one or two
transitions.  A \emph{run} is a sequence of input and output events from a
starting input; it makes no commitment beyond what its output events remove.

\paragraph{Consistency.}
Two residual states reached by runs are \emph{confluent} when, at every input
$k$ refining both of their current inputs, a single residual state with
current input $k$ is reachable from both: each is advanced to $k$ by an input
event and then, by exposing an output there, to the same state.  The
environment, not the system, decides which input $k$ arrives, so the two
states must be able to meet at any of them.  In the election, runs with
current inputs
$a\cdot\cdot$ and $ab\cdot$ have each exposed $\bot$; advancing both to $aba$
and exposing $a$ brings them to the same state.  Under the equality order, a
run that exposes $\bot$ at $a\cdot\cdot$---an output that is not exposable
there---would remove the row at $aba$, so no input event could advance it to
$aba$, and it could never meet the run that received $v_2$'s ballot there:
the two states would not be confluent.
Confluence of runs is what the CALM literature means by consistency, and we
take it as the definition: a specification is \emph{consistent} when its runs
are confluent.  Functional CALM, proved below, says that a functional
specification is consistent exactly when it is monotone.

Figure~\ref{fig:formalism-summary} collects the formal objects of this section

\subsection{Functional specifications and monotonicity}
\label{sec:functional-specifications}

\begin{definition}[Functional ordered specification]
\label{def:functional-specification}
A \emph{functional ordered specification} is a function
        \begin{equation}
  f:(I,\InputOrd)\longrightarrow(O,\OutputOrd),
  \label{eq:functional-specification}
        \end{equation}
where $(I,\InputOrd)$ and $(O,\OutputOrd)$ are partially ordered sets.  We call
$\InputOrd$ the \emph{input-refinement order}: $i\InputOrd j$ means that $j$
contains at least the input information in $i$.  We call $\OutputOrd$ the
\emph{output-refinement order}: $o\OutputOrd u$ means that $u$ preserves every
externally visible claim in $o$ and may add more.
\end{definition}

These are two separate partial orders.  The input order compares inputs; the
output order compares outputs.  Neither orders executions or rows in a table.
Either may be the \emph{equality order}, under which an element refines only
itself; on outputs this is the case in which no output can be extended, only
replaced.

\begin{definition}[Monotonicity]
\label{def:functional-monotonicity}
A functional ordered specification $f$ is \emph{monotone} if
        \begin{equation}
  i\InputOrd j
  \quad\Longrightarrow\quad
  f(i)\OutputOrd f(j).
  \label{eq:functional-monotonicity}
        \end{equation}
\end{definition}

This is the standard mathematical definition of a monotone function.  In
systems terms, an output exposed from partial input remains valid as more input
arrives.

\subsection{A semantic transition system}
\label{sec:determination}

A function gives one output for each input, but a refined input may require a
different output.  Before an execution can say anything binding, we need to
keep track of which input--output possibilities remain, which events remove
them, and which of those removals are acts of choice.  We do this with a
transition system, defined in full below, whose states are residual states and
whose transitions are of three kinds: input events, output events, and
commitments.  The definitions follow the vocabulary of
Section~\ref{sec:election-story} and the panels of
Figure~\ref{fig:election-frames}.  Choice commitments and output events in the
presence of output choice are defined here but discussed in
Section~\ref{sec:complete-calm}, where relational specifications make them
necessary.

\paragraph{The specification as a table.}
View the specification as a table $S\subseteq I\times O$ whose rows are the
pairs $(i,f(i))$.  For any subrelation $R\subseteq S$ and input $i$, the
\emph{fiber} of $R$ at $i$ is the set of outputs paired with $i$:
        \begin{equation}
  R(i)=\{o\in O:(i,o)\in R\}.
  \label{eq:fiber}
        \end{equation}
For the full specification this is $S(i)$, which for a function is $\{f(i)\}$.
Execution never adds a row.  Everything that follows is a story about which
rows have been removed from the table, and by what.

Inputs are \emph{cumulative}: an input is everything seen so far, and
$i\InputOrd j$ means that $j$ contains at least the information in $i$.  The
specification sees inputs only through the domain, its order, and the outputs
it admits for each; corrections, retractions, or conflicting ballots are
accommodated by designing the input domain to record them.  Write
$k\prec_I i$ when $k\InputOrd i$ and $k\neq i$, and
$\mathord\uparrow_I i=\{j\in I:i\InputOrd j\}$ for the refinements of $i$.

\paragraph{States.}
The states of our transition system are residuals of the initial specification and input.
Every panel of Figure~\ref{fig:election-frames} is such a state: the specification
with some rows removed, one input current.  In panel (b) the current input is
$ab\cdot$ and every row whose input does not refine it has been removed; such
rows are \emph{retired}.  A residual state obeys
the following conditions.

\begin{definition}[Residual state]
\label{def:residual-state}
A \emph{residual state} is a pair $\sigma=(i,R)$ such that
\begin{enumerate}
  \item $i\in I$ is the \emph{current input};
  \item $R\subseteq S$;
  \item the current input has a row in $R$:
        \begin{equation}
          R(i)\neq\varnothing;
          \label{eq:current-input-validity}
        \end{equation}
  \item only refinements of the current input have rows in $R$:
        \begin{equation}
          R(k)=\varnothing
          \qquad\text{whenever }i\not\InputOrd k.
          \label{eq:retired-input-absence}
        \end{equation}
\end{enumerate}
$R$ is the \emph{residual specification}.  An input $k$ is \emph{valid} when
$R(k)\neq\varnothing$, and otherwise \emph{invalid}.
\end{definition}

Valid inputs other than the current one are the potential futures:
refinements of $i$ whose rows have not been removed.

For an input $i$, the \emph{canonical initial state} is
        \begin{equation}
  \sigma_0(i)=\bigl(i,\ \{(k,o)\in S:\ i\InputOrd k\}\bigr),
  \label{eq:canonical-initial-state}
        \end{equation}
the specification restricted to $i$ and its refinements.
Figure~\ref{fig:election-frames}(a) is $\sigma_0(a\cdot\cdot)$.

\begin{definition}[Semantic transition system]
\label{def:transition-system}
The \emph{semantic transition system} of a specification has the residual
states as its states.  Its transitions are partial functions on residual
states of exactly three kinds.  Each is defined at a state only when the result
satisfies Definition~\ref{def:residual-state}, and each removes rows and adds
none.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \emph{Input event.}  For $j\in I$ with $i\InputOrd j$, the
        event $\mathsf{input}(j)$, produced by the environment, makes $j$ the
        current input and retires every row whose input does not refine $j$:
        \begin{equation}
          (i,R)\xrightarrow{\mathsf{input}(j)}
          \bigl(j,\ R\mathbin{\upharpoonright_I}j\bigr),
          \qquad
          R\mathbin{\upharpoonright_I}j=\{(k,u)\in R:\ j\InputOrd k\},
          \label{eq:input-event}
        \end{equation}
        defined when $R(j)\neq\varnothing$.
  \item \emph{Output event.}  For $o\in R(i)$, the event
        $\mathsf{output}(o)$, made by the system, exposes $o$: it keeps the
        current input fixed and removes every row whose output does not
        refine $o$,
        \begin{equation}
          (i,R)\xrightarrow{\mathsf{output}(o)}
          \bigl(i,\ R\mathbin{\upharpoonright_O}o\bigr),
          \qquad
          R\mathbin{\upharpoonright_O}o=\{(k,u)\in R:\ o\OutputOrd u\},
          \label{eq:residual-refining-fibers}
        \end{equation}
        always defined, since $o$ itself remains at $i$.
  \item \emph{Commitment.}  A commitment $p$, made by the system, keeps the
        current input fixed and strictly restricts the residual, $p(i,R)=(i,R')$
        with $R'\subsetneq R$, removing at least one row $(k,x)$ such that no
        retained output at $k$ refines $x$.  It is an \emph{input-sealing
        commitment} if it
        makes some valid input invalid, $R'(k)=\varnothing$ for some $k$ with
        $R(k)\neq\varnothing$ (necessarily $k\neq i$), and a \emph{choice
        commitment} otherwise, when every valid input remains valid.  The
        system's \emph{commitment basis} is the set of commitments it may use.
        A commitment is \emph{applicable} at $\sigma$ when $p(\sigma)$ is
        defined.
\end{enumerate}
A residual state is \emph{reachable} when it is obtained from some canonical
initial state by a finite sequence of these transitions.
\end{definition}

Nothing else changes a residual state; computation may inform which
transition the system takes, but is not itself a transition.  Every transition
removes rows, and taken row by row a removal is one of three things.

\begin{definition}[Retirement]
\label{def:retirement}
Let a transition take $(i,R)$ to $(i',R')$ and remove the row $(k,x)$.  The
removal is an \emph{input retirement} if $k$ does not refine $i'$: either $k$
lies strictly below $i'$, as the previous current input $i$ does, and has been
superseded, or $k$ is incomparable with $i'$ and can no longer be reached.  It
is an \emph{output retirement} if $k$ remains valid and $x$ is \emph{covered},
that is, some retained $u\in R'(k)$ has $x\OutputOrd u$.  Otherwise the row is
\emph{removed by commitment}: a still-valid input, or an output that no
retained output covers, has been excluded.
\end{definition}

Retirements are the gray rows of the figures and removals by commitment the
struck rows.  Input events perform input retirements and nothing else, since
they leave surviving fibers unchanged.  An input retirement need not be
covered: when $f$ is not monotone, the next input may retire a row whose
output nothing refines, but the environment made that input unreachable.
What distinguishes a removal by commitment is that the system excluded
something no event had ruled out.  An output event keeps the current input, so
it performs no input retirement; what it removes is either covered or not.

\begin{definition}[Exposable]
\label{def:exposable}
An output $o\in R(i)$ is \emph{exposable} at $(i,R)$ when every row that
$\mathsf{output}(o)$ removes is an output retirement.
\end{definition}

An output event whose output is not exposable removes a row by commitment.
A system may expose only exposable outputs: when $o$ is not exposable, the
system must first apply a commitment from its basis that removes the
alternatives, after which $o$ may be exposable.  For a functional specification every fiber
is a singleton, so a removed row is never covered: $f(i)$ is exposable at
$(i,R)$ exactly when exposing it removes no row, that is, when
$f(i)\OutputOrd f(j)$ for every valid $j\succeq_I i$.

\paragraph{Input events.}
Input events compose: $\mathsf{input}(j)$ followed by $\mathsf{input}(k)$ has
the same effect as $\mathsf{input}(k)$ alone, since
$\mathord\uparrow_I k\subseteq\mathord\uparrow_I j$, and $\mathsf{input}(i)$
at $(i,R)$ is the identity.  Arrival order, batching, and repeated delivery
therefore do not affect the state reached by input events alone; only the
input reached matters.  Panel (b) is reached from panel (a) by
$\mathsf{input}(ab\cdot)$ however $v_2$'s ballot was delivered.

\paragraph{Determination.}
The shaded outputs in Figure~\ref{fig:election-frames} are those that the
current input or any continuation of it could still require.  For
$R\subseteq S$ and an input $i$, the \emph{upper fiber}
        \begin{equation}
  R^\uparrow(i)=\{o\in O:\exists j\in I,\ i\InputOrd j\text{ and }(j,o)\in R\}
  \label{eq:upper-fiber}
        \end{equation}
collects the outputs admitted at $i$ or at any still-valid refinement of $i$.

\begin{definition}[Determined input and determined state]
\label{def:determination}
The current input $i$ of $(i,R)$ is \emph{determined} when its upper fiber is a
singleton:
        \begin{equation}
  |R^\uparrow(i)|=1.
  \label{eq:determined-input}
        \end{equation}
A residual state is \emph{determined} when its current input is determined.
\end{definition}

Determinedness is stronger than having one row at $i$: every fiber in the
election is a singleton, yet panels (a) and (b) are undetermined because valid
refinements disagree.  \emph{Determination} is also the name for the process
by which a residual becomes determined, by input events alone as in panel (c)
or, when they do not suffice, by commitments.

\paragraph{Input-sealing commitments.}
Figure~\ref{fig:election-frames}(d) removes the rows at $aba$ and $abb$ with
the current input fixed.  No input event does this; it is an input-sealing
commitment, an act of the system, and for a functional specification it is the
only kind of commitment that can ever be needed, since every fiber has one row
and there is never an output to choose among.  Once no strict refinement of
the current input is valid, $R^\uparrow(i)=R(i)$, which for a functional
specification is a singleton: the state is determined.
Whether an input-sealing commitment is available is part of the system model,
not of the specification: it must be in the commitment basis.  A system that
must still accept a late ballot, with a rejection or other fallback response,
keeps that row and has no such commitment to apply.  A commitment records the
binding act, not the computation that selected it.

\paragraph{Consistency.}
The CALM literature's consistency is confluence: the outcome of a computation
is the same whatever the order in which input arrives and whatever outputs
were exposed along the way.  We state it as confluence of runs of the transition system,
for any specification table $S\subseteq I\times O$, functional or not.

\begin{definition}[Run]
\label{def:run}
A \emph{run} is a finite sequence of input and output events from a canonical
initial state, using no commitment from the basis.
\end{definition}

A run is a path in the transition system as defined, in which
$\mathsf{output}(o)$ is available for every $o\in R(i)$; which of its output
events a system may perform is taken up in
Section~\ref{sec:functional-calm-theorem}.


\begin{lemma}[Residual of a run]
\label{lem:run-residual}
A run from $\sigma_0(i_0)$ whose final current input is $i$ and whose
output events are $\mathsf{output}(o_1),\ldots,\mathsf{output}(o_m)$ ends at
        \begin{equation}
  \bigl(i,\ \{(k,x)\in S:\ i\InputOrd k\ \text{and}\ o_\ell\OutputOrd x
  \text{ for every }\ell\}\bigr).
  \label{eq:run-residual}
        \end{equation}
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Each input event intersects $R$ with $\{(k,x):j\InputOrd k\}$ and each
output event with $\{(k,x):o\OutputOrd x\}$; intersections commute, and the input
restrictions compose to the restriction at the final input $i$.
\end{proof}

\begin{definition}[Consistency]
\label{def:consistency}
An ordered specification is \emph{consistent} when its runs are confluent:
for every finite family of runs with final states $(i_1,R_1),\ldots,(i_k,R_k)$
and every \emph{common refinement} $j$ of $i_1,\ldots,i_k$, an input with
$i_r\InputOrd j$ for every $r$, a single state with current input $j$ is
reachable from every $(i_r,R_r)$ by input and output events; the runs are
then \emph{confluent at} $j$.
\end{definition}

\begin{proposition}[Confluence at a common refinement]
\label{prop:confluence}
Runs with final states $(i_1,R_1),\ldots,(i_k,R_k)$ reach a common state with
current input $j$ if and only if
        \begin{equation}
  \bigcap_{r=1}^{k}R_r(j)\neq\varnothing,
  \label{eq:reconciliation}
        \end{equation}
and then, for any $u$ in the intersection, $\mathsf{input}(j)$ followed by
$\mathsf{output}(u)$ takes every $(i_r,R_r)$ to
$\bigl(j,\{(k,x)\in S: j\InputOrd k,\ u\OutputOrd x\}\bigr)$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
A common state with current input $j$ has a nonempty fiber at $j$, and its
rows belong to every $R_r$.  Conversely let $u$ be in the intersection.  Then
$\mathsf{input}(j)$ is defined at every $(i_r,R_r)$, and
by Lemma~\ref{lem:run-residual} $u\in R_r(j)$ says that every output exposed
on run $r$ lies below $u$, so after $\mathsf{output}(u)$ the same lemma
leaves exactly the rows of $S$ above $j$ and $u$, whichever run we started
from.
\end{proof}

Confluence is asked at every common refinement of the final inputs, not merely at some
later input, and families of one run are included: a run must remain able to
advance to every refinement of its current input, which fails exactly when one
of its output events has emptied a fiber there.  The runs need
not have exposed the same outputs; whatever outputs they exposed, in whatever
order input arrived, one output admitted at the combined input refines all of it,
so the residual at $j$ is a function of $j$ and not of the runs.  The
definition is semantic: it describes no protocol and promises no delivery,
liveness, or termination detection.

\subsection{The Functional CALM theorem}
\label{sec:functional-calm-theorem}

Both CALM theorems are proved in the same way, and the only-if direction of
each uses a device that should be named before it is used.  Call an output
event \emph{unsafe} when its output is not exposable at the state where the
event occurs, and a run \emph{unsafe} when it contains one.  Unsafe does not
mean undefined: the event is a transition of the system of
Definition~\ref{def:transition-system}.  It means that the event removes a row
by commitment, so a system may not perform it as an output event; where its
basis permits, the system first makes the commitment that removes the
alternatives, after which the output is exposable
(Section~\ref{sec:determination}).  Definition~\ref{def:consistency}
quantifies over all runs, unsafe ones included, and the following shows that
it must.

\begin{proposition}[Safe runs are confluent]
\label{prop:safe-runs}
For any ordered specification, a finite family of runs in which every output
event is safe is confluent at every common refinement of its final inputs.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Let $j$ be a common refinement of the final inputs and consider the output
events of all the runs in any order that respects the order within each run.
We maintain an $x\in\fiber{j}$ that refines every output event processed so
far, starting from any element of the nonempty fiber $\fiber{j}$.  Let the
next event expose $o$ on run $r$ at state $\sigma$.  By
Lemma~\ref{lem:run-residual}, $x$ lies in $\sigma$'s fiber at $j$, since it
refines every output exposed earlier on run $r$; and $j$ refines
$\sigma$'s current input.  Because $o$ is exposable at $\sigma$, some $x'$ in
that fiber refines both $o$ and $x$; continue with $x'$.  The final $x$
refines every output exposed on every run, so by the lemma it lies in every
run's fiber at $j$, and Proposition~\ref{prop:confluence} gives a common
state.
\end{proof}

Consistency therefore has content only because unsafe runs are included; the
only-if proofs below exhibit an inconsistent specification's unsafe run and
show that it cannot be rejoined.

\begin{theorem}[Functional CALM]
\label{thm:functional-calm}
A functional ordered specification is consistent if and only if it is
monotone.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Every fiber is a singleton, so a run exposes outputs $f(i_1),\ldots,f(i_m)$
at inputs $i_\ell\InputOrd i$, where $i$ is its final current input, and by
Lemma~\ref{lem:run-residual} its fiber at $k\succeq_I i$ is $\{f(k)\}$ if
$f(i_\ell)\OutputOrd f(k)$ for every $\ell$ and empty otherwise.  By
Proposition~\ref{prop:confluence}, runs are confluent at a common refinement
$k$ exactly when every output exposed on any of them lies below $f(k)$.  If
$f$ is monotone this holds, since each exposed $f(i_\ell)$ has
$i_\ell\InputOrd k$.  If $f$ is not monotone, some $i\prec_I k$ has
$f(i)\not\OutputOrd f(k)$; the unsafe run that starts at $\sigma_0(i)$ and
exposes $f(i)$, which is not exposable there, has an empty fiber at $k$ and
cannot be advanced to its own refinement $k$.
\end{proof}

In the non-monotone case $\mathsf{output}(f(i))$ empties the fiber at $k$:
$f(i)$ is not exposable, and a system must first make an input-sealing
commitment, as in Figure~\ref{fig:nonmonotone-frame}, before it may expose
$f(i)$.  In the election, $a$ and $b$
have no common upper bound, and none is required: monotonicity constrains only
comparable inputs, and $aa\cdot$ and $ab\cdot$ have no common refinement
because ballots are immutable.

This is the familiar CALM dichotomy stated at the semantic interface rather than
inside a particular programming language.  Classical CALM fits the pattern with
a familiar pair of orders: inputs are finite fact sets $J$ ordered by
inclusion, and outputs are answer sets $Q(J)$, also ordered by inclusion.
Equation~\ref{eq:functional-monotonicity} becomes
        \begin{equation}
  J\subseteq J'
  \quad\Longrightarrow\quad
  Q(J)\subseteq Q(J'),
  \label{eq:query-monotonicity}
        \end{equation}
the standard definition of a monotone query~\cite{abiteboul1995foundations},
which positive Datalog satisfies and negation may not.  Functional CALM
generalizes the semantic statement from fact sets under inclusion to arbitrary
input and output refinement partial orders.  The CRDT state interface
has the same functional shape, with received payloads ordered by inclusion as
input and the semilattice order as output order~\cite{shapiro2011crdt};
Sections~\ref{sec:query-instance} and~\ref{sec:crdt-instance} take up both
instances.

Historically, CALM stands for \emph{Consistency As Logical Monotonicity},
and its C has been read both as consistency and as confluence;
Definition~\ref{def:consistency} is both.  Functional CALM states the result
for any functional specification, regardless of the formalism used to express
it; once specifications become relational in the next section, the
monotonicity in question is Lifted Monotonicity.

Functional CALM handles uncertainty about input arrival, and prior to this work, that
was the whole of CALM: one input, one answer, and monotonicity decides whether
the answer can be exposed early.  Many specifications are not like that:
the functional dependency
fails, the system must choose among alternative outputs, and nothing so far says
whether choosing requires coordination.  The next section answers that, and the answer is
again a monotonicity condition.

\section{Complete CALM: Semantic Choice and Coordination}
\label{sec:complete-calm}

A semantic specification may admit more than one output for the same input.
Serializability may allow several transaction orders, and leader election may
allow any eligible leader.  Such specifications are relational rather than
functional, and we call them ambiguous.  Complete CALM extends Functional CALM
to this semantic output choice, and this section follows the plan of
Section~\ref{sec:functional-calm}.  Section~\ref{sec:ambiguity-story} walks
through the framework informally on two ambiguous examples.
Section~\ref{sec:relational-specifications} defines relational specifications
and Lifted Monotonicity, Section~\ref{sec:safe-exposure} extends the
transition system to output choice and to Lamport histories of several
participants, and
Section~\ref{sec:complete-calm-theorem} proves Complete CALM.
Section~\ref{sec:cap-corollary} then turns to coordination and Complete CAP.

\paragraph{Ambiguity.}
Some specifications are ambiguous: they admit several results for one
input, so the input column does not determine the output column and the
functional dependency fails.  Figure~\ref{fig:ambiguity-frames}(f) is the
ordering of two concurrent transactions $A$ and $B$: the input is the pair,
the output is a serial order, and either order is admitted, so the table has
two rows at one input.  Before $[A,B]$ can be exposed, a commitment must
remove the row for $[B,A]$: a second kind of commitment, a \emph{choice
commitment}.
Panel (g) is a traceroute
diagnostic for a service reachable from source $s$ directly at server
\texttt{s1} or through hop $a$ at server \texttt{s2}.  An output is the
prefix-closed set of routes observed so far, so $\{s{\to}a\}$, a probe that has
reached the hop but not the server, is an admitted output, and so is any union
of admitted outputs.  Exposing $\{s{\to}\texttt{s1}\}$ removes the two rows that
do not refine it, yet the remaining rows $\{s{\to}\texttt{s1},\ s{\to}a\}$ and
$\{s{\to}\texttt{s1},\ s{\to}a,\ s{\to}a{\to}\texttt{s2}\}$ still say
everything they said.  Nothing is lost: the output is exposable, and
exposing it is not a commitment.

What made the difference between panels (f) and (g)?  In both, the system
selected one of several admissible outputs.  In transaction ordering the other
output had nowhere to go: no admissible output refines both $[A,B]$ and
$[B,A]$, so neither was exposable without removing the other outright.  In traceroute every other output had somewhere to go: whatever two
route sets the specification admits, it also admits their union, which refines
both.  A set of outputs with this property, in which every finite subset has a
common refinement within the set, is called \emph{directed}.  Directedness is
what makes outputs exposable in the presence of ambiguity.  If the outputs
admitted at an input are directed, then whichever of them the system exposes,
every other output can still be refined with that value, so nothing is lost.
If they are not directed, some output becomes exposable only after a
commitment removes an alternative.

Two things can therefore go wrong as a relational specification runs.  Input
can grow in a way that leaves an admitted output with no refinement, as in the
election under the equality order.  Or the outputs admitted at one input can
fail to be directed, as in transaction ordering.  Complete CALM
(Section~\ref{sec:complete-calm-theorem}) rules out both with one condition, \emph{Lifted
Monotonicity}: whenever input grows from $i$ to $j$, every finite set of
outputs admitted at $i$ has a common refinement admitted at $j$.  Taking a
single output, this says every admitted output continues as input grows.
Taking $j=i$, it says the outputs admitted at one input are directed.  The
condition is a monotonicity condition: it says that the set of outputs
admitted grows, in the appropriate sense, as the input grows.

\paragraph{The commitment basis.}
The commitment basis is part of a system's design, alongside the
specification, and the choice of basis is a real one.  Familiar concurrency
control protocols differ exactly here.  Figure~\ref{fig:cc-commitments} returns to
transaction ordering with a third transaction $C$, so that six serial orders
are admitted.  Under two-phase locking, $A$ acquiring a lock ahead of $B$ commits $A$
before $B$ and nothing more; $C$'s position stays open until it contends for a
lock.  Under multiversion timestamp ordering, $B$ receiving the first timestamp
commits $B$ to first place and nothing more; $A$ and $C$ remain unordered.
Both protocols proceed by a sequence of such commitments.  The two bases
differ in the shape of a commitment, a relative order between two conflicting
transactions or an absolute position in the final order, and neither shape is
dictated by the specification.  Section~\ref{sec:design-boundary} returns to
basis design, and Appendix~\ref{app:additional-applications} works through a
basis with a single commitment, after which every remaining output is
exposable.

\paragraph{Instantiating the model.}
Everything so far is semantic: it concerns the specification and its orders,
and it holds of a single-node database ordering transactions just as it holds
of the election.  Now let the system be distributed.  It consists of participants, each of
which knows the specification, which is fixed and public, but sees only its
own events: the input that has reached it, the output events and commitments
it has itself made.  Each participant therefore has its own residual, obtained
from the specification by removing the rows its own events removed,
and two participants that have seen different events hold different
residuals.  Panels (a) and (b) of Figure~\ref{fig:election-frames} can be read
this way: one participant has so far received only $v_1$'s ballot, another has
also received $v_2$'s, and each has removed what its own input retired.  This
is the whole connection to executions.  Take any Lamport diagram of the system
and cut it at any consistent moment; each participant's events below the cut
determine its residual, and those residuals are all this paper ever consults.
Two cuts that yield the same residuals are indistinguishable here, however
their messages were ordered or batched
(Proposition~\ref{prop:residual-quotient}).  Complete CALM is stated at this
level: every Lamport history in which participants only receive input and
expose outputs is confluent, in that its participants can always be brought to one
residual at any input combining theirs, exactly when the specification is
monotone.

Commitments are precisely the semantic actions that require coordination;
input events and exposing exposable outputs may be done autonomously.
This follows directly from the discussion above.  Input arrives at each
participant on its own.  Exposing an exposable output retires only covered
rows, so every output
another participant still admits has a refinement consistent with the output
exposed, and combining inputs later contradicts nothing.  A commitment removes an alternative that another
participant's residual may still admit, so it is safe only if every such
participant agrees; that agreement is coordination.  Determination that needs
a commitment is therefore precisely the determination that needs coordination.
The rest of this section makes these claims precise, in the order of
Section~\ref{sec:functional-calm}: definitions, the transition system, the
theorem, and then coordination.

\subsection{Relational specifications and Lifted Monotonicity}
\label{sec:relational-specifications}

\begin{definition}[Ordered relational specification]
\label{def:relational-specification}
An \emph{ordered relational specification} is
        \begin{equation}
  \Spec=(I,\InputOrd,O,\OutputOrd,S),
  \qquad S\subseteq I\times O,
  \label{eq:relational-specification}
        \end{equation}
where $(I,\InputOrd)$ and $(O,\OutputOrd)$ are partially ordered sets.  A row
$(i,o)\in S$ says that output $o$ is admissible at input $i$.  Its fiber
$\fiber{i}$, as in Equation~\ref{eq:fiber}, may now contain several outputs.
The original specification has a nonempty fiber at every input under discussion.
A functional specification is the special case $\fiber{i}=\{f(i)\}$.
\end{definition}

The output-refinement order lifts from individual outputs to nonempty sets of
outputs.  For nonempty $A,B\subseteq O$, define
        \begin{equation}
  A\LiftedOutputOrd B
  \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
  \forall a\in A\;\exists b\in B:\;a\OutputOrd b.
  \label{eq:lifted-output-order}
        \end{equation}
This pointwise lift is a preorder: every output in the first set can continue
to some output in the second.  It is often called the Hoare preorder.

\begin{definition}[Relational Monotonicity]
\label{def:relational-monotonicity}
An ordered relational specification satisfies \emph{Relational Monotonicity}
when the mapping $i\mapsto\fiber{i}$ is monotone
under the pointwise lift:
        \begin{equation}
  i\InputOrd j
  \quad\Longrightarrow\quad
  \fiber{i}\LiftedOutputOrd\fiber{j}.
  \label{eq:relational-monotonicity}
        \end{equation}
\end{definition}

For singleton fibers this reduces to functional monotonicity,
Equation~\ref{eq:functional-monotonicity}.  Relational Monotonicity governs how
outputs continue \emph{between} fibers as input grows.  It says nothing about how different
outputs relate \emph{within} one fiber.  We now turn to that second question.

\begin{definition}[Compatibility]
\label{def:compatibility}
Let $F\subseteq O$ be a set of outputs.  Outputs $o_1,\ldots,o_k$ are
\emph{compatible in $F$} if some $u\in F$ refines all of them,
$o_r\OutputOrd u$ for every $r$, and \emph{incompatible in $F$} otherwise.
They are \emph{compatible at input $i$} when they are compatible in the fiber
$\fiber{i}$.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Directed fiber; Directed Outputs]
\label{def:fiber-directedness}
A fiber $F\subseteq O$ is \emph{directed} when every nonempty finite
$X\subseteq F$ is compatible in $F$.  An ordered relational specification has
\emph{Directed Outputs} when every fiber $\fiber{i}$ is directed.
\end{definition}

A singleton fiber is directed.  So is any fiber closed under a join operation,
such as a set of fact sets closed under union.  A fiber containing two
incomparable outputs with nothing admitted above both is not.  Compatibility is
not comparability: two incomparable outputs are compatible whenever some third
admitted output refines both, as in traceroute.

\paragraph{Directed and nondirected specifications.}

The two examples of Section~\ref{sec:ambiguity-story} sit on either side of
this definition.  In the traceroute of Figure~\ref{fig:ambiguity-frames}(g),
any two admitted route sets have their union admitted, and the union refines
both, so every finite set of admitted outputs is compatible and the fiber is
directed; exposing $\{s{\to}\texttt{s1}\}$ removed two rows, and both removals
were output retirements.  In the transaction ordering of panel (f), no
admitted output refines both $[A,B]$ and $[B,A]$, so the fiber is not
directed, and removing either row is a commitment.  Leader election is
nondirected for the same reason: if \texttt{server1} and \texttt{server2} are
both eligible and the interface permits either as sole leader, no admitted
output refines both choices.

\paragraph{Lifted Monotonicity.}
\label{sec:complete-calm-criterion}

Section~\ref{sec:ambiguity-story} named two things that can go wrong as an
ambiguous specification runs; Relational Monotonicity rules out the first and
Directed Outputs the second.  Relational Monotonicity concerns refinement
across inputs, and Directed Outputs concerns the outputs admitted at one fixed
input.  Neither implies the other.  The two combine into a single monotonicity condition under a second
lift of the output order.  For nonempty $A,B\subseteq O$, define
        \begin{equation}
  A\JointOutputOrd B
  \;\Longleftrightarrow\;
  \text{every nonempty finite }X\subseteq A\text{ is compatible in }B,
  \label{eq:joint-output-order}
        \end{equation}
the \emph{joint lift} of $\OutputOrd$.  The relation is transitive, and
$A\JointOutputOrd A$ holds exactly when $A$ is directed.

\begin{definition}[Lifted Monotonicity; monotone specification]
\label{def:lifted-monotonicity}
An ordered relational specification satisfies \emph{Lifted Monotonicity}, and
is called \emph{monotone}, when the mapping $i\mapsto\fiber{i}$ is monotone
under the joint lift:
        \begin{equation}
  i\InputOrd j
  \quad\Longrightarrow\quad
  \fiber{i}\JointOutputOrd\fiber{j}.
  \label{eq:lifted-monotonicity}
        \end{equation}
\end{definition}

\begin{proposition}[Lifted Monotonicity decomposes]
\label{prop:lifted-monotonicity-decomposition}
An ordered relational specification satisfies Lifted Monotonicity if and only
if it satisfies Relational Monotonicity and Directed Outputs.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Assume Relational Monotonicity and Directed Outputs, and let $i\InputOrd j$ and
$X\subseteq\fiber{i}$ be nonempty and finite.  Relational Monotonicity gives,
for each $x\in X$, an $x'\in\fiber{j}$ with $x\OutputOrd x'$; Directed Outputs
gives $u\in\fiber{j}$ refining every $x'$, hence every $x$.  So
$\fiber{i}\JointOutputOrd\fiber{j}$.

Conversely, assume Lifted Monotonicity.  Taking $j=i$,
$\fiber{i}\JointOutputOrd\fiber{i}$ says that $\fiber{i}$ is directed.  Taking
$X=\{o\}$ for $o\in\fiber{i}$ and $i\InputOrd j$ gives $u\in\fiber{j}$ with
$o\OutputOrd u$, which is Relational Monotonicity.
\end{proof}

For singleton fibers Lifted Monotonicity is functional monotonicity,
Equation~\ref{eq:functional-monotonicity}.  The analysis of output events below needs
it in a form that speaks of several inputs at once: an execution may already
have exposed outputs at different earlier inputs, and to continue without
invalidating any of them, one future output must refine them all.

\begin{proposition}[Complete CALM criterion]
\label{prop:complete-calm-criterion}
An ordered relational specification is monotone if and only if it satisfies the
\emph{Complete CALM criterion}: for every nonempty finite family of rows
$(i_r,o_r)\in S$, $r=1,\ldots,k$, and every common refinement $j$ of
the $i_r$, there is a $u\in\fiber{j}$ such that
        \begin{equation}
  o_r\OutputOrd u
  \qquad\text{for every }r\in\{1,\ldots,k\}.
  \label{eq:complete-calm-criterion}
        \end{equation}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Assume Lifted Monotonicity.  For each $r$,
$\fiber{i_r}\JointOutputOrd\fiber{j}$ gives $u_r\in\fiber{j}$ with
$o_r\OutputOrd u_r$, and $\fiber{j}$ is directed by
Proposition~\ref{prop:lifted-monotonicity-decomposition}, so some
$u\in\fiber{j}$ refines every $u_r$.  Conversely, assume the criterion, and let
$i\InputOrd j$ and $X\subseteq\fiber{i}$ be nonempty and finite.  The family
$\{(i,x):x\in X\}$ has common refinement $j$, so some $u\in\fiber{j}$
refines all of $X$; that is $\fiber{i}\JointOutputOrd\fiber{j}$.
\end{proof}

Finite families of every size are needed because pairwise compatibility does
not imply compatibility of a larger family
(Appendix~\ref{app:additional-applications}).  The inputs $i_r$ need not be
comparable: inputs within one run form a chain, so incomparable $i_r$ belong
to different runs that end at the same $j$.  The criterion is read at $j$,
looking back: regardless of how $j$ was reached, the outputs exposed along the
way have a common refinement admitted there.

Taken together, the criterion is ordinary monotonicity lifted twice.  For a
monotone function, as the input grows from $i$ to $j$ the single output at $i$
is refined by the single output at $j$.  Lifting from functions with a single
output per input to relations offering a set of outputs, Relational
Monotonicity asks that \emph{each} output admitted at $i$ be refined by
\emph{some} output admitted at $j$.  Lifting again from single output choices
to finite sets of alternate outputs, as different runs might expose, Lifted
Monotonicity asks that \emph{every finite set} of outputs admitted at $i$ be
\emph{jointly} refined by \emph{a single} output admitted at $j$.

\subsection{The transition system under output choice}
\label{sec:safe-exposure}

The transition system of Section~\ref{sec:determination} applies unchanged to
a relational specification; the difference is that a fiber $R(i)$ may now
contain several outputs, so choice commitments, and output events that remove
rows without being commitments, become relevant.  This subsection takes up
output events first, then the runs of several participants and the histories
they form.

\paragraph{Output events.}
Exposing $o\in R(i)$ promises that later output at $i$ and at every
refinement of $i$ will refine $o$, and $\mathsf{output}(o)$ keeps the promise
by producing $R\mathbin{\upharpoonright_O}o$
(Equation~\ref{eq:residual-refining-fibers}).  With several outputs in a
fiber, an output event can remove rows while its output remains exposable
(Definition~\ref{def:exposable}); the following characterizes when.

\begin{proposition}[Exposability]
\label{prop:exposability}
$o$ is exposable at $(i,R)$ if and only if for every valid $j$ with
$i\InputOrd j$ and every $x\in R(j)$ there is $u\in R(j)$ with
        \begin{equation}
  o\OutputOrd u
  \qquad\text{and}\qquad
  x\OutputOrd u,
  \label{eq:safe-exposure}
        \end{equation}
that is, $o$ is compatible in $R(j)$ with every $x\in R(j)$
(Definition~\ref{def:compatibility}).  When $o$ is exposable,
$\mathsf{output}(o)$ leaves every valid input valid, and $o$ is the least
element of the upper fiber of the resulting state.  When $o$ is not exposable,
$\mathsf{output}(o)$ removes a row by commitment.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
The event retains $R'(j)=\{u\in R(j):o\OutputOrd u\}$ at each valid
$j\succeq_I i$.  A removed $(j,x)$ is an output retirement exactly when some
retained $u$ has $x\OutputOrd u$, that is, when some $u\in R(j)$ lies above
both $o$ and $x$; for a retained $x$, $u=x$ serves.  When
Equation~\ref{eq:safe-exposure} holds, each $R'(j)$ contains such a $u$ and is
nonempty, and every retained output refines $o$.  A removal that is not an
output retirement is not an input retirement either, since the current input
is unchanged, so it is a removal by commitment.
\end{proof}

Equation~\ref{eq:safe-exposure} is directedness
(Definition~\ref{def:fiber-directedness}) read against the residual rather
than the specification.  Exposing an exposable output excludes nothing whose
content could not still be reached.  If $o$ is not exposable, some $x$ at $i$
or one of its refinements has no admitted refinement in common with $o$, and
exposing $o$ would destroy the content of $x$; the system must instead first
apply a commitment from its basis that removes $x$, after which $o$ may be
exposable, and Section~\ref{sec:cap-corollary} says what that costs.  In
Figure~\ref{fig:ambiguity-frames}, exposing $\{s{\to}\texttt{s1}\}$ in panel
(g) removes $\{s{\to}a\}$ but retains a refinement of both, an output
retirement; the commitment to $[A,B]$ in panel (f) removes $[B,A]$ with no
retained common refinement.

\paragraph{Participants and runs.}
A \emph{participant} is a holder of a residual state that changes only by its
own transitions: input events supplied by its environment, and the output
events and commitments it makes.  A participant that uses no commitment from the
basis performs a run (Definition~\ref{def:run}).  By the composition property of input events, two runs
that receive the same input in different orders or groupings, with no
output events or commitments, reach the same state; what can differ is which
output events and commitments a participant makes along the way.  Replicas, in
the usual sense, are participants that receive the same input in different
orders; their runs end at the same input, so consistency already covers them,
with no need for a separate notion of replica consistency.

\paragraph{Histories.}
Runs of several participants fit together into a history in Lamport's sense.
A \emph{distributed history} $H$ is a set of events, each at one participant,
with each participant's events totally ordered, each receive matched to a
send from another participant or from the environment, and
\emph{happens-before} the transitive closure of local order and matching.  A
receive that advances the receiving participant's input is an input event; a
receive that does not leaves the residual unchanged, as does an output event
that removes no row.  A \emph{consistent cut} $C$ is a subset of events closed
under happens-before, and $(H,C)$ is a \emph{pointed history}.  For each
participant $p$, the events of $C$ at $p$, in local order, form a run when
they include no commitment from the basis; in general they form a sequence of
transitions.  Write $\sigma_p^C$ for its final residual state and
$\operatorname{end}(H,C)=(\sigma_p^C)_{p}$ for the vector of residuals, the
\emph{semantic endpoint} of the cut.

\begin{proposition}[Residuals are the semantic quotient of histories]
\label{prop:residual-quotient}
Fix a specification, a participant set, and a commitment basis.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item $\operatorname{end}(H,C)$ is the same for every replay of the events
        of $C$ in an order that respects happens-before.
  \item Whether a transition is applicable at participant $p$ after $(H,C)$,
        and the residual it produces, depend only on $\sigma_p^C$.
  \item Consequently, pointed histories $(H,C)$ and $(H',C')$ with equal
        endpoints admit the same semantic continuations, and the endpoint map
        induces a bijection between its equivalence classes and the reachable
        residual vectors.
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Each coordinate $\sigma_p^C$ is determined by $p$'s events in local order,
which every happens-before-respecting replay preserves, and events at
different participants act on different coordinates; receives that advance no
input change no coordinate.  This gives (1).  Input events, output events, and
commitments are each partial functions of the local residual, which gives
(2).  Then (3) is (2) applied coordinate by coordinate, together with the
observation that any function induces a bijection between the classes of its
kernel equivalence and its image.
\end{proof}

Everything the specification cares about in a Lamport diagram survives in its
cut's residuals; message order, batching, and who has learned what are
forgotten exactly when they do not advance anyone's input.  Confluence
therefore has a meaning for histories.  Let $j$ be a common refinement of the
participants' current inputs at $C$.  The pointed history $(H,C)$ is
\emph{confluent at $j$} when $H$ can be extended past $C$, by input and
output events at the participants, so that every participant has current input
$j$ and all participants hold the same residual.  The extension is a semantic
object, as runs are: it says that the participants' states can be so
continued, not that any protocol delivers $j$ or communicates anything.

\subsection{The Complete CALM theorem}
\label{sec:complete-calm-theorem}

In the rest of this section, \emph{reachable} means reachable from a canonical
initial state by a run, with no commitment from the basis.  Consistency
(Definition~\ref{def:consistency}) was stated for any specification; for a
relational one, different runs may have exposed different outputs at the same
input, and an output event may have removed rows.

\begin{theorem}[Complete CALM]
\label{thm:complete-calm}
For an ordered relational specification, the following are equivalent:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item it satisfies Lifted Monotonicity:
        $i\InputOrd j$ implies $\fiber{i}\JointOutputOrd\fiber{j}$;
  \item it is consistent: its runs are confluent
        (Definition~\ref{def:consistency});
  \item for every finite set of participants, every distributed history in
        which each participant's events form a run---no commitment from the
        basis is used---is confluent, at every consistent cut, at every
        common refinement of the participants' current inputs.
\end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
\emph{(1) and (2).}  By Proposition~\ref{prop:confluence}, consistency says
that for every finite family of runs with final states $(i_r,R_r)$ and every common refinement $j$
of the $i_r$, the intersection $\bigcap_r R_r(j)$ is nonempty.  By
Lemma~\ref{lem:run-residual}, $u\in\fiber{j}$ lies in this intersection
exactly when it refines every output exposed on any of the runs; each such
output $o$ was exposed at some input $i_o\InputOrd j$ with $o\in\fiber{i_o}$.

Assume Lifted Monotonicity.  The rows $(i_o,o)$, over all outputs exposed on
the family, are finitely many and have common refinement $j$, so
Proposition~\ref{prop:complete-calm-criterion} supplies $u\in\fiber{j}$
refining them all, and $u$ lies in the intersection.

Conversely, assume consistency, and let rows $(i_1,o_1),\ldots,(i_k,o_k)$ have
a common refinement $j$.  For each $r$, the run that starts at
$\sigma_0(i_r)$ and exposes $o_r$ ends with
$R_r(j)=\{u\in\fiber{j}:o_r\OutputOrd u\}$.
Consistency gives $u$ in every $R_r(j)$, an admitted common refinement of the
$o_r$, which is the Complete CALM criterion.

\emph{(2) and (3).}  By Proposition~\ref{prop:residual-quotient}, the
endpoint of $(H,C)$ is the vector of final states of the participants' runs,
and what each participant can do next depends only on its coordinate.  If
runs are confluent, Proposition~\ref{prop:confluence} gives $u$ in the
intersection of the participants' fibers at $j$, and extending $H$ by an
input event to $j$ and $\mathsf{output}(u)$ at each participant brings them
all to $\bigl(j,\{(k,x)\in S:j\InputOrd k,\ u\OutputOrd x\}\bigr)$.
Conversely, any finite family of runs is realized by a history in which one
participant performs each run and no messages are exchanged; a common
extension at $j$ shows a common state with current input $j$ reachable from
every run's final state.
\end{proof}

The theorem is direct from the definitions.  Its content is the
identification: the one semantic obstruction to confluence is a finite family
of admissible rows with no admitted common refinement at a common input
refinement.  When none exists, participants may expose the outputs they
have, in whatever order input arrives, and remain reconcilable, though the
outputs they exposed may differ.  When such a family exists, some admissible output cannot be
exposed without excluding another outright, that is, without a commitment.

Exposability follows rather than being assumed.  In a consistent
specification, every $o\in R(i)$ is exposable at every reachable $(i,R)$: for
a valid $j\succeq_I i$ and $x\in R(j)$, the run reaching $(i,R)$ extended by
$\mathsf{output}(o)$ and the same run extended by $\mathsf{input}(j)$ and
$\mathsf{output}(x)$ must be confluent at $j$, which by
Lemma~\ref{lem:run-residual} supplies $u\in R(j)$ above both $o$ and $x$,
and Proposition~\ref{prop:exposability} applies.  A consistent specification
therefore needs no coordination: its runs are made of input events and
output events with exposable outputs alone, and the next subsection shows that only
commitments require agreement among participants
(Definition~\ref{def:coordination}).

\subsection{Coordination and the Complete CAP Theorem}
\label{sec:cap-corollary}

Exposing an exposable output narrows without excluding and can be done by
every participant at will.  A commitment excludes content, and the next result
says what that costs when several participants hold the same input.  In what
follows, a participant \emph{selects} an admissible output at the input it
holds by exposing it when it is exposable, and otherwise by first applying a
commitment that retains exactly the outputs refining it at its input and that
input's refinements.

\begin{definition}[Coordination]
\label{def:coordination}
\emph{Coordination} is agreement among participants, obtained before a
commitment is made, that no participant will select an alternative the
commitment excludes.
\end{definition}

\begin{theorem}[Commitments require coordination]
\label{thm:commitments-require-coordination}
Suppose the participants share a common commitment basis.  Let a commitment
$d$ in the basis be applicable at a reachable state $\sigma=(i,R)$ and remove
a row at the current input $i$ by commitment: some $x\in R(i)$ with no
refinement in $R'(i)$, where $(i,R')=d(\sigma)$.  Then there is a participant
state $\tau$, reachable from the canonical initial state by the same input
events followed by the selection of $x$, whose residual at $i$ consists of
refinements of $x$; and $d(\sigma)$ and $\tau$ have no common row at $i$.
Consequently $d$ cannot be applied without agreement that no participant takes
the course leading to $\tau$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Let a second participant receive the same input events as the first, reaching
a state with the same current input and with $x\in\fiber{i}$ still admitted,
and let it select $x$: by exposing it if it is exposable, and otherwise by a
commitment retaining exactly the outputs above $x$, which the common basis
makes available to it.  Its residual at $i$ is then contained in
$\{u\in\fiber{i}:x\OutputOrd u\}$, while the first participant's residual
$R'(i)$ contains no such $u$ by hypothesis.  The intersection at $i$ is
therefore empty: the two states cannot be merged, and no later input or output
event can restore a removed row.  The only way to avoid this outcome is for
the second participant not to select $x$, which it can be relied upon to do
only by agreement.
\end{proof}

CAP asks the same question in a more specific setting.  A \emph{partition}
divides the participants into two or more groups and stops all information
from crossing between groups until the partition heals: in a history, no
message sent on one side is received on another while it lasts, while each
participant continues to receive input from its own environment.  The
classical CAP theorem is a one-way impossibility: a linearizable register
cannot remain available through arbitrary partitions~\cite{gilbert2002cap}.
Complete CALM turns it into a dichotomy.

\begin{theorem}[Complete CAP]
\label{thm:complete-cap}
For an ordered relational specification $\Spec$, the following are equivalent:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item under every partition and every order of input arrival on each side,
        every admissible output at a participant's current input is exposable
        there, so that the participant may expose it without a commitment and
        without waiting for anything from across the partition
        (\emph{availability}), and for every common refinement $j$ of the
        current inputs on the two sides, some row at $j$ survives in every
        participant's state (\emph{reconciliation});
  \item $\Spec$ is monotone.
\end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Reconciliation is confluence of the histories a partition permits, at the
cut where it heals and at the common refinements of the two sides' current
inputs.  Every finite family of runs is realized by a history in which no
messages are exchanged, which every partition permits; so reconciliation under
every partition is condition~(3) of Theorem~\ref{thm:complete-calm}, hence
monotonicity, and monotonicity implies availability by the remark following
that theorem.
\end{proof}

Reconciliation is refinement under $\OutputOrd$: outputs exposed on different
sides of the partition are jointly refinable, not identical or totally ordered.  This
is weaker than linearizability and is what makes the characterization
bidirectional.  Together with Theorem~\ref{thm:commitments-require-coordination}
the division is exact.  In a monotone specification exposing outputs is all
that is ever needed, and availability and reconciliation both hold under partition.
In a non-monotone one the theorem identifies the family of rows responsible:
a participant that would select one of its members must make a commitment,
and must either wait to agree with the other side, giving up availability, or
make the commitment alone and risk an unreconcilable merge, giving up
reconciliation.

A linearizable register is the classical instance of the tradeoff side: two
reads of concurrent writes may be explained by opposite serial orders, no
total order refines both, and the theorem withholds availability under
partition.  Whether a commitment requires agreement across a partition also
depends on which participant may hold which input and make which choice;
Appendix~\ref{app:deployment} refines the theorem by such a \emph{deployment}
and shows that a causal register, though not monotone, needs no coordination
when each participant answers only its own reads.  With the central results
in place, we now compare their consequences across the surrounding
literature.

\subsection{Query monotonicity}
\label{sec:query-instance}

Section~\ref{sec:functional-calm} gave the functional encoding
$J\mapsto Q(J)$.  Relational-transducer CALM proves the operational result for
that language: monotone queries admit coordination-free evaluation under its
data-placement and execution assumptions
~\cite{ameloot2013relational,ameloot2015weaker}.  Functional CALM does not replace
that theorem.  It isolates the semantic order-preservation property so that it
can be compared with interfaces outside Datalog.

Why did earlier CALM results not state Directed Outputs separately?  Their
semantic query specification is functional: each input $J$ has one result
$Q(J)$, so each output fiber is a singleton and directed automatically.
CRDTs also present a functional state for a fixed set of incorporated
updates; semilattice join additionally provides least common refinements across
partial inputs.  Complete CALM makes multiple semantic outputs at one input explicit,
so directedness can no longer be assumed.

\subsection{CRDTs: from convergent state to safe observations}
\label{sec:crdt-instance}

  A CRDT answers one important question: if replicas receive the same updates,
will they converge to equivalent states despite differences in update delivery
and merge order?  Applications immediately ask a second question: what may a
client safely conclude from one replica's current state?  The CRDT merge theorem does not answer this second
question.  Keep CALM and CRDT On called out this gap and proposed monotonicity as
the test for safe queries~\cite{laddad2022keep}.  Functional CALM makes that
test precise for the whole client-visible interface.

Let $(D,\sqsubseteq,\sqcup)$ be the state join-semilattice, and let the input
be the finite set $U$ of immutable, joinable payloads received so far, ordered
by inclusion.  The replica's state is
        \begin{equation}
  s(U)=s_0\sqcup\bigsqcup U,
  \label{eq:crdt-state-function}
        \end{equation}
so $U\subseteq V$ implies $s(U)\sqsubseteq s(V)$, and
$s(U_1)\sqcup s(U_2)=s(U_1\cup U_2)$: the state interface is monotone, and
join is the least state refining both inputs~\cite{shapiro2011crdt}.  A
client observation $q:(D,\sqsubseteq)\to(R,\preceq_R)$ replaces the state
output with a new promise, and by Functional CALM the client-visible interface
$q\circ s$ is consistent exactly when
        \begin{equation}
  U\subseteq V \quad\Longrightarrow\quad q(s(U))\preceq_R q(s(V)).
  \label{eq:crdt-query-monotonicity}
        \end{equation}
Monotonicity of $q$ on all of $D$ is sufficient but not required; $q$ need
only preserve refinements between reachable states.  Operation-based CRDTs have the same form.  Their payloads
are operations delivered in causal order, and the set of operations a replica
has received, closed under causal predecessors, grows by union; the causal
order, once made explicit in the payload, is the state lattice, and the
replica's state is a function of that set.  This is the emulation of
operation-based by state-based CRDTs in the original CRDT
paper~\cite{shapiro2011crdt}, the operation-context semantics of replicated
data types~\cite{burckhardt2014replicated}, and the partially ordered log of
pure operation-based CRDTs~\cite{baquero2014operation}; see
also~\cite{hellerstein2025turtles}.

\paragraph{A safe merge can have an unsafe observation.}
Consider a two-phase set with state $(A,R)$: $A$ records additions and $R$
records removals.  Both components grow by union, so replica state merges
perfectly well.  Its user-facing query is
        \begin{equation}
  q_{\mathrm{2P}}(A,R)=A\setminus R,
  \qquad F_{\mathrm{2P}}=q_{\mathrm{2P}}\circ s.
  \label{eq:crdt-2p-query}
        \end{equation}
Suppose the payload set $U_0$ yields
\[
  s(U_0)=(\{\mathsf{potato},\mathsf{Ferrari}\},\varnothing),
  \qquad
  (q_{\mathrm{2P}}\circ s)(U_0)
    =\{\mathsf{potato},\mathsf{Ferrari}\}.
\]
If the application treats membership in this result as a lasting claim, it may
ship the Ferrari.  Now let $U_1$ add a delayed payload removing the Ferrari.
Then $U_0\subseteq U_1$, but
\[
  s(U_1)=(\{\mathsf{potato},\mathsf{Ferrari}\},\{\mathsf{Ferrari}\}),
  \qquad
  (q_{\mathrm{2P}}\circ s)(U_1)=\{\mathsf{potato}\}.
\]
The internal state advanced, but the client-visible claim did not.  An ordinary
point-in-time read may merely expose its chosen local cut.  The bug appears when
the program interprets that answer as complete, stable, or sufficient to justify
an irreversible action.  Such a promise needs a seal on the relevant updates.
State convergence does not establish monotonicity of an application query.

\paragraph{Threshold queries.}
Kuper and Newton's LVars introduced \emph{threshold queries}: Boolean-valued
application queries that become true when a lattice state crosses a threshold
~\cite{kuper2013lvars}.  Order the Boolean codomain by
$\mathsf{false}\preceq_B\mathsf{true}$.  For example, let a grow-only set $d$
record distinct failed login attempts and define
        \begin{equation}
  q_{100}(d)=[\,|d|\geq100\,],
  \qquad Q_{100}=q_{100}\circ s.
  \label{eq:crdt-thresholds}
        \end{equation}
Once $Q_{100}(U)=\mathsf{true}$, every refinement $V\supseteq U$ still
contains at least 100 attempts, so $Q_{100}(V)=\mathsf{true}$, and before the
threshold $\mathsf{false}$ is exposable because it may refine to
$\mathsf{true}$.  The query is monotone, so Functional CALM says that it
extracts a stable fact from an incomplete replica state without claiming that
the state itself is complete.

\paragraph{Beyond least merges.}
A join-semilattice provides a uniform \emph{least} refinement and a fixed merge
operator.  Complete CALM asks only for \emph{some} admitted common refinement of
each relevant finite family.  For example, suppose two shards expose
$[a_1,a_2]$ and $[b_1,b_2]$, and the output fiber admits every order-preserving
interleaving.  Both
        \begin{equation}
  [a_1,a_2,b_1,b_2]\qquad\text{and}\qquad[b_1,b_2,a_1,a_2]
  \label{eq:log-interleavings}
        \end{equation}
are minimal common refinements, and neither refines the other.  Consequently,
the pair has no least common refinement, so this output order is not a
join-semilattice.  It is nevertheless directed: any finite family of sequences
has an admitted order-preserving interleaving.  A join-semilattice is
therefore a sufficient discipline, not a requirement of the Complete CALM
criterion; strong eventual consistency, which it provides, is a stronger
promise than consistency in our sense, and last-writer-wins shows that
convergence can also be weaker, discarding one concurrent value that an
interface promised to preserve.

\subsection{Highly available transactions}
\label{sec:hat-instance}

For a transactional interface, an input is the set of begin, read, write, and
commit requests received so far, ordered by inclusion.  An output is a growing
set of public facts such as $\mathsf{read}(T,x,v)$,
$\mathsf{commit}(T)$, and session-order facts, also ordered by inclusion.  The
isolation or session guarantee determines which fact sets are admissible.
A deployment assigns transactions and requests to replicas.  The Complete
CAP Theorem tests the combinations those replicas can select across a partition,
under the contact, failure, and session assumptions of the interface
~\cite{bailis2013hat,terry1994session}.

\paragraph{Accumulating facts.}
Suppose two replicas execute transactions on disjoint keys.  One exposes
$\mathsf{read}(T_1,x,0)$ and $\mathsf{commit}(T_1)$; the other exposes
$\mathsf{read}(T_2,y,0)$ and $\mathsf{commit}(T_2)$.  If each read observes a
committed version at its replica, the union contains no dirty read or write
conflict.  Under the stated read-committed interface, later delivery preserves
all four facts.  This is the positive accumulation pattern behind HAT results;
it is not a claim that every combination of session guarantees is available.

\paragraph{Serializability and transaction ordering.}
The ordering issue becomes a distributed obstruction when exposed reads force
opposite serialization orders.  Initially $x=y=0$.  On one side, $T_1$ reads
$x=0$, writes $y=1$, and commits.
On the other, $T_2$ reads $y=0$, writes $x=1$, and commits.  Preserving $T_1$'s
read requires $T_1$ before $T_2$ in any serial explanation; preserving $T_2$'s
read requires $T_2$ before $T_1$.  The serialization graph contains a cycle, so
no serial history refines both sets of facts~\cite{adya2000generalized}.

\paragraph{Snapshot isolation.}
Let overlapping transactions $T_1$ and $T_2$ read the same snapshot and both
write $x$.  Separate replicas could each expose its local commit.  Snapshot
isolation's first-committer-wins rule admits no output containing both commit
facts~\cite{berenson1995critique,bailis2013hat}.  Eventual storage convergence
may choose a final state; it cannot retract a commit already exposed to a
client.

Convergent storage does not automatically provide a highly available
transactional interface.  Storage convergence constrains states after update delivery;
isolation constrains reads and commits exposed before then.

\subsection{Invariant confluence}
\label{sec:iconfluence-instance}

Let the input be a set of applied transactions, ordered by inclusion.  We use
the standard invariant-confluence assumptions: each transaction remains
applicable where the specification admits it and maps an invariant-valid state
to another invariant-valid state; independently reached states share the
required ancestor; the prescribed merge is associative and preserves all
included effects; and merged states remain reachable.  Outputs are reachable,
invariant-valid states.  We order outputs by merge refinement: $s\OutputOrd s'$
when $s'$ is an admitted merge that preserves every effect in $s$ (quotienting
states with identical effects if necessary to obtain a partial order).

Invariant confluence asks whether independently reachable valid states merge to
a valid state~\cite{bailis2014coordination}.  Under the assumptions above,
individual transaction preservation supplies Relational Monotonicity, while
invariant confluence supplies common refinements for independently reached
states at the combined transaction input.  Associativity and reachability let
the pairwise merge argument extend inductively to finite families.  Thus the
prescribed merge witnesses the Complete CALM criterion for the deployed transaction
families.

A uniqueness constraint supplies the standard counterexample.  Two replicas
each observe that username \texttt{sam} is free and allocate it to different
accounts.  Each local state satisfies uniqueness, but no invariant-valid common
refinement preserves both allocations.  The system must reserve names, reject
one allocation, or weaken the invariant.

State convergence, transaction availability, and invariant preservation test
three different specifications.  A convergent merge may produce an invalid
state; an available transaction may expose facts that later violate an
invariant; an invariant-confluent application need not provide a particular
isolation level.  The shared model makes these distinctions explicit rather
than collapsing the properties.  We close by drawing out the resulting design
lessons and scope limits.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Semantic choice versus operational nondeterminism}

Output choice in Complete CALM concerns the semantic interface, not whether
executions are deterministic.  CALM and CRDTs already account for operational nondeterminism in message delivery, batching, retries, and
merge order.  Their usual declared outputs are fixed for a given input.  Complete CALM
adds specifications whose semantic interface itself offers several outputs and
models the commitments that resolve them.

\subsection{A semantic criterion, not an implementation discipline}

The input/output relation and its two refinement orders omit internal
computation.  This is useful for stating a common boundary and insufficient for
building a system.  Relational transducers provide a language and operational
network semantics; semilattices provide a local algebra and deterministic
merge; transaction protocols provide scheduling, validation, and recovery.
Neither Functional CALM nor Complete CALM says how to represent $S$, choose the
refinement orders, compute a common refinement, or do so efficiently.  Those
questions belong to the programming language, protocol, and verification
method used to realize the specification.

\subsection{Specification design determines the boundary}
\label{sec:design-boundary}

The Complete CALM criterion is a property of the declared specification, not a
permanent label on ``register,'' ``transaction,'' or ``shopping cart.''
The deployment determines whether a commitment requires agreement between
participants.
A linearizable register and a causal register admit different explanations.  A
CRDT state and an exact read expose different promises.  A database permitted
to abort one transaction implements a different relation from one required to
commit both.

Narrowing $S$ may eliminate an incompatibility.  Broadening $O$ with a new
output that refines two alternatives may also eliminate it.  Changing the
deployment may keep an incompatibility under one participant's authority.  Each option
changes the interface or system design; none is a loophole in the criterion.

Read in the other direction, Proposition~\ref{prop:residual-quotient} is a
design criterion.  Choosing
the input domain and its order decides which histories the semantics treats as
the same: two cuts yield one residual exactly when they reach the same input
and have removed the same rows.  A finer input order forgets less of the
execution and obliges the specification to say more; a coarser one forgets
more.  The specification then states admissible outcomes over residuals,
without re-encoding the history it was built to forget, and the proposition
says that nothing it can ask is lost in doing so.

A reader who thinks in state machines can compile one into this form.  At the
finest grain, an input is the sequence of environment events the machine has
consumed, ordered by extension, and a row $(i,o)$ is admissible when some run
of the machine on $i$ produces $o$; the register example of
Appendix~\ref{app:deployment} is of this kind.  If the machine's state
summarizes its past, so that two event sequences reaching the same state have
the same futures, the states themselves may serve as inputs, ordered by
reachability, provided that order is antisymmetric.  That is the coarsening the
proposition licenses, and the election of Section~\ref{sec:election-story} is the
result for a vote-counting machine whose state is the set of ballots received.

\subsection{Consistency as confluence}
\label{sec:consistency-as-confluence}

The word consistency names two different things in the literature, and the
framework separates them.  A consistency model---linearizability, causal
consistency, snapshot isolation, read committed---is a specification: it says
which outputs are admissible for a given history of operations.  Consistency
in CALM's sense is a property of a specification: its histories are
confluent.  Written as a table with orders, every traditional model therefore
falls on one side of the dichotomy.  Linearizability and serializability
admit total orders that cannot refine two concurrent explanations, so they
are not monotone (Appendix~\ref{app:deployment},
Section~\ref{sec:hat-instance}).  Causal consistency admits one explanation
for concurrent reads and is monotone under the deployment in which each
participant answers its own reads (Appendix~\ref{app:deployment}).  Read
committed and the other HAT guarantees accumulate facts and are monotone
(Section~\ref{sec:hat-instance}).  Strong eventual consistency asks more
than confluence, a least common refinement that every replica reaches, and
last-writer-wins eventual consistency asks less, converging by discarding
content (Section~\ref{sec:crdt-instance}).  What the traditional definitions
leave implicit, and the framework requires, is the output order: the same
operation history yields a monotone or a non-monotone specification depending
on what an exposed read is taken to promise
(Section~\ref{sec:design-boundary}).  Confluence is thus not one more
consistency model; it is the question each model answers one way or the
other, whether what it admits can be provided without coordination.

\subsection{Safety is not liveness}

The theorems identify when exposed outputs remain refinement-consistent; they
do not guarantee message delivery, termination, or computability of a common
refinement.  The availability premise of the Complete CAP Theorem excludes an
implementation that never responds, but a full protocol result must still
state appropriate fairness, failure, and cost assumptions.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

The CALM conjecture connected logical monotonicity with coordination
freedom~\cite{hellerstein2010declarative,hellerstein2020keeping}, and Ameloot
et al.\ proved it for relational transducers, studying how data placement and
system knowledge change coordination
requirements~\cite{ameloot2013relational,ameloot2015weaker};
Sections~\ref{sec:functional-calm-theorem} and~\ref{sec:query-instance}
relate Functional CALM to that result.  Brewer's conjecture and the
Gilbert--Lynch theorem~\cite{brewer2000towards,gilbert2002cap} are taken up in
Section~\ref{sec:cap-corollary} and Appendix~\ref{app:deployment}.  CRDTs
derive strong eventual consistency from state or operation
algebras~\cite{shapiro2011crdt}, and Keep CALM and CRDT On first proposed
monotonicity as the criterion for safe observations of a
replica~\cite{laddad2022keep}; Section~\ref{sec:crdt-instance} places both in
the model.  Work on consistency semantics likewise distinguishes visibility,
ordering, availability, and
convergence~\cite{burckhardt2014principles,mahajan2011consistency}.
Highly available transactions~\cite{bailis2013hat} and invariant
confluence~\cite{bailis2014coordination} are Sections~\ref{sec:hat-instance}
and~\ref{sec:iconfluence-instance}.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

A semantic specification abstracts distribution away.  Functional CALM asks when
a functional specification can be implemented over distributed input arrival
without coordination: monotonicity means no seal is needed.  This generalizes the
classic result from logical fact sets to arbitrary input and output refinement
orders.

Complete CALM extends the model to specifications that offer several outputs for one
input.  Modeling output events and commitments reveals Directed Outputs as the
additional requirement for a coordination-free specification.
When directedness fails, some admissible alternative must be removed by a
commitment.  If different participants can make incompatible commitments,
coordination determines which alternative survives.

The Complete CAP Theorem is the partition form of the same equivalence.  It recovers the
classical impossibility when a cut separates incompatible choices and supplies
the positive side when all separated choices have common refinements.  A cut
may prevent agreement on input sealing or separate participants empowered to
make incompatible output choices.  Query monotonicity, CRDT merge,
transactional availability, and invariant confluence then become directly comparable without being identified
with one another.

\appendix
\section{Complete CAP under a Deployment}
\label{app:deployment}

Theorem~\ref{thm:complete-cap} (Complete CAP) quantifies over all participants
and inputs.  Whether a commitment is necessary is a semantic question; whether
it requires agreement across a partition also depends on \emph{deployment}:
which participant may hold which input and select which row.  A participant
that controls an entire choice may make the commitment locally.  This appendix
refines the theorem accordingly.

Return to leader election, with \texttt{server1} and \texttt{server2} both
eligible.  If one participant alone is entitled to name the leader, a
partition changes nothing: the two rows are an incompatible family, but both
belong to that participant, and it selects between them without consulting
anyone.  If instead one participant may name \texttt{server1} and another may
name \texttt{server2}, a partition that separates them lets each select its
row alone, and the two selections have no common refinement when the partition
heals.  The same specification is safe under the first deployment and not
under the second; what differs is which incompatible families a partition can
split between its sides.

\begin{definition}[Deployment; separable family]
\label{def:deployment}
A \emph{deployment} over a participant set $P$ assigns to each participant $p$
a set $\Delta_p\subseteq S$ of rows it may select; $(i,o)\in\Delta_p$ means
that $p$ may hold $i$ as a current input and select $o$ there, on its own
authority.  A partition $\Pi$ of $P$ (Section~\ref{sec:cap-corollary}) divides
the participants into
two or more nonempty components.  A finite family of rows is \emph{separable under
$\Pi$} when its members can be attributed to participants, each row lying in
its participant's $\Delta_p$, with at least two components represented.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Monotone across partitions]
\label{def:monotone-across-partitions}
Relative to a deployment, $\Spec$ is \emph{monotone across $\Pi$} when every
family separable under $\Pi$, together with every common refinement $j$
of its inputs, has an admitted common refinement in $\fiber{j}$.  It is
\emph{monotone across partitions} when it is monotone across every partition of
$P$.
\end{definition}

This is the Complete CALM criterion
(Proposition~\ref{prop:complete-calm-criterion}) restricted to the families a partition can separate.  Monotonicity implies monotonicity across partitions, since separable
families are families.  The converse fails whenever every incompatible family
lies within one participant's $\Delta_p$.  Two mutually exclusive choices owned
by one participant do not become separable merely because another component
acts.

\begin{theorem}[Complete CAP under a deployment]
\label{thm:complete-cap-deployment}
Fix a deployment.  The following are equivalent.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Under every partition $\Pi$ and every order of input arrival within
        each component: participants in one component may coordinate with one
        another, but each component selects outputs at the rows its
        participants hold without waiting for information from any other
        component (\emph{availability}); and for every common refinement
        $j$ of the current inputs held in different components, some row at
        $j$ survives in every participant's state, so that the components'
        selections reconcile when the partition heals (\emph{reconciliation}).
  \item $\Spec$ is monotone across partitions.
\end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
(2$\Rightarrow$1.)  Fix $\Pi$.  Families whose members all lie in one component
may be resolved by coordination within that component, which the partition does
not block.  The families that must reconcile without communication are the
separable ones.  Consider participants in at least two components, each having
made finitely many selections at its current input or its refinements, and a
common refinement $j$ of those inputs.  The family of all their
selections is separable, so monotonicity across $\Pi$ supplies $u\in\fiber{j}$
above every selected output.  Each participant's residual at $j$ is
$\fiber{j}$ intersected with the outputs above its own selections, and $u$
belongs to every such set.  Hence some row at $j$ survives in every state,
under every arrival order on each side.

(1$\Rightarrow$2.)  If monotonicity across some $\Pi$ fails, a separable family
$(i_1,o_1),\ldots,(i_k,o_k)$ and a common refinement $j$ admit no common
refinement in $\fiber{j}$.  Its participants lie in at least two components,
and availability lets each select $o_r$ at $i_r$ without learning the others'
selections.  Each participant's residual at $j$ then lies within the outputs
above its $o_r$, and no output of $\fiber{j}$ is above all of them, so no row
at $j$ survives in every state.  Avoiding this requires either a wait across
the cut, which surrenders availability, or refusing some of the rows, which
implements a narrower specification $S'\subsetneq S$.
\end{proof}

The difference from Theorem~\ref{thm:complete-cap} is that participants on the
same side of a cut are allowed to coordinate.  Only the families that a
partition separates must reconcile without communication, and only those
families are tested.  When the deployment lets every participant hold every
row, every family is separable under some partition, monotonicity across
partitions is monotonicity, and the theorem is Theorem~\ref{thm:complete-cap}.
Deployment determines which inputs and choices can occur independently, just
as data placement does in the relational-transducer model.

\subsection{Registers}
Consider two replicas of a register initially holding no value.  Participant
$p$ completes a write of \dog{} while $q$ concurrently completes a write of
\catv.  The replicas exchange both writes, after which a partition separates
them.  Each then serves a local read; both writes complete before either read
begins.

An input is a finite causally closed operation history, ordered by
induced-subhistory extension.  An output is an explanation $(L,\rho)$, where
$L$ is an operation order admitted by the consistency model and $\rho$ maps each
completed read to the write supplying its response.  Refinement extends $L$
without reversing an existing order and extends $\rho$ without changing an
answered read.

For linearizability, $L$ is a total order extending real time
~\cite{herlihy1990linearizability}.  If $p$ returns \catv, its explanation
requires $w_p(x,\dog)<_L w_q(x,\catv)$.  If $q$ returns \dog, its explanation
requires the opposite order.  No total order refines both explanations.  The
partition separates the read owners, recovering the classical CAP impossibility
as the negative side of the Complete CAP theorem
~\cite{gilbert2002cap}.

Under causal consistency, the writes remain concurrent and one explanation can
assign $p$'s read to \catv{} and $q$'s read to \dog{}
~\cite{lamport1978time,ahamad1995causal}.  The responses have a common
refinement.  Two possible answers for the same read remain incompatible, but
only that read's owner selects one; under a deployment in which each
participant holds its own views, no partition separates one local decision
into two independent choices, and the causal register is monotone across
partitions.

\section{Coordination-Free Frontiers}
\label{sec:frontiers}

So far we have taken each interface as given and tested the CALM criteria.  A
designer often faces the inverse question: how much can the interface promise
before coordination becomes unavoidable?  The answer depends on what must stay
fixed.  Here we fix the inputs, visible claims, refinement orders, and---when
partitions matter---the deployment.

We compare interface strength by the behaviors a specification admits.  An
interface $T$ is \emph{stronger} than $S$ when every behavior admitted by $T$ is
also admitted by $S$, but not conversely.  The stronger interface rules out more
implementations and gives clients a tighter guarantee.  For example,
linearizability is stronger than causal consistency because it admits fewer
histories.  Thus ``stronger'' corresponds to a smaller set of allowed behaviors.
This is a separate order from output refinement: $o\OutputOrd u$ says that one
particular output $u$ preserves the information already exposed by $o$.

We obtain two frontier results.  The first concerns information arriving over
time: how precisely can an interface answer while preserving every answer it
has already exposed?  The second concerns information arriving independently:
how much cross-node agreement can an interface require without withdrawing any
choice that it still offers locally?

\subsection{Remembering exactly what may remain true}

A small lookup table illustrates both the construction and its possible
compaction.
Suppose a data item initially resides at node $a$ and later moves to $b$.  Let
$o_a$ claim that a lookup starting at $a$ succeeds, and let $o_b$ make the same
claim for a lookup starting at $b$.  The richer output $o_{a\leadsto b}$ records
both that $b$ is the current location and that a lookup from $a$ succeeds by
following its forwarder to $b$.  It therefore preserves both simpler lookup
claims:
        \begin{equation}
  o_a\OutputOrd o_{a\leadsto b},
  \qquad o_b\OutputOrd o_{a\leadsto b}.
  \label{eq:lookup-output-dominance}
        \end{equation}
For $i_0\InputOrd i_1$, the transformation is explicit in
Table~\ref{tab:lookup-lift}.

\begin{table}[t]
  \centering
  \small
  \caption{Remembering every output a lookup relation has admitted so far.
  Dominated outputs can then be dropped without loss of content.}
  \label{tab:lookup-lift}
  \begin{tabular}{@{}c l l l@{}}
    \toprule
    Input & Original fiber $X(i)$ & Remembered $X^\uparrow(i)$
          & Pruned $M(i)$ \\
    \midrule
    $i_0$ & $\{o_a\}$
          & $\{o_a\}$
          & $\{o_a\}$ \\
    $i_1$ & $\{o_b,o_{a\leadsto b}\}$
          & $\{o_a,o_b,o_{a\leadsto b}\}$
          & $\{o_{a\leadsto b}\}$ \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
\end{table}

The middle column is the original specification table, grouped into one fiber
per input.  The next column remembers: at each input it collects every
output admitted by the original table at that input or any preceding input.
The last column removes $o_a$ and $o_b$ because the retained
$o_{a\leadsto b}$ refines both.  Nothing they said has been forgotten.  If two
remembered outputs were incomparable, both would have to remain unless another
retained output refined them.

We now state this construction for an arbitrary ordered relational specification
$S\subseteq I\times O$.  Write its original fiber as
        \begin{equation}
  X(i)=S(i)=\{o\in O:(i,o)\in S\}.
  \label{eq:hull-original-fiber}
        \end{equation}
The transformation replaces the output carrier $O$ by $\mathcal P(O)$,
compared under the pointwise lift of Equation~\ref{eq:lifted-output-order}.
Its unpruned output is
        \begin{equation}
  X^\uparrow(i)=\bigcup_{j\InputOrd i}X(j).
  \label{eq:monotone-hull}
        \end{equation}
Equivalently, each original row $(j,o)$ contributes $o$ to the transformed row
at every $i\succeq_I j$.  Literal union remembers all rows; the pointwise lift
captures their semantic coverage.  In particular, one retained output may cover
many remembered rows by refining them.

Call $A:I\to\mathcal P(O)$ a \emph{powerset relaxation} of $X$ when
$X(i)\LiftedOutputOrd A(i)$ at every input: every currently admitted output has
a retained refinement.  Such a relaxation is monotone when
$i\InputOrd j$ implies $A(i)\LiftedOutputOrd A(j)$.

\begin{theorem}[Remembered-output frontier]
\label{thm:monotone-hull}
The map $X^\uparrow$ is a monotone powerset relaxation.  Moreover, every monotone
powerset relaxation $A$ semantically covers every output admitted so far:
        \begin{equation}
  X^\uparrow(i)\LiftedOutputOrd A(i)
  \qquad\text{for every }i.
  \label{eq:hull-coverage}
        \end{equation}
If $X^\uparrow(i)$ is finite, let $M(i)$ contain its undominated elements.  Then
$M(i)$ and $X^\uparrow(i)$ are equivalent under $\LiftedOutputOrd$, the map $M$
is monotone, and $M(i)$ is the unique smallest subset of $X^\uparrow(i)$ that
covers all of $X^\uparrow(i)$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
The union includes $X(i)$, so $X^\uparrow$ is a relaxation.  If
$i\InputOrd k$, every predecessor of $i$ is also a predecessor of $k$; hence
$X^\uparrow(i)\subseteq X^\uparrow(k)$, which implies monotonicity under
$\LiftedOutputOrd$.

Now let $A$ be any monotone powerset relaxation and take
$o\in X^\uparrow(i)$.  Some $j\InputOrd i$ has $o\in X(j)$.  Relaxation gives
an $a\in A(j)$ with $o\OutputOrd a$, and monotonicity gives a
$b\in A(i)$ with $a\OutputOrd b$.  Thus $b$ refines $o$, proving
Equation~\ref{eq:hull-coverage}.

For a finite set, every element is below an undominated element.  Therefore
$X^\uparrow(i)\LiftedOutputOrd M(i)$; the reverse direction follows from
$M(i)\subseteq X^\uparrow(i)$.  The same observation proves monotonicity of $M$.
Finally, any subset of $X^\uparrow(i)$ that covers an undominated element must
contain that element itself.  Every covering subset therefore contains $M(i)$,
which proves minimality and uniqueness.
\end{proof}

The theorem's perhaps surprising content is minimality.  Remembering all prior
outputs is an obvious safe construction, but every monotone relaxation must
preserve their semantic content.  Dominance is the only free pruning available
when representatives must come from the remembered outputs; further pruning
requires introducing some other admitted output that refines what was removed.

\subsection{Persistent forwarding in search structures}

B-link trees provide a concrete systems instance.  During a node split, a parent
may still direct a search to the old node.  The tree remains searchable because
the old node carries a high key and a right link to the new range; the parent
can be repaired later~\cite{lehman1981efficient}.  In other words, the
implementation preserves an old search starting point even though it is no
longer exact.

We can state the required invariant without committing to one tree algorithm.
Inputs are finite prefixes $h$ of legal restructuring histories.  For a key $k$
and stable search position $p$, put $(k,p)$ in $R(h)$ when the key-directed
search procedure can start at $p$ and follow a finite path to a current
occurrence of $k$.  A restructuring step must satisfy three obligations:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item exposed search positions retain stable identities;
  \item every old key-directed edge is replaced by a key-directed path; and
  \item every old occurrence of $k$ can reach a current occurrence of $k$.
\end{enumerate}
The last two obligations allow paths to change while preserving their meaning.
Deletion or reclamation needs a separate seal or lifetime mechanism because it
can violate them.

\begin{proposition}[Persistent-forwarding monotonicity]
\label{prop:persistent-forwarding}
If the three obligations hold, then $h\InputOrd h'$ implies
$R(h)\subseteq R(h')$.  Positive forward-reachability claims are therefore
monotone, and by Functional CALM the specification is consistent.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Take a search path valid at $h$.  At $h'$, replace each old edge by the path that
simulates it.  From the old terminal occurrence, follow the preserved path to a
current occurrence.  The concatenated path validates the original search
claim.
\end{proof}

This positive result meets the remembered-output frontier.  For a fixed key in
a split-only migration chain, let $X(g)$ be the exact search certificates at
prefix $g$.  Every historical certificate in $X^\uparrow(h)$ has a refinement
in $R(h)$, namely its current forwarding path:
        \begin{equation}
  X^\uparrow(h)\LiftedOutputOrd R(h).
  \label{eq:forwarding-hull}
        \end{equation}
Theorem~\ref{thm:monotone-hull} says that any monotone relaxation must provide
this semantic coverage.  Current forwarding paths may compact many obsolete
certificates by dominating them, just as in Table~\ref{tab:lookup-lift}.  This is deliberately a
split-only statement.  Full implementations with deletion, merging, or memory
reclamation must prove stronger lifetime invariants or coordinate before old
search positions are reclaimed.

\subsection{Preserving independent local choices}

A second small example captures the cross-node question.  Suppose replicas $p$
and $q$ may each expose configuration 0 or 1.  With no cross-replica constraint,
the four complete outputs are
        \begin{equation}
  (0,0),\quad(0,1),\quad(1,0),\quad(1,1).
  \label{eq:product-config-example}
        \end{equation}
Now strengthen the interface by requiring both replicas to expose the same
configuration.  Only $(0,0)$ and $(1,1)$ remain.  Each replica still appears to
have both local choices, so the strengthening has changed no one-replica
interface.  During a partition, however, $p$ may choose 0 while $q$ chooses 1.
The mixed pair $(0,1)$ is locally legal but globally forbidden, and no output
can preserve both choices.  The new global constraint therefore requires
agreement or sacrifices one of the advertised local choices.

The theorem generalizes this example.  Fix a finite participant set $P$.  At
input $i$, let $V_p(i)$ be the complete views locally available to participant
$p$.  The baseline \emph{product interface} admits every combination of local
views.  A stronger interface may remove combinations.  We call it locally
conservative when it leaves each one-participant interface unchanged and adds
only cross-participant constraints.

For the formal result, assume three local properties:
views can continue when input grows; complete views are maximal under local
refinement; and locally co-occurring claims of one owner have a common local
refinement.  The product interface consists of every tuple in
$\prod_{p\in P}V_p(i)$ and every partial tuple obtained by omitting coordinates.
A \emph{normalized strengthening} $T$ selects some complete product tuples and
admits exactly their partial restrictions.  Local conservativity means that, at
every input, the projection of $T$ onto coordinate $p$ remains all of $V_p(i)$.

\begin{theorem}[Independent-product frontier]
\label{thm:product-frontier}
Suppose each coordinate has one owner, every relevant group of owners can be
separated by a legal partition, and availability permits every locally admitted
choice.  The product interface is monotone across partitions, so by
Theorem~\ref{thm:complete-cap-deployment} it remains available and consistent
under every partition.  Every proper normalized, locally conservative
strengthening is not monotone across partitions.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
For the product interface, take any family of choices that a partition can
separate.  Claims made by one owner have a common local refinement by assumption;
claims of different owners occupy different coordinates and can simply be put
in the same tuple.  Continue each coordinate at the common refined input.  The
resulting tuple preserves the whole family, as monotonicity across partitions
requires.

Now let $T$ be a proper strengthening.  Some complete product tuple $x$ is
missing from $T$.  Local conservativity says that every coordinate $x_p$ is
still locally available to its owner, perhaps as part of a different admitted
tuple.  A partition can separate those owners, and availability lets each choose
its coordinate.  If their selections had a common $T$-refinement, normalize and
complete it.  Maximality of the coordinates forces the result to be exactly
$x$, the tuple that $T$ forbids.  Hence the separated family has no admitted
common refinement.
\end{proof}

\paragraph{A register frontier.}
The abstract product has a direct consistency interpretation.  For each process
$p$, let $V_p(i)$ contain the register views that are individually explainable
by some legal real-time-respecting execution.  A view records the source write
for $p$'s reads, but it need not order concurrent writes in the same way as
another process.  The product interface allows all combinations of these
individually legal views.  Downward-closed history extension preserves old
views, so this causal-view interface is monotone across partitions when each
read has a unique owner.

Linearizability keeps every one-process behavior but adds one cross-process
requirement: all views must be projections of a single global total order.  In
the execution from Appendix~\ref{app:deployment}, one read forces
$\dog<\catv$ and the other forces $\catv<\dog$.  Each local view is legal alone,
but their pair has no global explanation.  Linearizability therefore removes a
product tuple and crosses the frontier.

The qualification is important.  A system can avoid this obstruction by
removing a local choice, assigning the choice to one authority, restricting
the deployment, or forbidding only combinations that a partition cannot
separate.
The theorem does not rank every consistency model.  It identifies the exact
cost of adding cross-participant constraints while promising to preserve all
local behavior.

\section{Additional Applications}
\label{app:additional-applications}

The examples below show how the same two questions recur in classical
distributed tasks: can each choice survive more input, and can independent
choices survive together?  We isolate those semantic obstructions here.  The
results do not replace model-specific theorems about failures, computability, or
termination.

\subsection{One commitment, then monotone authority}

Agreement protocols illustrate what coordination can accomplish.  Before an
authority is established, several incompatible authority lineages may remain
possible.  Publishing one lineage rules the others out: that is a commitment.
Suppose the commitment fixes both the initial authority and a rule that uniquely
determines every certified successor.  The system can then expose three kinds
of growing evidence: a certified epoch chain $E$, votes or certificates $V$,
and decisions $D$ indexed by epoch and slot.  Define refinement by
        \begin{equation}
  (E,V,D)\OutputOrd(E',V',D')
  \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
  E\preceq_{\rm pref}E',\quad V\subseteq V',\quad D\subseteq D'.
  \label{eq:authority-refinement}
        \end{equation}
Protocol safety ensures that a later certificate extends the unique lineage and
preserves prior decisions.  Public authority changes are therefore refinements,
not revisions: votes, certificates, epochs, and decisions all accumulate after
the initial semantic commitment.

This statement does not claim that later certificates are free.  Producing one
may require communication and agreement.  It says something more specific:
later protocol work does not choose among remaining semantic branches or
retract a public result.  If the first commitment did not fix a unique
succession rule, competing next epochs would present another semantic choice
and require another commitment.

\subsection{Why $k$-set agreement needs finite families}

Consensus forbids two different decision values.  The more general $k$-set
agreement task allows at most $k$ values, which makes pairwise examples
misleading when $k>1$.  Fix $k\geq1$, at least $k+1$ participants, and $k+1$
distinct valid proposals $v_1,\ldots,v_{k+1}$.  Outputs are irrevocable partial
decision maps $m:P\rightharpoonup V$, ordered by extension, with at most $k$
values in their range.

At participant $p_r$'s local proposal input, the decision
$\{p_r\mapsto v_r\}$ is admissible.  The union of all proposal inputs is a common
input refinement.  Each local decision can continue there by itself, and any
family of at most $k$ distinct decisions can coexist.  The family of all $k+1$
cannot: every common refinement would preserve $k+1$ values and violate the
specification.  This is exactly the obstruction detected by Lifted
Monotonicity.  If a partition lets the owners decide independently, the
same family witnesses the negative side of Complete CAP.  Consensus is the
special case $k=1$.

This argument identifies the semantic incompatibility.  Classical wait-free and
topological impossibility theorems additionally depend on a computation model,
failure assumptions, and progress requirements~\cite{saks2000set}.

\subsection{Membership-sensitive naming}

Tight strong renaming presents a different source of incompatibility.  The input
is the finite participant set $P$, ordered by inclusion.  The common output
universe contains finite partial naming maps; at input $P$, legal outputs are
injective maps
        \begin{equation}
  m:P\longrightarrow\{1,\ldots,|P|\},
  \label{eq:tight-renaming}
        \end{equation}
ordered by map extension.

Now start two processes independently.  At input $\{p\}$, tightness forces the
only name $p\mapsto1$; at input $\{q\}$ it likewise forces $q\mapsto1$.  The
combined input $\{p,q\}$ refines both singleton inputs.  Either assignment can
continue alone by giving the other process name 2, so Relational Monotonicity
is not the problem.  They cannot continue together: an injective map cannot
preserve both assignments to name 1.  Complete CALM fails on this two-row family,
and a deployment that places $p$ and $q$ in different components gives the
corresponding Complete CAP obstruction.

Notice why simply extending one completed naming is not a witness.  If $n$
processes already use names $1$ through $n$, a new process can receive $n+1$
without changing an old assignment.  The obstruction comes from independent
partial memberships making incompatible early choices.  The classical renaming
hierarchy adds model-specific topological and wait-free conclusions
~\cite{herlihy1991waitfree,herlihy1999topological}.

\subsection{What does a snapshot promise?}

The word ``snapshot'' covers interfaces with very different semantic strength.
Consider inputs that are finite downward-closed causal histories, ordered by
induced extension.  A modest \emph{cut} interface outputs a finite
downward-closed subset of the available history as a lower bound on what has
happened.  Old cuts remain legal as history grows, and the union of finitely
many cuts remains downward closed.  This interface satisfies Lifted
Monotonicity and Directed Outputs.

That easy positive result should not be mistaken for a conventional snapshot
implementation.  The cut interface promises neither freshness nor completeness;
it could legally return an old cut.  A stronger interface may promise that a
vector or cut is the complete state at a globally finished boundary.  Under
equality of final answers, another relevant event changes the required result.
Functional CALM then says that the boundary must be sealed before the
completeness claim is exposed.

These are safety statements about two declared interfaces.  A distributed
snapshot protocol must also collect a consistent cut and terminate under its
channel and failure assumptions~\cite{chandy1985distributed}.  An atomic
snapshot records values at a chosen linearization point instead.  Classic
wait-free shared-memory implementations demonstrate that total ordering alone
does not prove a need for coordination; the process, memory, placement, and
failure model all matter~\cite{afek1993atomic}.
