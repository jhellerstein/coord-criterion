\title{Complete CALM: A Unified Criterion for Coordination}

\author{Joseph M. Hellerstein}
\affiliation{%

  \country{USA}

\begin{abstract}
When does a system inherently require coordination?  CAP, CALM, CRDTs, Highly
Available Transactions, and invariant confluence draw related coordination
boundaries in different mathematical languages.  This fragmentation has allowed
conventional wisdom to drift from what the literature actually proves.

Existing accounts characterize when coordination can be avoided, but do not
generally model what coordination accomplishes when it is needed.  We give a
semantic framework in which to do so: a specification is a table of admissible
input--output pairs with ordered columns, and the state of a computation is
the \emph{residual} of that table, the rows still admissible.  Residuals are
provably the semantic quotient of Lamport histories, so every question below
is a question about executions.  Within this framework we complete the
picture in two steps.  \emph{Functional CALM} generalizes
the classical result from Datalog and fact inclusion to functions between
arbitrary partially ordered input and output domains, giving the broader
folklore interpretation of CALM a formal semantic basis.  Independent of implementation
language, monotonicity characterizes exactly when a function's output can be
exposed as distributed input arrives without
sealing that input.

\emph{Complete CALM} extends the analysis from functions to relational
specifications that allow a choice among outputs.  Such a specification is
\emph{coordination-free}---any admissible output may be exposed at will, and
independent exposures reconcile on merge under every arrival order---exactly
when it has \emph{lifted monotonicity}, every admitted output surviving as
input grows, and \emph{directed outputs}, every finite set of outputs admitted
at one input having a common refinement.
An \emph{exposure} makes an output visible while retaining a refinement of every
admissible alternative; a \emph{commitment} rules at least one out; and \emph{coordination} is
agreement before participants make incompatible commitments.
This covers settings such as leader election and transactional commit ordering.
Complete CALM yields a Complete CAP Theorem that extends classical CAP from a
one-way impossibility about linearizability to a bidirectional dichotomy
parameterized by the declared consistency semantics and the deployment.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Coordination occurs when one participant must wait for decisions or agreement
from another before acting.  The wait adds latency and can destroy availability
when the needed communication crosses a network partition.  Avoiding
unnecessary coordination is therefore a central goal in distributed systems
and databases.  This paper asks which specifications intrinsically require it.

The CALM Conjecture proposed a crisp, bidirectional answer: consistency can be
achieved without coordination exactly when the computation is
monotone~\cite{hellerstein2010declarative}.  Ameloot et al.\ proved it, in
both directions, for Datalog queries evaluated by networks of relational
transducers~\cite{ameloot2013relational,hellerstein2020keeping}.  The theorem
is therefore tied to one formalism. The intuition behind the CALM Theorem is often
invoked in broader contexts, leaving a gap between folklore and what was proven.

Several other traditions draw closely related boundaries, each in its own
formalism.  The CAP Theorem proves an impossibility for available linearizable registers
under partition~\cite{brewer2000towards,gilbert2002cap}.  State-based CRDTs give
a strong sufficient discipline for coordination-free convergence using
join-semilattices and inflationary updates~\cite{shapiro2011crdt}, but those
state-convergence guarantees do not by themselves constrain reads or downstream
client behavior~\cite{laddad2022keep}.  Highly
Available Transactions (HATs) classify transaction guarantees that survive
partitions~\cite{bailis2013hat}.  Invariant confluence gives a necessary and
sufficient condition for independently executed transactions to merge without
breaking an application invariant~\cite{bailis2014coordination}.

The results plainly share an idea, but their mathematical frameworks do not
compose: logic programs and transducers, register executions, semilattices,
transaction histories, and invariant-preserving database states expose
different objects and assumptions.  This makes simple cross-cutting questions
awkward.  Does CALM encompass CAP?  Are CRDT join laws necessary for
coordination freedom, or one strong sufficient discipline?  Does convergent
storage provide highly available transactions?  Do convergence and transaction
availability preserve application invariants?  Section~\ref{sec:cap-corollary}
answers the first question; the CRDT, HAT, and invariant-confluence parts of
Section~\ref{sec:instances} answer the others.

We begin by introducing a general notion of specification, agnostic to any
language or implementation details.  It is consistent with state-machine-style
specifications and can be compiled from them
(Section~\ref{sec:complete-calm}); what it elides is the incidental detail of
execution order, keeping only what the specification can ask about.  A
\emph{functional semantic
specification} (\S\ref{sec:functional-specifications}) is an arbitrary function
\[
  f:(I,\InputOrd)\longrightarrow(O,\OutputOrd)
\]
between a partially ordered input domain and a separately partially ordered
output domain.  It is monotone in the standard mathematical sense when
\[
  i\InputOrd j \quad\Longrightarrow\quad f(i)\OutputOrd f(j).
\]
Distributed implementations make input arrival uncertain: participants may hold different
input refinements, received in different orders and batches.  Monotonicity says
that output exposed from any one of them remains valid as more input arrives.
If the function is not monotone, exposing an output may first require closing
off the input refinements that could contradict it.  We call the resulting
condition an input \emph{seal} (\S\ref{sec:determination}); establishing one
may require agreement among the participants who could supply those inputs.

Our first step, \emph{Functional CALM} (\S\ref{sec:functional-calm-theorem}),
generalizes the CALM result from relational logic languages
and fact inclusion to these arbitrary functions and refinement partial orders.
This generalization provides a common language for coordination-freedom results
over functional specifications, including query evaluation and
the usual CRDT state interface.  This does not assume deterministic execution:
message timing, local state, batching, retries, and merge order may vary.
``Functional'' means only that each fixed semantic input has one specified
output.

Our second step addresses a case those interfaces do not expose: the semantic
specification itself may be intentionally \emph{ambiguous}. By this we do not mean it is
informal, but rather that it formally allows several distinguishable outputs for one input.
As examples, leader election may allow any participant to be eligible; a database may permit several
serial schedules.  The specification for many such systems is then a relation
(\S\ref{sec:relational-specifications}):
\[
  S\subseteq I\times O,
  \qquad
  \fiber{i}=\{o\in O:(i,o)\in S\},
\]
where \(\fiber{i}\)---the \emph{fiber} at \(i\)---is the set of outputs
that are admitted as ``within spec'' at input \(i\).

Moving from
a function to a relation creates two distinct obligations.
First, input refinement must now compare \emph{sets} of possible outputs.  We
lift the output order to these sets: \(\fiber{i}\) refines to \(\fiber{j}\)
when every output admitted at \(i\) has some refinement admitted at \(j\).
Monotonicity of the fiber map under this lifted preorder is \emph{Lifted
Monotonicity} (\S\ref{sec:relational-specifications}).  It ensures that each
alternative output will remain valid as input grows.

Second, Lifted Monotonicity says nothing about ambiguity---different alternatives
admitted at the \emph{same} input.  Safely exposing one requires that it can
coexist with the others through admitted common refinements.  We call this
within-fiber requirement \emph{Directed Outputs}, based on the order-theoretic notion
of directedness
(\S\ref{sec:relational-specifications}).  When it fails, forward progress must rule
out an alternative; we call that step a \emph{commitment}
(Definition~\ref{def:transition-system}).  If
different participants can make incompatible commitments, they must coordinate
first.  Complete CALM combines Lifted Monotonicity and Directed Outputs
(\S\ref{sec:complete-calm-criterion}) to characterize when a specification is
coordination-free: outputs may be exposed at will, independent exposures
reconcile on merge, and the outcome does not depend on arrival order
(\S\ref{sec:safe-exposure}).

For singleton fibers---as in functional specifications---Lifted Monotonicity reduces to ordinary monotonicity,
and the Directed Outputs property holds automatically.

Complete CALM also yields a Complete CAP Theorem
(\S\ref{sec:cap-corollary}).  Classical CAP gives an
impossibility for linearizable registers under partition.
The Complete CAP Theorem extends from impossibility to dichotomy, characterizing
both sides of the boundary: specifications that are subject to CAP tradeoffs and those that are not.
Linearizability becomes a special case on the tradeoff side. This unifies the ideas of CAP (im)possibility
and coordination avoidance in a single formalism.

% partition has an allowed common refinement, consistency and
% availability can coexist through the partition.  Otherwise the system must wait
% across the cut and lose availability, or make incompatible commitments and lose

This paper makes four contributions:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item A semantic framework for distributed computation.  A specification
        is a relation with ordered inputs and outputs; a \emph{residual state}
        records what remains admissible; transitions are input events,
        exposures, and \emph{commitments}, the last drawn from a declared
        \emph{commitment basis} that fixes what a system may make binding.
        Residuals are provably the semantic quotient of Lamport histories:
        any consistent cut of an execution yields them, and they suffice for
        every question the paper asks.  The framework is consistent with
        state-machine specifications and can be compiled from them, eliding
        only incidental execution order.  Everything below is stated and proved
        in this framework, and Section~\ref{sec:instances} shows that CRDTs,
        highly available transactions, and invariant confluence are instances
        of it.
  \item Functional CALM generalizes and proves the CALM Conjecture in the context of
        arbitrary functions, represented as semantic input/output specifications
        with separate input- and output-refinement partial orders.
  \item Complete CALM extends the model to semantic output choice, formalizes
        exposure and commitment, and identifies Lifted Monotonicity plus
        Directed Outputs as the criterion for a coordination-free
        specification: exposure at will, reconciliation on merge, and
        insensitivity to arrival order.  Commitments are shown to require
        coordination.  In partition form this is the Complete CAP Theorem,
        which extends the classical impossibility to a bidirectional dichotomy
        parameterized by the declared consistency specification and the
        deployment.
  \item Two coordination-free frontier theorems characterize how far an
        interface can be strengthened without requiring coordination.  The first
        identifies exactly which earlier outputs a monotone interface must
        preserve; the second shows when adding constraints across independently
        acting participants necessarily creates a CAP tradeoff.
\end{enumerate}

Section~\ref{sec:nutshell} walks through the framework on a running
example.  Section~\ref{sec:functional-calm} develops Functional CALM.
Section~\ref{sec:complete-calm} introduces semantic choice, commitment,
Complete CALM, and Complete CAP.  Section~\ref{sec:instances} applies the
results to familiar systems, and Section~\ref{sec:discussion} draws out the
design lessons and scope limits.  Section~\ref{sec:frontiers} then derives
maximal coordination-free interfaces.  Appendix~\ref{app:additional-applications}
gives further classical applications.

\paragraph{A specification is a table.}
The centerpiece of the framework is a very simple notion of specification: a
table of input--output pairs, listing the outputs admissible for each input.
The specification for squaring has the rows $(2,4)$, $(3,9)$, $(4,16)$, and so
on forever.  In database terms, squaring's table satisfies a functional
dependency: the input column determines the output column.  We call such a
specification \emph{functional}.  A specification is a model of what is
allowed, not a data structure: no component of a system holds it or consults
it.  Everything that follows is a matter of removing rows from a
specification, and for now we speak of ``the system'' as a single machine or actor;
who does what in a distributed implementation comes at the end.

Figure~\ref{fig:election-frames}(a) is the specification of a small election.
Three voters $v_1,v_2,v_3$ each cast at most one immutable ballot for $a$ or
$b$; the result is $\bot$ (no result yet), $a$, or $b$.  An input represents the
ballots seen so far, written as a three-letter string whose $k$th position holds
$v_k$'s ballot or a dot if it has not arrived; only rows above $a\cdot\cdot$,
where $v_1$ has voted $a$, are shown.  The first row says that with $v_1$'s
ballot alone the result is $\bot$; row $aa\cdot$ says that once $v_2$ also votes
$a$ the result is $a$; row $abb$ says that if $v_2$ and $v_3$ both vote $b$ the
result is $b$.

The rows are only part of the specification.  Each column also carries a
partial order, and both orders are design decisions.  The input order also
fixes how much of history the system is obliged to remember: two executions
that reach the same input are, for the specification, the same.  The \emph{input order}
says what counts as more input: here, more ballots, so $ab\cdot$ \emph{refines}
$a\cdot\cdot$, while $aa\cdot$ and $ab\cdot$ are incomparable.  The \emph{output
order} says when a later output keeps every promise an earlier one made: here
$\bot$ lies below both $a$ and $b$, so ``no result yet'' can eventually refine
to a winner, but $a$ and $b$ are incomparable, so neither winner refines the
other.  A
different design could make all outputs incomparable, so that every result is
final; we return to that choice below.  Given these orders, one can follow any input
upward through its refinements, say $a\cdot\cdot$ to $ab\cdot$ to $aba$: the
output climbs too, from $\bot$ to $a$, and never steps down or sideways.  That
is what it means for the \emph{specification} to be \emph{monotone}, a property
of the rows together with the orders.

\paragraph{Input arrives.}
Now $v_2$'s ballot arrives, for $b$; panel (b) shows the result.  The
\emph{current input}, the ballots seen so far, has advanced from $a\cdot\cdot$
to $ab\cdot$, shown in bold.  What an input event does to the specification
is governed by one rule: only the rows at the current input and its
refinements remain, and every other row is \emph{retired}.  The specification
with the retired rows removed is a \emph{residual state}, the account of what
is still possible, and the inputs that still have rows are the \emph{valid} ones.

Input events are the march of external history.  The environment produces
them, and the system neither chooses nor pays for them.  They constrain the
future, as we see next, but they involve no choice by the system, and the
residual state they produce depends only on the current input reached: the
same ballots delivered in another order or grouping yield the same residual
state.

Panel (b) retires rows for both of the reasons the rule allows.  The row at
$a\cdot\cdot$ is retired because its input has been superseded: $ab\cdot$
refines it, so the election will never again be asked about $a\cdot\cdot$
alone.  The rows at
$aa\cdot$, $aaa$, and $aab$ are retired because their inputs have become
unreachable: this specification admits one ballot per voter, so no input in it
refines both $aa\cdot$ and $ab\cdot$, and those rows described futures the
election can no longer have.  That is a choice made in the specification, not
a limit of the model; a specification that admitted a second, conflicting
ballot would record both and say what the result then is.

\paragraph{What could still happen.}
While input may still grow, what matters is not only the current row but what
any input that could still arrive might require.  In Lamport's terms, the
refinements of the current input are the inputs that some extension of the
history can still produce.  The future of the election is therefore its
current input together with the valid refinements, and the outputs of those
rows form the current input's \emph{upper fiber}.  In
panel (b) those rows are the current row and the two refinements $aba$ and
$abb$ in which $v_3$ votes, so the upper fiber, shaded, is $\{\bot,a,b\}$:
either candidate could still win.

Panel (c) shows the alternative in which $v_2$ voted $a$.  The rows at or
above $aa\cdot$ all say $a$, so the upper fiber is the singleton $\{a\}$ and
the current input is \emph{determined}: it fixes the output regardless of what
ballot $v_3$ could cast.  Narrowing the upper fiber until a single output
remains is \emph{determination}.  In database theory terms it establishes a
\emph{conditional functional dependency} on the residual: for all input values
at or above the current input, the output is determined to be the same.  Here
input arrival determined the residual on its own; below we meet the case where
the system must act.

\paragraph{Commitments.}
Return to panel (b) and let the registrar close the polls: $v_3$'s ballot,
should it arrive, will not be counted.  Panel (d) shows the result.  The rows
at $aba$ and $abb$ are removed although no ballot arrived and the current
input has not changed: those inputs have been sealed off.  This is a
\emph{commitment}, specifically an \emph{input-sealing commitment}, and it is
an act of the system rather than of the environment.  It
irrevocably disallows refinements the original specification admitted.  Only
the system may perform it, and only if it is among the commitments the system
declares in advance, its \emph{commitment basis}.  Afterward $ab\cdot$ is
\emph{sealed}:
no refinement of it remains valid, and its upper fiber has collapsed to
$\{\bot\}$, so it too is determined.
For a functional specification, input-sealing commitments are the only kind
of commitment there is.  Each row's input determines its output, so there are
never choices to be made among outputs; the only thing to commit to is sealing input.

\paragraph{Exposure.}
\emph{Exposing} a result to a client is a different act from committing.
Exposing $o$ promises that the final result will refine $o$.  The promise is
already kept whenever $o$ lies below everything in the upper fiber, and such an
$o$ is \emph{exposure-ready}.  Read the footers of
Figure~\ref{fig:election-frames}: $\bot$ is ready in every panel, because every
shaded output refines it, and $a$ is ready in panel (c).  A system may expose
an output only when doing so removes no genuine alternative; for a functional
specification that means the output is ready.  A ready output may be exposed
at any time, removing no row.

\paragraph{When monotonicity fails.}
Now change one thing: make all outputs incomparable, dropping
$\bot\OutputOrd a,b$.  The rows are the same,
but in panels (a) and (b) nothing is ready: $\bot$ no longer lies below the $a$
and $b$ in the upper fiber, so announcing it would promise something a later
ballot could contradict.  The specification is no longer monotone: as inputs
grow under $\InputOrd$, outputs change in a way $\OutputOrd$ does not sanction.
To announce anything, the registrar must first make an input-sealing
commitment.
Figure~\ref{fig:nonmonotone-frame} shows the result of closing the polls at
$a\cdot\cdot$: every refinement is sealed off by the commitment, the upper fiber
collapses to $\{\bot\}$, and only now is $\bot$ exposure-ready.  That contrast
is Functional CALM (Section~\ref{sec:functional-calm}): a monotone functional
specification can expose outputs as input arrives; a non-monotone one must
first seal input by commitment.

    \bottomrule
    \multicolumn{2}{@{}l}{\footnotesize each removed row refines to a remaining one:}\\
    \multicolumn{2}{@{}l}{\footnotesize an exposure}
  \end{tabular}
  \caption{Two specifications with several rows at the current input $i$, each
  shown after one output has been exposed.  In (g) an output is a prefix-closed
  set of routes from source $s$, which reaches server \texttt{s1} directly and
  server \texttt{s2} through hop $a$.  Shaded cells are the upper fiber that
  remains.  Legend as in Figure~\ref{fig:election-frames}.}
  \label{fig:ambiguity-frames}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{Ambiguity.}
Some specifications are ambiguous: they admit several results for one
input, so the input column does not determine the output column and the
functional dependency fails.  Figure~\ref{fig:ambiguity-frames}(f) is leader
election with two eligible servers, either permitted as sole leader, so the
table has two rows at one input.  Committing to \texttt{server1} removes the row
for \texttt{server2}: a second kind of commitment, a \emph{choice commitment}.
Panel (g) is a traceroute
diagnostic for a service reachable from source $s$ directly at server
\texttt{s1} or through hop $a$ at server \texttt{s2}.  An output is the
prefix-closed set of routes observed so far, so $\{s{\to}a\}$, a probe that has
reached the hop but not the server, is a legal output, and so is any union of
legal outputs.  Exposing $\{s{\to}\texttt{s1}\}$ removes the two rows that
do not refine it, yet the remaining rows $\{s{\to}\texttt{s1},\ s{\to}a\}$ and
$\{s{\to}\texttt{s1},\ s{\to}a,\ s{\to}a{\to}\texttt{s2}\}$ still say
everything they said.  Nothing is lost: the output is \emph{exposable}, and
exposing it is not a commitment.

What made the difference between panels (f) and (g)?  In both, the system
selected one of several admissible outputs.  In leader election the other
output had nowhere to go: no admissible output refines both \texttt{server1}
and \texttt{server2}, so neither was exposable without removing the other
outright.  In traceroute every other output had somewhere to go: whatever two
route sets the specification admits, it also admits their union, which refines
both.  A set of outputs with this property, in which every finite subset has a
common refinement within the set, is called \emph{directed}.  Directedness is
what makes exposure possible in the presence of ambiguity.  If the outputs
admitted at an input are directed, then whichever of them the system exposes,
every other output can still be refined with that value, so nothing is lost.
If they are not directed, some output becomes exposable only after a
commitment removes an alternative.  Complete CALM
(Section~\ref{sec:complete-calm}) isolates exactly these two requirements:
outputs must continue as input grows, as in the election, which is
\emph{Lifted Monotonicity}; and the outputs admitted at one input must be
directed, as in traceroute but not in leader election.

\paragraph{Commitments versus everything else.}
The legend of Figures~\ref{fig:election-frames} and~\ref{fig:ambiguity-frames}
distinguishes two kinds of removed row, and the distinction is exactly
commitment versus not.  A gray row is redundant: some remaining row says
everything it said, so removing it narrows nothing.  A struck row was an
alternative in its own right: removing it narrows what is within spec, and
only a commitment does that.

The other two kinds of transition remove rows without committing.  Input
arrival retires the rows the new input does not refine (panels (b), (c)); it
belongs to the environment.  Exposure removes the rows that do not refine the
exposed output (panel (g)); it belongs to the system but is not a commitment.
The two CALM conditions say when these two transitions remove only redundant
rows: monotonicity says this of input arrival, as in panel (b), where the
retired $\bot$ at $a\cdot\cdot$ is refined by the $\bot$ at $ab\cdot$;
directedness says it of exposure, as in panel (g).  A commitment, by contrast,
always narrows; that is its purpose (panels (d), (e), (f)).  Computation is not on
this list at all.  Tallying the ballots in panel (c) to notice that the result
is determined removes no row; computation has no effect on the residual, and
so never counts as determination.

\paragraph{Instantiating the model.}
Everything so far is semantic: it concerns the specification and its orders,
and it would hold of a single-node database choosing a serialization order
among concurrent transactions just as it holds of the election.  Now let the
system be distributed.  It consists of participants, each of
which knows the specification, which is fixed and public, but sees only its
own events: the input that has reached it, the exposures and commitments it
has itself made.  Each participant therefore has its own residual, obtained
from the specification by removing the rows its own events removed,
and two participants that have seen different events hold different
residuals.  Panels (a) and (b) of Figure~\ref{fig:election-frames} can be read
this way: one participant has so far received only $v_1$'s ballot, another has
also received $v_2$'s, and each has removed what its own input retired.  This
is the whole connection to executions.  Take any Lamport diagram of the system
and cut it at any consistent moment; each participant's events below the cut
determine its residual, and those residuals are all this paper ever consults.
Two cuts that yield the same residuals are indistinguishable here, however
their messages were ordered or batched
(Proposition~\ref{prop:residual-quotient}).

Commitments are precisely the semantic actions that require coordination;
input events and ready output exposures may be done autonomously.  This
follows directly from the discussion above.  Input arrives at each participant
on its own.  A ready exposure removes only redundant rows, so every other
participant's residual still refines what was exposed, and combining inputs
later contradicts nothing.  A commitment removes an alternative that another
participant's residual may still admit, so it is safe only if every such
participant agrees; that agreement is coordination.  Determination that needs
a commitment is therefore precisely the determination that needs coordination.
Section~\ref{sec:functional-calm} formalizes this for functions, where only
input-sealing commitments are ever needed; Section~\ref{sec:complete-calm}
does so for relations.

\section{Functional CALM}
\label{sec:functional-calm}

A semantic specification describes allowed input/output behavior without
committing to a language, placement, protocol, or message schedule.  We begin with the
case addressed in both the original CALM and the CRDT state interface: each
input has one specified output.

\subsection{Functional specifications with refinement orders}
\label{sec:functional-specifications}

\begin{definition}[Functional ordered specification]
\label{def:functional-specification}
A \emph{functional ordered specification} is a function
        \begin{equation}
  f:(I,\InputOrd)\longrightarrow(O,\OutputOrd),
  \label{eq:functional-specification}
        \end{equation}
where $(I,\InputOrd)$ and $(O,\OutputOrd)$ are partially ordered sets.  We call
$\InputOrd$ the \emph{input-refinement order}: $i\InputOrd j$ means that $j$
contains at least the input information in $i$.  We call $\OutputOrd$ the
\emph{output-refinement order}: $o\OutputOrd u$ means that $u$ preserves every
externally visible claim in $o$ and may add more.
\end{definition}

These are two separate partial orders.  The input order compares inputs; the
output order compares outputs.  Neither orders executions or rows in a table.
A specification describes
admissible behavior; it is not a data structure that an implementation must
store or consult.  The same holds for the semantic states derived from it
below.

\begin{definition}[Monotonicity]
\label{def:functional-monotonicity}
A functional ordered specification $f$ is \emph{monotone} if
        \begin{equation}
  i\InputOrd j
  \quad\Longrightarrow\quad
  f(i)\OutputOrd f(j).
  \label{eq:functional-monotonicity}
        \end{equation}
\end{definition}

This is the standard mathematical definition of a monotone function.  In
systems terms, an output exposed from partial input remains valid as more input
arrives.

\subsection{A semantic transition system}
\label{sec:determination}

A function gives one output for each input, but a refined input may require a
different output.  Before an execution can say anything binding, we need to
keep track of which input--output possibilities remain, which events remove
them, and which of those removals are acts of choice.  We do this with a
transition system, defined in full below, whose states are residual states and
whose transitions are of three kinds: input events, exposures, and
commitments.  The definitions follow the vocabulary of Section~\ref{sec:nutshell}
and the panels of Figure~\ref{fig:election-frames}.  Choice commitments and
exposure events are defined here but discussed in
Section~\ref{sec:complete-calm}, where relational specifications make them
necessary.

\paragraph{The specification as a table.}
View the specification as a table $S\subseteq I\times O$ whose rows are the
pairs $(i,f(i))$.  For any subrelation $R\subseteq S$ and input $i$, the
\emph{fiber} of $R$ at $i$ is the set of outputs paired with $i$:
        \begin{equation}
  R(i)=\{o\in O:(i,o)\in R\}.
  \label{eq:fiber}
        \end{equation}
For the full specification this is $S(i)$, which for a function is $\{f(i)\}$.
Execution never adds a row.  Everything that follows is a story about which
rows have been removed from the table, and by what.

Inputs are \emph{cumulative}: an input is everything seen so far, and
$i\InputOrd j$ means that $j$ contains at least the information in $i$.
Cumulative means that the input only grows under $\InputOrd$; it does not imply
that an input is internally consistent in any other, application-specific
sense.  The input domain $(I,\InputOrd)$ may be designed to accommodate
multiple possible worlds or probability distributions, corrections,
retractions, or other application-specific structure.  The specification sees
none of this internal structure; it sees inputs only through the domain, its
order, and the outputs it admits for each.  In our election example, the input
domain admits one ballot per voter by design; an alternate specification that
admitted a second, conflicting ballot would record both and say which outputs
are then admissible.  Write $k\prec_I i$ when
$k\InputOrd i$ and $k\neq i$, and $\mathord\uparrow_I i=\{j\in I:i\InputOrd j\}$
for the refinements of $i$.

\paragraph{States.}
The states of our transition system are residuals of the initial specification and input.
Every panel of Figure~\ref{fig:election-frames} is such a state: the specification
with some rows removed, one input current.  In panel (b) the current input is
$ab\cdot$ and every row whose input does not refine it has been removed; such
inputs are \emph{retired}.  A residual state obeys
the following conditions.

\begin{definition}[Residual state]
\label{def:residual-state}
A \emph{residual state} is a pair $\sigma=(i,R)$ such that
\begin{enumerate}
  \item $i\in I$ is the \emph{current input};
  \item $R\subseteq S$;
  \item the current input has a row in $R$:
        \begin{equation}
          R(i)\neq\varnothing;
          \label{eq:current-input-validity}
        \end{equation}
  \item only refinements of the current input have rows in $R$:
        \begin{equation}
          R(k)=\varnothing
          \qquad\text{whenever }i\not\InputOrd k.
          \label{eq:retired-input-absence}
        \end{equation}
\end{enumerate}
$R$ is the \emph{residual specification}.  An input $k$ is \emph{valid} when
$R(k)\neq\varnothing$, and otherwise \emph{invalid}.
\end{definition}

Condition 3 says that the current input still has an acceptable output;
condition 4 says that retirement is complete.  Valid inputs other than the
current one are the potential futures: refinements of $i$ whose rows have not
been removed.

For an input $i$, the \emph{canonical initial state} is
        \begin{equation}
  \sigma_0(i)=\bigl(i,\ \{(k,o)\in S:\ i\InputOrd k\}\bigr),
  \label{eq:canonical-initial-state}
        \end{equation}
the specification restricted to $i$ and its refinements.
Figure~\ref{fig:election-frames}(a) is $\sigma_0(a\cdot\cdot)$.

\begin{definition}[Semantic transition system]
\label{def:transition-system}
The \emph{semantic transition system} of a specification has the residual
states as its states.  Its transitions are partial functions on residual
states of exactly three kinds.  Each is defined at a state only when the result
satisfies Definition~\ref{def:residual-state}, and each removes rows and adds
none.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \emph{Input event.}  For $j\in I$ with $i\InputOrd j$, the
        event $\mathsf{update}(j)$, produced by the environment, makes $j$ the
        current input and keeps exactly the rows whose input refines $j$:
        \begin{equation}
          (i,R)\xrightarrow{\mathsf{update}(j)}
          \bigl(j,\ R\mathbin{\upharpoonright_I}j\bigr),
          \qquad
          R\mathbin{\upharpoonright_I}j=\{(k,u)\in R:\ j\InputOrd k\},
          \label{eq:input-update-residual}
        \end{equation}
        defined when $R(j)\neq\varnothing$.
  \item \emph{Exposure event.}  For $o\in R(i)$, the event
        $\mathsf{expose}(o)$, made by the system, keeps the current input
        fixed and keeps exactly the rows whose output refines $o$:
        \begin{equation}
          (i,R)\xrightarrow{\mathsf{expose}(o)}
          \bigl(i,\ R\mathbin{\upharpoonright_O}o\bigr),
          \qquad
          R\mathbin{\upharpoonright_O}o=\{(k,u)\in R:\ o\OutputOrd u\},
          \label{eq:residual-refining-fibers}
        \end{equation}
        defined exactly when $o$ is \emph{exposable at $i$}: for every valid
        $j$ with $i\InputOrd j$ and every $x\in R(j)$ there is $u\in R(j)$ with
        \begin{equation}
          o\OutputOrd u
          \qquad\text{and}\qquad
          x\OutputOrd u.
          \label{eq:safe-exposure}
        \end{equation}
  \item \emph{Commitment.}  A commitment $p$, made by the system, keeps the
        current input fixed and strictly restricts the residual: $p(i,R)=(i,R')$
        with $R'\subsetneq R$.  It is an \emph{input-sealing commitment} if it
        makes some valid input invalid, $R'(k)=\varnothing$ for some $k$ with
        $R(k)\neq\varnothing$ (necessarily $k\neq i$), and a \emph{choice
        commitment} otherwise, when every valid input remains valid.  The
        system's \emph{commitment basis} is the set of commitments it may use.
        A commitment is \emph{applicable} at $\sigma$ when $p(\sigma)$ is
        defined.
\end{enumerate}
A residual state is \emph{reachable} when it is obtained from some canonical
initial state by a finite sequence of these transitions.  Where only some
kinds are allowed, we say so: a state reachable by input events alone, for
instance, has had no commitment and no exposure.
\end{definition}

Nothing else changes a residual state.  In particular, computation changes
nothing: it may inform which transition the system takes, but it is not itself
a transition.  The rest of this subsection discusses input events and
input-sealing commitments, which are all a functional specification needs;
Section~\ref{sec:complete-calm} takes up choice commitments and exposure
events.

\paragraph{Input events.}
In panel (b) the environment has delivered $v_2$'s ballot and
$\mathsf{update}(ab\cdot)$ has advanced the current input from $a\cdot\cdot$ to
$ab\cdot$.  The environment produces input events; the system does not
choose them.
An event retires rows, and so narrows the future, but it makes no choice among
outputs: it adds no row and leaves every surviving fiber unchanged.  The event
is undefined when the new input has no row left,
for instance because an earlier commitment sealed it off: the environment cannot
make an invalid input current.  Input events compose: $\mathsf{update}(j)$
followed by $\mathsf{update}(k)$ has the same effect as $\mathsf{update}(k)$
alone, since $\mathord\uparrow_I k\subseteq\mathord\uparrow_I j$.  Arrival
order, batching, and repeated delivery therefore do not affect the state
reached by input events alone; only the input reached matters.  In practice the environment usually delivers changes, such as single
ballots, rather than whole inputs.  A change $\delta$ applied to the current
input yields a new input $i\oplus\delta=j$, and is legal exactly when $j$ is a
legal refinement of $i$.  The model does not treat $\delta$ as a kind of input;
the event it causes is simply $\mathsf{update}(j)$, and nothing about $\delta$
beyond the $j$ it produces matters.  A change already incorporated gives
$i\oplus\delta=i$, and $\mathsf{update}(i)$ at $(i,R)$ is the identity, so
duplicate delivery changes nothing.

\paragraph{Input-sealing commitments.}
Figure~\ref{fig:election-frames}(d) removes the rows at $aba$ and $abb$ with
the current input fixed.  No input event does this; it is an input-sealing
commitment, an act of the system, and for a functional specification it is the
only kind of commitment that can ever be needed, since every fiber has one row
and there is never an output to choose among.  Whether such a commitment is
available is part of the system model, not of the specification: it must be in
the commitment basis.  A system that lacks the authority to seal input simply
has no input-sealing commitment to apply, and a system that must still accept a
late ballot with a rejection or other fallback response keeps that row and has
not sealed the input.  A commitment records the binding act, not the
computation that selected it.

\paragraph{Determination.}
The shaded outputs in Figure~\ref{fig:election-frames} are those that the
current input or any continuation of it could still require.  For
$R\subseteq S$ and an input $i$, the \emph{upper fiber}
        \begin{equation}
  R^\uparrow(i)=\{o\in O:\exists j\in I,\ i\InputOrd j\text{ and }(j,o)\in R\}
  \label{eq:upper-fiber}
        \end{equation}
collects the outputs admitted at $i$ or at any still-valid refinement of $i$.

\begin{definition}[Determined input and determined state]
\label{def:determination}
The current input $i$ of $(i,R)$ is \emph{determined} when its upper fiber is a
singleton:
        \begin{equation}
  |R^\uparrow(i)|=1.
  \label{eq:determined-input}
        \end{equation}
A residual state is \emph{determined} when its current input is determined.
\end{definition}

Determinedness is stronger than having one row at $i$.  Every fiber in the
election is a singleton, yet panels (a) and (b) are undetermined because valid
refinements disagree; panel (c) is determined because none does.

\begin{definition}[Input-sealed state]
\label{def:input-sealing}
The current input $i$ of $(i,R)$ is \emph{sealed} when no strict refinement of $i$
is valid:
        \begin{equation}
  R(j)=\varnothing\qquad\text{for every }j\succ_I i.
  \label{eq:sealed-input}
        \end{equation}
A residual state is \emph{input-sealed} when its current input is sealed.
\end{definition}

When $i$ is sealed, $R^\uparrow(i)=R(i)$: nothing beyond the current fiber can
be required, and in the functional case $i$ is then determined.  Panel (d) is
sealed and determined; panel (c) is determined but not sealed.  Sealing is a
condition on input and determinedness a condition on output, and determination
can arrive without any commitment.  Inputs incomparable with $i$ are unaffected
by sealing it.

\emph{Determination} is also the name for the process by which a residual
becomes determined.  Input events may determine a residual on their own, as in
Figure~\ref{fig:election-frames}(c); when they do not, the system narrows it
through commitments.  With the current input fixed, a determination by
commitment is a sequence
\[
  (i,R_0)\xrightarrow{p_1}(i,R_1)
  \xrightarrow{p_2}\cdots\xrightarrow{p_m}(i,R_m)
\]
of applicable commitments, empty when the state is already determined.  Only
the commitments are the system's doing, and only they will be counted.

\paragraph{Exposure readiness.}
Exposing $o$ at $i$ promises that whatever is finally produced for $i$, or
for any refinement of $i$, will refine $o$.  The promise is already kept when
$o$ lies below everything in the upper fiber.

\begin{definition}[Exposure-ready output]
\label{def:exposure-ready}
For the current input $i$ of $(i,R)$ and $o\in R(i)$, the output $o$ is
\emph{exposure-ready at $i$} when
        \begin{equation}
  (j,o')\in R\ \text{and}\ i\InputOrd j
  \quad\Longrightarrow\quad
  o\OutputOrd o'.
  \label{eq:exposure-ready}
        \end{equation}
Equivalently, $o$ is admitted at $i$ and is the least element of the upper
fiber $R^\uparrow(i)$.  The first qualification cannot be dropped: a least
element of the upper fiber that occurs only at a proper refinement of $i$ is
not exposable at $i$.
\end{definition}

Readiness is not a second permission to expose.  The one permission is
exposability (Definition~\ref{def:transition-system}), and readiness is the
case in which the exposure event changes nothing.

\begin{proposition}[Readiness is identity exposure]
\label{prop:readiness-identity}
For the current input $i$ of $(i,R)$ and $o\in R(i)$, the output $o$ is
exposure-ready at $i$ if and only if $o$ is exposable at $i$ and
$R\mathbin{\upharpoonright_O}o=R$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
If $o$ is ready, every $x\in R(j)$ with $i\InputOrd j$ satisfies
$o\OutputOrd x$, so $u=x$ witnesses Equation~\ref{eq:safe-exposure}, and the
restriction retains every row.  Conversely, if the restriction retains every
row, then every $x\in R(j)$ refines $o$, and since every valid $j$ refines
$i$, that is Equation~\ref{eq:exposure-ready}.
\end{proof}

A ready output may therefore be exposed at any time, removing no row; and
since input events and commitments only remove rows, every output retained
later at $i$ or its valid refinements continues to refine $o$.  In the election
$\bot$ is ready in every panel and $a$ in panel (c); under the equality order
nothing is ready in panels (a) and (b).  That contrast is the content of the
next theorem.

\subsection{The Functional CALM theorem}
\label{sec:functional-calm-theorem}

A distributed implementation assigns partial input and computation to
participants.  A participant holding input $i$ has reached a residual state by
input events alone, with no commitment made and nothing sealed.  We say that
a functional specification permits \emph{incremental exposure without
sealing} when, in every residual state reachable by input events alone,
$f(i)$ is exposure-ready at every current input $i$
(Definition~\ref{def:exposure-ready}).

\begin{theorem}[Functional CALM]
\label{thm:functional-calm}
A functional ordered specification permits incremental exposure without
sealing if and only if it is monotone.  In that case a current input $i$ is
determined exactly when $f(j)=f(i)$ for every valid refinement $j$ of $i$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Let $(i,R)$ be reachable by input events alone.  Input events remove only rows at
retired inputs, so every $j\succeq_I i$ retains its row $(j,f(j))$, and the
upper fiber is $R^\uparrow(i)=\{f(j):j\succeq_I i\}$.

If $f$ is monotone, then $f(i)\OutputOrd f(j)$ for every such $j$, which is
Equation~\ref{eq:exposure-ready}.  The determinedness claim follows because
the upper fiber is the set of those $f(j)$.

If $f$ is not monotone, some $i\InputOrd j$ satisfies
$f(i)\not\OutputOrd f(j)$.  In the canonical initial state $\sigma_0(i)$,
the input $j$ is valid, so $f(j)\in R^\uparrow(i)$ and $f(i)$ is not
exposure-ready.  The fiber at $i$ offers no output choice to resolve: its only
row is $(i,f(i))$.  Before $f(i)$ can be exposed, the implementation must
therefore carry out a determination whose input-sealing commitments remove the
row at $j$ and at every other refinement whose output does not refine $f(i)$.
A determination that effects a full seal at $i$ is sufficient; closing only the
offending refinements may also suffice.  If other participants can
independently admit $j$, making the closure binding requires their agreement.
Without input-sealing authority, $f(i)$ cannot be exposed at this point.
\end{proof}

The election's output order is not a lattice: $a$ and $b$ have no common upper
bound.  None is required.  Monotonicity constrains only comparable inputs, and
$aa\cdot$ and $ab\cdot$ are incomparable with no common refinement, because
ballots are immutable.  Where two inputs do have a common refinement $j$,
monotonicity makes $f(j)$ an upper bound of both outputs.

This is the familiar CALM dichotomy stated at the semantic interface rather than
inside a particular programming language.  Classical CALM fits the pattern with
a familiar pair of orders: inputs are finite fact sets $J$ ordered by
inclusion, and outputs are answer sets $Q(J)$, also ordered by inclusion.
Equation~\ref{eq:functional-monotonicity} becomes
        \begin{equation}
  J\subseteq J'
  \quad\Longrightarrow\quad
  Q(J)\subseteq Q(J'),
  \label{eq:query-monotonicity}
        \end{equation}
the standard definition of a monotone query~\cite{abiteboul1995foundations}.
Positive Datalog queries satisfy this condition.  Negation may not: an answer
derived from the absence of a fact can be invalidated when that fact arrives.
Relational-transducer CALM connects query monotonicity to coordination-free
programs in its operational model~\cite{ameloot2013relational,ameloot2015weaker}.
Functional CALM generalizes the semantic statement from fact sets under
inclusion to arbitrary input and output refinement partial orders.

The usual state-based CRDT interface has the same functional shape.  Let $U$ be
the immutable joinable delta or state payloads received so far, ordered by
inclusion, and define $s(U)=s_0\sqcup\bigsqcup U$.  The semilattice order is
the output-refinement order.  This function is monotone, while lattice-join provides a
canonical refinement when replicas have seen different partial inputs
~\cite{shapiro2011crdt}.  Section~\ref{sec:crdt-instance} returns to what this
stronger algebra provides.

Historically, CALM stands for \emph{Consistency As Logical Monotonicity}.
Here, ``logical'' refers not to a particular logic or programming language, but
to preservation of semantic claims under declared information-refinement
orders.  Functional CALM states that condition for any functional semantic
specification, regardless of the formalism used to express it.  Once
specifications become relational, the monotonicity in question is monotonicity
under an order lifted from outputs to sets of outputs, and the acronym can be
read as \emph{Consistency As Lifted Monotonicity}.

Functional CALM handles uncertainty about input arrival.  We next allow the
specification itself to offer several outputs for one input.

\section{Complete CALM: Semantic Choice and Coordination}
\label{sec:complete-calm}

A semantic specification may admit more than one output for the same input.
Leader election may allow either eligible leader, and serializability may allow
several transaction orders.  Such specifications are relational rather than
functional.  Complete CALM extends Functional CALM to this semantic output choice.

\subsection{Relational specifications and output fibers}
\label{sec:relational-specifications}

\begin{definition}[Ordered relational specification]
\label{def:relational-specification}
An \emph{ordered relational specification} is
        \begin{equation}
  \Spec=(I,\InputOrd,O,\OutputOrd,S),
  \qquad S\subseteq I\times O,
  \label{eq:relational-specification}
        \end{equation}
where $(I,\InputOrd)$ and $(O,\OutputOrd)$ are partially ordered sets.  A row
$(i,o)\in S$ says that output $o$ is admissible at input $i$.  Its fiber
$\fiber{i}$, as in Equation~\ref{eq:fiber}, may now contain several outputs.
The original specification has a nonempty fiber at every input under discussion.
A functional specification is the special case $\fiber{i}=\{f(i)\}$.
\end{definition}

The two partial orders retain their earlier meanings: $\InputOrd$ compares
inputs, $\OutputOrd$ compares outputs, and neither orders rows of $S$.

The output-refinement order lifts from individual outputs to nonempty sets of
outputs.  For nonempty $A,B\subseteq O$, define
        \begin{equation}
  A\LiftedOutputOrd B
  \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
  \forall a\in A\;\exists b\in B:\;a\OutputOrd b.
  \label{eq:lifted-output-order}
        \end{equation}
This lifted relation is a preorder: every output in the first set can continue
to some output in the second.  It is often called the Hoare preorder.

\begin{definition}[Lifted Monotonicity]
\label{def:lifted-monotonicity}
An ordered relational specification satisfies \emph{Lifted Monotonicity},
the \emph{growth} condition, when the mapping $i\mapsto\fiber{i}$ is monotone
under the lifted output preorder:
        \begin{equation}
  i\InputOrd j
  \quad\Longrightarrow\quad
  \fiber{i}\LiftedOutputOrd\fiber{j}.
  \label{eq:lifted-monotonicity}
        \end{equation}
Equivalently, whenever $i\InputOrd j$ and $o\in\fiber{i}$, some
$o'\in\fiber{j}$ satisfies $o\OutputOrd o'$.
\end{definition}

For singleton fibers this reduces to functional monotonicity,
Equation~\ref{eq:functional-monotonicity}.  Growth governs how outputs continue
\emph{between} fibers as input grows.  It says nothing about how different
outputs relate \emph{within} one fiber.  We now turn to that second question.

\begin{definition}[Compatibility]
\label{def:compatibility}
Outputs $o_1,\ldots,o_k$ are \emph{compatible at input $i$} if there is an
admitted common refinement $u\in\fiber{i}$ such that $o_r\OutputOrd u$ for
every $r$.  Otherwise they are \emph{incompatible at $i$}.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Directed fiber; Directed Outputs]
\label{def:fiber-directedness}
A fiber $F\subseteq O$ is \emph{directed} when every nonempty finite
$X\subseteq F$ has an admitted common refinement: some $u\in F$ with
$x\OutputOrd u$ for all $x\in X$.  An ordered relational specification has
\emph{Directed Outputs}, the \emph{coherence} condition, when every fiber
$\fiber{i}$ is directed; that is, every nonempty finite collection in
$\fiber{i}$ is compatible at $i$.
\end{definition}

A singleton fiber is directed.  So is any fiber closed under a join operation,
such as a set of fact sets closed under union.  A fiber containing two
incomparable outputs with nothing admitted above both is not.  Compatibility is
not comparability: Figure~\ref{fig:orders} shows an output chain, two
incomparable but compatible outputs, and two incompatible outputs.  Only the
last pair lacks a common refinement.

\subsection{Nondirected ambiguous specifications}

The traceroute specification admits every finite combination of its compatible
observations.  Other specifications admit alternatives that cannot be jointly
refined.  Leader election is a simple example.  If \texttt{server1} and
\texttt{server2} are eligible and the interface permits either as the sole
leader, no allowed output refines both choices.  The output fiber is not
directed.

A serial commit log gives a database example.  Suppose concurrent transactions
$T_1$ and $T_2$ both commit.  For the same completed input history, the log may
admit either $[T_1,T_2]$ or $[T_2,T_1]$.  Prefix refinement cannot preserve both
orders, so this fiber is also not directed.  Section~\ref{sec:hat-instance}
gives a stronger witness in which exposed reads force opposite serialization
orders.

\subsection{The Complete CALM criterion}
\label{sec:complete-calm-criterion}

Complete CALM has two formal requirements: Lifted Monotonicity and
Directed Outputs.  A specification satisfying both is called monotone.

\begin{definition}[Complete CALM criterion; monotone specification]
\label{def:complete-calm-criterion}
An ordered relational specification satisfies the \emph{Complete CALM
criterion}, and is called \emph{monotone}, when it has both of the following
properties:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
  \item \emph{Lifted Monotonicity (growth):} the mapping $i\mapsto\fiber{i}$
        is monotone under $\LiftedOutputOrd$, as in
        Equation~\ref{eq:lifted-monotonicity}; and
  \item \emph{Directed Outputs (coherence):} every output fiber is directed
        under $\OutputOrd$, as in Definition~\ref{def:fiber-directedness}.
\end{enumerate}
\end{definition}

The first clause concerns refinement across inputs; the second concerns
outputs admitted at one fixed input.  Neither implies the other.  This
two-clause form isolates the distinct obligations, but an execution may already
have exposed several outputs at different earlier inputs.  To continue without
invalidating any of them, one future output must refine the whole finite family.
The following equivalent form packages exactly that combined obligation and is
the form used in the exposure analysis below.  A \emph{common input
refinement} of inputs $i_1,\ldots,i_k$ is an input $j$ with $i_r\InputOrd j$
for every $r$; it is \emph{legal} when $\fiber{j}\neq\varnothing$, which every
input under discussion is by Definition~\ref{def:relational-specification}.

\begin{proposition}[Finite-family form]
\label{prop:complete-calm-decomposition}
An ordered relational specification is monotone if and only if, for every
nonempty finite family of rows $(i_1,o_1),\ldots,(i_k,o_k)\in S$ and every
common input refinement $j$ of $i_1,\ldots,i_k$, there is a $u\in\fiber{j}$
such that
        \begin{equation}
  o_r\OutputOrd u
  \qquad\text{for every }r\in\{1,\ldots,k\}.
  \label{eq:complete-calm-criterion}
        \end{equation}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Assume the Complete CALM criterion.  Lifted Monotonicity gives, for each row,
$o'_r\in\fiber{j}$ with $o_r\OutputOrd o'_r$.  Directed Outputs gives
$u\in\fiber{j}$ refining every $o'_r$, and hence every $o_r$.

Conversely, assume the finite-family condition.  To prove Lifted Monotonicity,
fix arbitrary $i\InputOrd j$ and $o\in\fiber{i}$.  Apply the condition to the
singleton family $(i,o)$ and its common input refinement $j$.  It yields
$u\in\fiber{j}$ with $o\OutputOrd u$, exactly
$\fiber{i}\LiftedOutputOrd\fiber{j}$.

To prove Directed Outputs, fix arbitrary $i$ and a nonempty finite collection
$o_1,\ldots,o_k\in\fiber{i}$.  Apply the condition to
$(i,o_1),\ldots,(i,o_k)$ with common input refinement $j=i$.  It yields
$u\in\fiber{i}$ refining every $o_r$.
\end{proof}

The condition ranges over every common input refinement because the environment
may determine which refinement occurs.  Finite families are required because
pairwise compatibility need not imply compatibility of a larger family.
Growth in $\InputOrd$ leads to extension in $\OutputOrd$: whatever was
admissible on the parts remains jointly refinable on the whole.  The fibers of
a monotone specification grow with the input the way the values of a monotone
function do, widened to sets of mutually compatible alternatives.

\subsection{Commitments under output choice}
\label{sec:residuals-commitment}

The transition system of Section~\ref{sec:determination} applies unchanged to
a relational specification; the difference is that a fiber $R(i)$ may now
contain several outputs, so the second kind of commitment in
Definition~\ref{def:transition-system}, the choice commitment, has work to do.
Sealing input removes rows at inputs that will never become current.  Choosing
an output removes rows at inputs that remain valid: in leader election,
committing to \texttt{server1} removes the row for \texttt{server2} at the same
input.

The basis is part of the system model.  Its members are the acts through which
the system can make possibilities irrevocably unavailable, and the system can
use only the commitments it declares.  In particular, the ability to choose
among outputs does not by itself include the ability to seal input; that
requires an input-sealing commitment in the basis.  The two exercise different
authority: a choice commitment speaks only for the system's own output, while
an input-sealing commitment declares that input the environment could still
supply will not count.  A commitment may remove many rows at once.

Whether removing a row destroys information depends on the output order.

\begin{definition}[Content-preserving and content-destroying restrictions]
\label{def:content-restriction}
Let $(i,R)$ and $(i,R')$ be residual states with $R'\subsetneq R$, and let $k$
be valid in both.  The restriction is \emph{content-preserving at $k$} when
every removed output has a retained refinement:
        \begin{equation}
  \forall x\in R(k)\setminus R'(k)\quad
  \exists u\in R'(k),\qquad x\OutputOrd u.
  \label{eq:content-preserving}
        \end{equation}
It is \emph{content-destroying at $k$} otherwise: some removed $x$ has no
refinement left in $R'(k)$, and the content of $x$ has become unattainable.  A
restriction is content-preserving when it is so at every input valid in both
states, and content-destroying when it is content-destroying at some such
input.
\end{definition}

Under the equality order on outputs, no distinct output refines another, so
every strict restriction is content-destroying; so is every strict restriction
of the leader-election fiber, whose two outputs have no common refinement.  The
distinction matters in a fiber like traceroute's, where a restriction may
remove an output while a retained output still carries everything it said.
The next subsection shows that such restrictions are not commitments at all.

\subsection{Exposure}
\label{sec:safe-exposure}

Consider exposing $o\in R(i)$ to an external observer.  The exposure promises
that later output at $i$ and at every refinement of $i$ will refine $o$.  When
$o$ is exposure-ready (Definition~\ref{def:exposure-ready}), the promise is
already entailed by the residual and the exposure changes nothing
(Proposition~\ref{prop:readiness-identity}).  Readiness
asks that $o$ already lie below everything that remains possible.  A weaker
condition suffices for an exposure to be honest: that nothing remaining possible
\emph{contradicts} $o$, in the sense that every remaining possibility shares an
admitted refinement with it.  A participant that exposes such an $o$ undertakes
to refine it, and it can always keep that undertaking, whatever else remains,
because the undertaking excludes nothing that could not still be reached.
Keeping the promise means restricting the residual at $i$ and its refinements
to the outputs that refine $o$.  That is the exposure event
$\mathsf{expose}(o)$ of Definition~\ref{def:transition-system}, which
produces $R\mathbin{\upharpoonright_O}o$
(Equation~\ref{eq:residual-refining-fibers}) and is permitted exactly when $o$
is exposable at $i$ (Equation~\ref{eq:safe-exposure}): compatible with every
possibility at $i$, in that every remaining output at $i$ or a valid refinement
shares an admitted refinement with $o$.  Exposability has two requirements, and
neither implies the other.  Admission at $i$, $o\in R(i)$, rules out exposing
a value that only some refinement of $i$ admits, before the input that would
admit it has arrived.  Compatibility rules out exposing an admitted value that
a still-valid refinement contradicts: under the equality order, $\bot$ is
admitted in panel (a) of the election but shares no refinement with the $a$
and $b$ admitted at $aba$ and $abb$.

\begin{proposition}[Exposure narrows without committing]
\label{prop:exposure-narrowing}
Let $o$ be exposable at $i$ in $(i,R)$ and let $R'=R\mathbin{\upharpoonright_O}o$.
Then $(i,R')$ is a residual state with the same valid inputs as $(i,R)$; the
restriction from $R$ to $R'$ is content-preserving
(Definition~\ref{def:content-restriction}); and $o$ is exposure-ready at $i$ in
$(i,R')$.  If $o$ was already exposure-ready in $(i,R)$, then $R'=R$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Let $j$ be valid with $i\InputOrd j$ and let $x\in R(j)$.  Exposability supplies
$u\in R(j)$ above both $o$ and $x$.  Since $u$ refines $o$, it is retained in
$R'(j)$.  Thus every fiber at or above $i$ remains nonempty, no input becomes
invalid, and every removed $x$ has the retained refinement $u$.  Every
retained output refines
$o$, which is readiness.  If $o$ was already least, nothing fails to refine it
and nothing is removed.
\end{proof}

An exposure event is therefore not a commitment, even though it may remove rows:
it excludes nothing whose content could not still be reached.  If $o$ is not
exposable, some $x$ at $i$ or one of its refinements has no admitted refinement in
common with $o$.  Exposing $o$ would then silently destroy the content of $x$.
The system must instead make a content-destroying commitment explicitly,
reducing $R$ to a residual in which $o$ may become exposable.

When $\OutputOrd$ is equality, an output is exposable only if it is the sole
possibility, and exposure reduces to announcing a determined result.  When every
upper fiber has a least element, that element is exposable and its exposure
changes nothing; this is the case of a monotone functional specification.  The
new case is a fiber such as $\{a,b,t\}$ with $a$ and $b$ incomparable and both
below $t$: neither $a$ nor $b$ is ready, but each is exposable, and exposing
$a$ narrows the fiber to $\{a,t\}$ while leaving the content of $b$ reachable
at $t$.

It is worth following one exposure carefully.  Suppose traceroute first exposes
$o=\{s{\to}\texttt{server1}\}$.  The output
$x=\{s{\to}a\}$ is incomparable with $o$: neither refines the other.  The
restriction therefore removes $(i,x)$.  At first this may look like a choice
against $x$, but the residual retains
$u=\{s{\to}\texttt{server1},s{\to}a,
      s{\to}a{\to}\texttt{server2}\}$.  Because $u$ refines both $o$ and
$x$, all information in $x$ remains represented; the removal is
content-preserving, not a commitment.  Leader election behaves differently.
Selecting \texttt{server1} removes \texttt{server2} with no retained common
refinement, so that removal is a content-destroying commitment.

\paragraph{Runs and schedules.}
A \emph{run} of a participant is a finite sequence of transitions from a
canonical initial state: input events supplied by the environment,
interleaved with the participant's own exposure events and commitments.  Its
\emph{schedule} is the sequence of inputs the environment supplies.  Two
schedules are \emph{input-equivalent} when they end at the same input; they may
differ in the intermediate inputs they pass through, which under a change
presentation means in the order and grouping of arrivals and in repeated
delivery.  By the composition property of input events, runs with input-equivalent
schedules that neither expose nor commit reach the same state.  What can
depend on the schedule is what a participant exposes or commits between its
input events.  A replica is a participant that sees a shared input stream in its own
order, so several replicas and several runs of one participant under
input-equivalent schedules are the same object seen twice.

\paragraph{Histories.}
Runs of several participants fit together into a history in Lamport's sense.
A \emph{distributed history} $H$ is a set of events, each at one participant,
with each participant's events totally ordered, each receive matched to a
send, and \emph{happens-before} the transitive closure of local order and
matching.  A receive that advances the receiving participant's input is an
input event; a receive that does not leaves the residual unchanged, as
does the exposure of a ready output (Proposition~\ref{prop:readiness-identity}).  A
\emph{consistent cut} $C$ is a subset of events closed under happens-before.
For each participant $p$, the events of $C$ at $p$, in local order, form a run;
write $\sigma_p^C$ for its final residual state and
$\operatorname{end}(H,C)=(\sigma_p^C)_{p}$ for the vector of residuals, the
\emph{semantic endpoint} of the cut.

\begin{proposition}[Residuals are the semantic quotient of histories]
\label{prop:residual-quotient}
Fix a specification, a participant set, and a commitment basis.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item $\operatorname{end}(H,C)$ is the same for every replay of the events
        of $C$ in an order that respects happens-before.
  \item Whether a transition is applicable at participant $p$ after $(H,C)$,
        and the residual it produces, depend only on $\sigma_p^C$.
  \item Consequently, pointed histories $(H,C)$ and $(H',C')$ with equal
        endpoints admit the same semantic continuations, and the endpoint map
        induces a bijection between its equivalence classes and the reachable
        residual vectors.
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Each coordinate $\sigma_p^C$ is determined by $p$'s events in local order,
which every happens-before-respecting replay preserves, and events at
different participants act on different coordinates; receives that advance no
input change no coordinate.  This gives (1).  Input events, exposure events,
and commitments are each partial functions of the local residual, which gives
(2).  Then (3) is (2) applied coordinate by coordinate, together with the
observation that any function induces a bijection between the classes of its
kernel equivalence and its image.
\end{proof}

The proposition is what licenses the definitions below to speak of residuals
rather than executions.  Everything the specification cares about in a Lamport
diagram survives in its cut's residuals; message order, batching, and who has
learned what are forgotten exactly when they do not advance anyone's input.
Run insensitivity and reconciliation on merge, stated next over residuals, are
therefore statements about executions.

Read in the other direction, the proposition is a design criterion.  Choosing
the input domain and its order decides which histories the semantics treats as
the same: two cuts yield one residual exactly when they reach the same input
and have removed the same rows.  A finer input order forgets less of the
execution and obliges the specification to say more; a coarser one forgets
more.  The specification then states admissible outcomes over residuals,
without re-encoding the history it was built to forget, and the proposition
says that nothing it can ask is lost in doing so.

A reader who thinks in state machines can compile one into this form.  At the
finest grain, an input is the sequence of environment events the machine has
consumed, ordered by extension, and a row $(i,o)$ is admissible when some run
of the machine on $i$ produces $o$; the register example of
Section~\ref{sec:cap-corollary} is of this kind.  If the machine's state
summarizes its past, so that two event sequences reaching the same state have
the same futures, the states themselves may serve as inputs, ordered by
reachability, provided that order is antisymmetric.  That is the coarsening the
proposition licenses, and the election of Section~\ref{sec:nutshell} is the
result for a vote-counting machine whose state is the set of ballots received.

In the rest of this section, \emph{reachable} means reachable from a canonical
initial state by input events and exposure events alone, with no commitment.

\paragraph{Coordination-freedom.}
The CAP intuition has two halves.  Availability: a replica can answer from the
input it has, without waiting to learn what other replicas have received or
exposed.  Consistency: answers given independently reconcile when the
replicas' inputs are combined.  Both must hold whatever the arrival order.  The
definition below states the three resulting conditions, each as a property of
residual states.

\begin{definition}[Coordination-free specification]
\label{def:coordination-free}
An ordered relational specification is \emph{coordination-free} when the
following hold.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \emph{Exposure at will.}  At every reachable state, every output
        admitted at a current input is exposable there
        (Definition~\ref{def:transition-system}).
  \item \emph{Reconciliation on merge.}  Whenever participants hold reachable
        states $(i_1,R_1),\ldots,(i_k,R_k)$ and $j$ is a legal common
        refinement of their current inputs $i_1,\ldots,i_k$, some row at $j$
        survives in every state:
        \begin{equation}
          \bigcap_{r=1}^{k}R_r(j)\neq\varnothing.
          \label{eq:reconciliation}
        \end{equation}
  \item \emph{Run insensitivity.}  For finitely many runs of one participant
        under input-equivalent schedules, each exposing as it pleases and none
        committing, every scheduled input event is defined, and at the shared
        final input $a$ some row survives in every run.
\end{enumerate}
\end{definition}

Condition~1 is the availability half: a participant may expose any admissible
output at its current input, and the exposure is entirely its own to make.
Condition~2 is the consistency half: whatever separate participants exposed on
their separate inputs, there remains an admissible output at every legal
combination of those inputs that refines all of their exposures, so that merging
the participants neither empties a fiber nor forbids the combined input.
Condition~3 restates the second for a single participant that could have seen
its inputs in another order, grouped differently, or with repeats; it makes the
outcome a property of the inputs rather than of the schedule.  None of the
three requires that participants expose the \emph{same} output.  They require
that whatever was exposed can stand together.  The definition is semantic: it
describes no protocol and promises no delivery, liveness, or termination
detection.

\begin{theorem}[Complete CALM]
\label{thm:complete-calm}
For an ordered relational specification $\Spec$, the following are equivalent:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item $\Spec$ is coordination-free;
  \item $\Spec$ satisfies exposure at will;
  \item $\Spec$ satisfies the Complete CALM criterion.
\end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
(3$\Rightarrow$1.)  We first establish a claim: at every reachable state
$(i,R)$ and every valid $j$ with $i\InputOrd j$, the
residual fiber $R(j)$ is a nonempty \emph{upper} subset of $\fiber{j}$---closed
under taking refinements within $\fiber{j}$---that contains a common refinement
of any $o\in R(i)$ and any $x\in R(j)$.  Reachable states
arise from a canonical initial state, whose fibers are whole fibers of $S$, by
input events, which leave surviving fibers unchanged, and by exposure events,
which retain exactly the outputs above an exposed value.  An intersection of
upper sets is upper, so $R(j)$ is upper in $\fiber{j}$.  It is nonempty because
$j$ is valid.  Given $o$ and $x$, the finite-family form applied to
$(i,o),(j,x)$ with common refinement $j$ gives $u\in\fiber{j}$ above both, and
upperness puts $u$ in $R(j)$.

\emph{Exposure at will} is the claim read with $j$ and $x$ ranging over the
possibilities at $i$.

\emph{Reconciliation on merge.}  Let $j$ be a legal common refinement of the
current inputs $i_1,\ldots,i_k$.  The input $j$ is valid in every $(i_r,R_r)$:
it had a row initially, it refines $i_r$ and so was never retired by an
input event, and by Proposition~\ref{prop:exposure-narrowing} no exposure sealed
it off.  Each participant $r$ has removed rows only at $i_r$ and its refinements,
and $i_r\InputOrd j$, so $R_r(j)$ is a nonempty upper subset of $\fiber{j}$.
Choose $x_r\in R_r(j)$ for each $r$.  The finite-family form for
$(j,x_1),\ldots,(j,x_k)$ supplies $u\in\fiber{j}$ above every $x_r$, and
upperness places $u$ in every $R_r(j)$.  Hence the intersection is nonempty.

\emph{Run insensitivity.}  Each scheduled input event is defined because no exposure
empties a fiber at a valid input.  Runs under input-equivalent schedules end
at the same input $a$, which is a common refinement of the inputs at which any
run exposed, so the reconciliation argument applies with the runs in place of
the participants.

(1$\Rightarrow$2) holds by definition.

(2$\Rightarrow$3.)  By Proposition~\ref{prop:complete-calm-decomposition} it
suffices to prove growth and coherence.  \emph{Growth.}  Let $i\InputOrd j$
and $o\in\fiber{i}$.  In the canonical initial state $\sigma_0(i)$, the
input $j$ is valid and $\fiber{j}$ is nonempty; exposure at will makes $o$
exposable, so any $x\in\fiber{j}$ has a common refinement with $o$ in
$\fiber{j}$, which in particular lies above $o$.  \emph{Coherence.}  Suppose
some finite $X\subseteq\fiber{i}$ has no admitted common refinement, and choose
such an $X=\{x_1,\ldots,x_n\}$ of least size.  If $n=1$ then $x_1$ itself is a
refinement; so $n\geq2$.  If $n=2$, then $x_1$ is not exposable at
$\sigma_0(i)$, since $x_2$ is a possibility at $i$ sharing no admitted
refinement with it.  If $n\geq3$, a participant holding $i$ exposes
$x_1,\ldots,x_{n-2}$ in turn; each is exposable by hypothesis, and the
resulting state is reachable with residual fiber at $i$ the set of outputs
above all of $x_1,\ldots,x_{n-2}$.  By minimality of $X$ this fiber contains a
common refinement $w'$ of $x_1,\ldots,x_{n-1}$ and a common refinement $w''$
of $x_1,\ldots,x_{n-2},x_n$.  A common refinement of $w'$ and $w''$ would
refine all of $X$, so none is admitted, and $w'$ is not exposable in that
state.  In every case exposure at will fails.
\end{proof}

The theorem is direct from the definitions.  Its content is the
identification: the one semantic obstruction to exposing at will, to
reconciliation on merge, and to schedule independence is a finite family of
admissible rows with no admitted common refinement at a common input
refinement.  When none exists, replicas may expose what they have, in whatever
order it arrives, and remain reconcilable, though what they exposed may
differ.  When such a family exists, some admissible output cannot be exposed
without excluding another outright, that is, without a commitment.  Computing
a common refinement and guaranteeing termination remain protocol questions.

Why did earlier CALM results not state Directed Outputs separately?  Their
semantic query specification is functional: each input $J$ has one result
$Q(J)$, so each output fiber is a singleton and directed automatically.
State-based CRDTs also present a functional state for a fixed set of incorporated
updates; semilattice join additionally provides least common refinements across
partial inputs.  Complete CALM makes multiple semantic outputs at one input explicit,
so directedness can no longer be assumed.

\subsection{Coordination, deployment, and the Complete CAP Theorem}
\label{sec:cap-corollary}

Exposure narrows without excluding and can be done by every participant at
will.  A commitment excludes content, and the next result says what that costs
when several participants hold the same input.

\begin{definition}[Coordination]
\label{def:coordination}
\emph{Coordination} is agreement among participants, obtained before a
commitment is made, that no participant will select an alternative the
commitment excludes.
\end{definition}

\begin{theorem}[Commitments require coordination]
\label{thm:commitments-require-coordination}
Suppose the participants share a common commitment basis.  Let a basis action
$d$ be applicable at a reachable state $\sigma=(i,R)$ and content-destroying at
the current input $i$, excluding some $x\in R(i)$ that has no refinement in
$R'(i)$, where $(i,R')=d(\sigma)$.  Then there is a participant state $\tau$,
reachable from the canonical initial state by the same input events followed
by exposures or commitments of its own, whose residual at $i$ consists of
refinements of $x$; and $d(\sigma)$ and $\tau$ have no common row at $i$.
Consequently $d$ cannot be applied without agreement that no participant takes
the course leading to $\tau$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Let a second participant receive the same input events as the first, reaching a
state with the same current input and with $x\in\fiber{i}$ still admitted.  If $x$
is exposable there, it exposes $x$; otherwise it applies a restriction
retaining exactly the outputs above $x$ at $i$ and its refinements---a
commitment, applicable because $x$ itself remains.  This second action is a
member of the shared basis: the first participant's own action $d$ is a
content-destroying commitment at $i$ drawn from that basis, and the
common-basis hypothesis makes the same commitments available to every
participant holding the same input.  Either way its residual at $i$ is
contained in $\{u\in\fiber{i}:x\OutputOrd u\}$.  The first participant's
residual $R'(i)$ contains no such $u$ by hypothesis.  The intersection at $i$
is therefore empty: the two states cannot be merged, and no later input event
or exposure can restore a removed row.  The only way to avoid this outcome is
for the second participant not to select $x$, which it can be relied upon to
do only by agreement.
\end{proof}

Together the two theorems make the division exact.  A monotone specification is
one in which exposure is all that is ever needed.  A commitment excludes
content that some other participant could admissibly have selected, and it can
never be made unilaterally.  A determination that effects a seal uses
input-sealing commitments to rule out further input refinements; selecting
among incompatible outputs uses choice commitments to rule out alternatives at
a fixed input.  Functional specifications need only the first kind; Complete
CALM accounts for both.  If a system forbids some rows of $S$, its declared
interface is a proper subrelation $S'\subsetneq S$, and the criterion applies
to $S'$.

CAP asks the same question in a more specific setting.  A \emph{network
partition} divides the participants into two or more groups and stops all
information from crossing between groups until the partition heals.  During the
partition each participant continues to receive input from its own
environment.  \emph{Availability} asks that a participant be able to expose an
admissible output at the input it holds without waiting for anything from
across the cut.  \emph{Consistency} asks that the exposures made on different
sides remain jointly refinable when the partition heals and the inputs are
combined.  \emph{Partition tolerance} asks for both under every partition and
every schedule of arrivals on each side.  The classical CAP theorem is a
one-way impossibility: a linearizable register cannot remain available through
arbitrary partitions~\cite{gilbert2002cap}.  Complete CALM turns it into a
dichotomy.

\begin{theorem}[Complete CAP]
\label{thm:complete-cap}
For an ordered relational specification $\Spec$, the following are equivalent:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item under every partition and every schedule of inputs on each side, every
        participant may expose any admissible output at its current input
        (\emph{availability}), and for every legal common refinement $j$ of the
        current inputs on the two sides, some row at $j$ survives in every
        participant's state (\emph{consistency});
  \item $\Spec$ is monotone.
\end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Condition~1 asks for exposure at will, reconciliation on merge, and run
insensitivity, restricted to the participants and schedules that a partition
can produce.  Every finite family of participants and every finite set of
input-equivalent schedules can be so produced: place the participants on
either side of a cut and let each side's environment deliver its inputs in the
chosen order.  Condition~1 is therefore the conjunction of the three conditions
of Definition~\ref{def:coordination-free}, and Theorem~\ref{thm:complete-calm}
makes it equivalent to monotonicity.
\end{proof}

Here consistency means refinement consistency: exposures on different sides of
the partition are jointly refinable under $\OutputOrd$, not identical or
totally ordered.  This is weaker than linearizability and is what makes the
characterization bidirectional.  Monotone specifications retain availability
and refinement consistency under partition.  Non-monotone ones must surrender
one, and the theorem identifies the family of rows responsible: a participant
that would select one of its members must commit, and by
Theorem~\ref{thm:commitments-require-coordination} it must either wait to agree
with the other side, giving up availability, or commit alone and risk an
unreconcilable merge, giving up consistency.

Theorem~\ref{thm:complete-cap} quantifies over all participants and inputs.
Whether a commitment is necessary is a semantic question; whether it requires
agreement across a cut also depends on \emph{deployment}: which participant may
hold which input and select which row.  A participant that controls an entire
choice may commit locally.  In what follows, a participant \emph{selects} an
admissible output at the input it holds by exposing it when it is exposable
and by committing to it---retaining exactly the outputs that refine it at its
input and that input's refinements---when it is not.  The second kind of
selection is
content-destroying; the deployment says who is entitled to make it.

\begin{definition}[Deployment; separable family]
\label{def:deployment}
A \emph{deployment} over a participant set $P$ assigns to each participant $p$
a set $\Delta_p\subseteq S$ of rows it may select; $(i,o)\in\Delta_p$ means
that $p$ may hold $i$ as a current input and select $o$ there, on its own
authority.  A \emph{partition} $\Pi$ of $P$ divides the participants into two
or more nonempty components.  A finite family of rows is \emph{separable under
$\Pi$} when its members can be attributed to participants, each row lying in
its participant's $\Delta_p$, with at least two components represented.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Monotone across partitions]
\label{def:monotone-across-partitions}
Relative to a deployment, $\Spec$ is \emph{monotone across $\Pi$} when every
family separable under $\Pi$, together with every common input refinement $j$
of its inputs, has an admitted common refinement in $\fiber{j}$.  It is
\emph{monotone across partitions} when it is monotone across every partition of
$P$.
\end{definition}

This is the finite-family form restricted to the families a partition can
separate.  Monotonicity implies monotonicity across partitions, since separable
families are families.  The converse fails whenever every incompatible family
lies within one participant's $\Delta_p$.  Two mutually exclusive choices owned
by one participant do not become separable merely because another component
acts.

\begin{theorem}[Complete CAP under a deployment]
\label{thm:complete-cap-deployment}
Fix a deployment.  The following are equivalent.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Under every partition $\Pi$ and every schedule of inputs within each
        component: participants in one component may coordinate with one
        another, but each component selects outputs at the rows its
        participants hold without waiting for information from any other
        component (\emph{availability}); and for every legal common refinement
        $j$ of the current inputs held in different components, some row at
        $j$ survives in every participant's state, so that the components'
        selections reconcile when the partition heals (\emph{consistency}).
  \item $\Spec$ is monotone across partitions.
\end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
(2$\Rightarrow$1.)  Fix $\Pi$.  Families whose members all lie in one component
may be resolved by coordination within that component, which the partition does
not block.  The families that must reconcile without communication are the
separable ones.  Consider participants in at least two components, each having
made finitely many selections at its current input or its refinements, and a
legal common refinement $j$ of those inputs.  The family of all their
selections is separable, so monotonicity across $\Pi$ supplies $u\in\fiber{j}$
above every selected output.  Each participant's residual at $j$ is
$\fiber{j}$ intersected with the outputs above its own selections, and $u$
belongs to every such set.  Hence some row at $j$ survives in every state,
under every schedule on each side.

(1$\Rightarrow$2.)  If monotonicity across some $\Pi$ fails, a separable family
$(i_1,o_1),\ldots,(i_k,o_k)$ and a common refinement $j$ admit no common
refinement in $\fiber{j}$.  Its participants lie in at least two components,
and availability lets each select $o_r$ at $i_r$ without learning the others'
selections.  Each participant's residual at $j$ then lies within the outputs
above its $o_r$, and no output of $\fiber{j}$ is above all of them, so no row
at $j$ survives in every state.  Avoiding this requires either a wait across
the cut, which surrenders availability, or refusing some of the rows, which
implements a narrower specification $S'\subsetneq S$.
\end{proof}

The difference from Theorem~\ref{thm:complete-cap} is that participants on the
same side of a cut are allowed to coordinate.  Only the families that a
partition separates must reconcile without communication, and only those
families are tested.  When the deployment lets every participant hold every
row, every family is separable under some partition, monotonicity across
partitions is monotonicity, and the theorem is Theorem~\ref{thm:complete-cap}.
Deployment determines which inputs and choices can occur independently, just
as data placement does in the relational-transducer model.

\paragraph{Registers.}
Consider two replicas of a register initially holding no value.  Process $p$
completes a write of \dog{} while $q$ concurrently completes a write of
\catv.  The replicas exchange both writes, after which a partition separates
them.  Each then serves a local read; both writes complete before either read
begins.

An input is a finite causally closed operation history, ordered by
induced-subhistory extension.  An output is an explanation $(L,\rho)$, where
$L$ is an operation order admitted by the consistency model and $\rho$ maps each
completed read to the write supplying its response.  Refinement extends $L$
without reversing an existing order and extends $\rho$ without changing an
answered read.

For linearizability, $L$ is a total order extending real time
~\cite{herlihy1990linearizability}.  If $p$ returns \catv, its explanation
requires $w_p(x,\dog)<_L w_q(x,\catv)$.  If $q$ returns \dog, its explanation
requires the opposite order.  No total order refines both explanations.  The
partition separates the read owners, recovering the classical CAP impossibility
as the negative side of the Complete CAP theorem
~\cite{gilbert2002cap}.

Under causal consistency, the writes remain concurrent and one explanation can
assign $p$'s read to \catv{} and $q$'s read to \dog{}
~\cite{lamport1978time,ahamad1995causal}.  The responses have a common
refinement.  Two possible answers for the same read remain incompatible, but
only that read's owner selects one; under a deployment in which each
participant holds its own views, no partition separates one local decision
into two independent choices, and the causal register is monotone across
partitions.  With the central results in
place, we now compare their consequences across the surrounding literature.

\subsection{Query monotonicity}
\label{sec:query-instance}

Section~\ref{sec:functional-calm} gave the functional encoding
$J\mapsto Q(J)$.  Relational-transducer CALM proves the operational result for
that language: monotone queries admit coordination-free evaluation under its
data-placement and execution assumptions
~\cite{ameloot2013relational,ameloot2015weaker}.  Functional CALM does not replace
that theorem.  It isolates the semantic order-preservation property so that it
can be compared with interfaces outside Datalog.

\subsection{CRDTs: from convergent state to safe observations}
\label{sec:crdt-instance}

  A CRDT answers one important question: if replicas receive the same updates,
will they converge to equivalent states despite differences in update delivery
and merge order?  Applications immediately ask a second question: what may a
client safely conclude from one replica's current state?  The CRDT merge theorem does not answer this second
question.  Keep CALM and CRDT On called out this gap and proposed monotonicity as
the test for safe queries~\cite{laddad2022keep}.  Functional CALM makes that
test precise for the whole client-visible interface.

To place the distinction in our model, separate the CRDT's semantic state
interface from one way of implementing it.  Let $(D,\sqsubseteq,\sqcup)$ be the
state join-semilattice.  The semantic input is the replica's current state
$d\in D$, and the specified state output is that same value:
        \begin{equation}
  \mathsf{id}_D:(D,\sqsubseteq)\longrightarrow(D,\sqsubseteq),
  \qquad \mathsf{id}_D(d)=d.
  \label{eq:crdt-state-identity}
        \end{equation}
Merging state $e$ advances the input from $d$ to $d\sqcup e$.  The identity
specification is monotone because $d\sqsubseteq d\sqcup e$; moreover, the join
is the least state refining both inputs~\cite{shapiro2011crdt}.

A common implementation represents the current state by the finite set $U$ of
immutable, joinable payloads received so far:
        \begin{equation}
  s(U)=s_0\sqcup\bigsqcup U.
  \label{eq:crdt-state-function}
        \end{equation}
Then $U\subseteq V$ implies $s(U)\sqsubseteq s(V)$, and
$s(U_1)\sqcup s(U_2)=s(U_1\cup U_2)$.

A client observation replaces the identity output with a new semantic promise.

\begin{proposition}[CRDT observation criterion]
\label{prop:crdt-observation}
Let $q:(D,\sqsubseteq)\to(R,\preceq_R)$ be a deterministic observation.  Under
the payload model above, the client-visible interface $F=q\circ s$ permits
incremental exposure without sealing exactly when
        \begin{equation}
  U\subseteq V \quad\Longrightarrow\quad q(s(U))\preceq_R q(s(V)).
  \label{eq:crdt-query-monotonicity}
        \end{equation}
Thus $q$ need only preserve refinements between reachable states; monotonicity
on all of $D$ is sufficient.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
The condition is monotonicity of $q\circ s$, so Functional CALM applies.
\end{proof}

\paragraph{A safe merge can have an unsafe observation.}
Consider a two-phase set with state $(A,R)$: $A$ records additions and $R$
records removals.  Both components grow by union, so replica state merges
perfectly well.  Its user-facing query is
        \begin{equation}
  q_{\mathrm{2P}}(A,R)=A\setminus R,
  \qquad F_{\mathrm{2P}}=q_{\mathrm{2P}}\circ s.
  \label{eq:crdt-2p-query}
        \end{equation}
Suppose the payload set $U_0$ yields
\[
  s(U_0)=(\{\mathsf{potato},\mathsf{Ferrari}\},\varnothing),
  \qquad
  (q_{\mathrm{2P}}\circ s)(U_0)
    =\{\mathsf{potato},\mathsf{Ferrari}\}.
\]
If the application treats membership in this result as a lasting claim, it may
ship the Ferrari.  Now let $U_1$ add a delayed payload removing the Ferrari.
Then $U_0\subseteq U_1$, but
\[
  s(U_1)=(\{\mathsf{potato},\mathsf{Ferrari}\},\{\mathsf{Ferrari}\}),
  \qquad
  (q_{\mathrm{2P}}\circ s)(U_1)=\{\mathsf{potato}\}.
\]
The internal state advanced, but the client-visible claim did not.  An ordinary
point-in-time read may merely expose its chosen local cut.  The bug appears when
the program interprets that answer as complete, stable, or sufficient to justify
an irreversible action.  Such a promise needs a seal on the relevant updates.

\paragraph{Monotone state, nonmonotone query.}
Even a grow-only CRDT can have a nonmonotone application query.  Let
$q_{\mathrm{edible}}$ map a set of ingredients to a Boolean: it returns
$\mathsf{true}$ for $\{\mathsf{apples},\mathsf{honey}\}$, but $\mathsf{false}$
after $\mathsf{bleach}$ is added.  The state function $s$ is monotone under set
inclusion, while $q_{\mathrm{edible}}\circ s$ is not: refining the payload input
can change the result from $\mathsf{true}$ to $\mathsf{false}$.  State
convergence therefore does not establish monotonicity of an application query.

\paragraph{Threshold queries.}
Kuper and Newton's LVars introduced \emph{threshold queries}: Boolean-valued
application queries that become true when a lattice state crosses a threshold
~\cite{kuper2013lvars}.  Order the Boolean codomain by
$\mathsf{false}\preceq_B\mathsf{true}$.  For example, let a grow-only set $d$
record distinct failed login attempts and define
        \begin{equation}
  q_{100}(d)=[\,|d|\geq100\,],
  \qquad Q_{100}=q_{100}\circ s.
  \label{eq:crdt-thresholds}
        \end{equation}
Suppose $Q_{100}(U)=\mathsf{true}$.  For every possible refinement
$V\supseteq U$, the grow-only set still contains at least 100 attempts, so
$Q_{100}(V)=\mathsf{true}$.  This is exactly the exposability test of
Definition~\ref{def:transition-system}: restricting future outputs to those that
refine the exposed $\mathsf{true}$ removes no row and requires no commitment.  Indeed, $\mathsf{true}$ is maximal in the Boolean
output order, so any output refining it must equal $\mathsf{true}$.  Before the
threshold is reached, $\mathsf{false}$ is also exposable because it may
refine to $\mathsf{true}$; it simply says that the replica has not yet observed
100 failures.  The threshold query thus (eventually)
extracts a stable fact from an incomplete replica state without claiming that
the state itself is complete.

\paragraph{Beyond least merges.}
A join-semilattice provides a uniform \emph{least} refinement and a fixed merge
operator.  Complete CALM asks only for \emph{some} admitted common refinement of
each relevant finite family.  For example, suppose two shards expose
$[a_1,a_2]$ and $[b_1,b_2]$, and the output fiber admits every order-preserving
interleaving.  Both
        \begin{equation}
  [a_1,a_2,b_1,b_2]\qquad\text{and}\qquad[b_1,b_2,a_1,a_2]
  \label{eq:log-interleavings}
        \end{equation}
are minimal common refinements, and neither refines the other.  Consequently,
the pair has no least common refinement, so this output order is not a
join-semilattice.  It is nevertheless directed: any finite family of sequences
has an admitted order-preserving interleaving.  The election of
Section~\ref{sec:nutshell} goes further: its outcome order has no upper bound
for $a$ and $b$ at all, and needs none, because the inputs at which they arise
never merge.  A join-semilattice is therefore a restrictive sufficient
discipline, not a requirement of the Complete CALM criterion.

\subsection{Highly available transactions}
\label{sec:hat-instance}

For a transactional interface, an input is the set of begin, read, write, and
commit requests received so far, ordered by inclusion.  An output is a growing
set of public facts such as $\mathsf{read}(T,x,v)$,
$\mathsf{commit}(T)$, and session-order facts, also ordered by inclusion.  The
isolation or session guarantee determines which fact sets are admissible.
A deployment assigns transactions and requests to replicas.  The Complete
CAP Theorem tests the combinations those replicas can select across a partition,
under the contact, failure, and session assumptions of the interface
~\cite{bailis2013hat,terry1994session}.

\paragraph{Accumulating facts.}
Suppose two replicas execute transactions on disjoint keys.  One exposes
$\mathsf{read}(T_1,x,0)$ and $\mathsf{commit}(T_1)$; the other exposes
$\mathsf{read}(T_2,y,0)$ and $\mathsf{commit}(T_2)$.  If each read observes a
committed version at its replica, the union contains no dirty read or write
conflict.  Under the stated read-committed interface, later delivery preserves
all four facts.  This is the positive accumulation pattern behind HAT results;
it is not a claim that every combination of session guarantees is available.

\paragraph{Serializability and transaction ordering.}
The ordering issue becomes a distributed obstruction when exposed reads force
opposite serialization orders.  Initially $x=y=0$.  On one side, $T_1$ reads
$x=0$, writes $y=1$, and commits.
On the other, $T_2$ reads $y=0$, writes $x=1$, and commits.  Preserving $T_1$'s
read requires $T_1$ before $T_2$ in any serial explanation; preserving $T_2$'s
read requires $T_2$ before $T_1$.  The serialization graph contains a cycle, so
no serial history refines both sets of facts~\cite{adya2000generalized}.

\paragraph{Snapshot isolation.}
Let overlapping transactions $T_1$ and $T_2$ read the same snapshot and both
write $x$.  Separate replicas could each expose its local commit.  Snapshot
isolation's first-committer-wins rule admits no output containing both commit
facts~\cite{berenson1995critique,bailis2013hat}.  Eventual storage convergence
may choose a final state; it cannot retract a commit already exposed to a
client.

These examples answer the question posed in the introduction.  Convergent
storage does not automatically provide a highly available transactional
interface.  Storage convergence constrains states after update delivery;
isolation constrains reads and commits exposed before then.

\subsection{Invariant confluence}
\label{sec:iconfluence-instance}

Let the input be a set of applied transactions, ordered by inclusion.  We use
the standard invariant-confluence assumptions: each transaction remains
applicable where the specification admits it and maps an invariant-valid state
to another invariant-valid state; independently reached states share the
required ancestor; the prescribed merge is associative and preserves all
included effects; and merged states remain reachable.  Outputs are reachable,
invariant-valid states.  We order outputs by merge refinement: $s\OutputOrd s'$
when $s'$ is an admitted merge that preserves every effect in $s$ (quotienting
states with identical effects if necessary to obtain a partial order).

Invariant confluence asks whether independently reachable valid states merge to
a valid state~\cite{bailis2014coordination}.  Under the assumptions above,
individual transaction preservation supplies Lifted Monotonicity, while
invariant confluence supplies common refinements for independently reached
states at the combined transaction input.  Associativity and reachability let
the pairwise merge argument extend inductively to finite families.  Thus the
prescribed merge witnesses the Complete CALM criterion for the deployed transaction
families.

A uniqueness constraint supplies the standard counterexample.  Two replicas
each observe that username \texttt{sam} is free and allocate it to different
accounts.  Each local state satisfies uniqueness, but no invariant-valid common
refinement preserves both allocations.  The system must reserve names, reject
one allocation, or weaken the invariant.

State convergence, transaction availability, and invariant preservation test
three different specifications.  A convergent merge may produce an invalid
state; an available transaction may expose facts that later violate an
invariant; an invariant-confluent application need not provide a particular
isolation level.  The shared model makes these distinctions explicit rather
than collapsing the properties.  We close by drawing out the resulting design
lessons and scope limits.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Input arrival versus output choice}

Functional CALM and Complete CALM separate two reasons a commitment may be necessary.
A failure of growth means that more input can invalidate an exposed output.
The system must exclude the incompatible future inputs through input-sealing
commitments from its basis before that output is exposure-ready.  A failure of
coherence means that outputs admitted at one input cannot all be preserved;
proceeding requires a choice commitment.  Whether either commitment
requires agreement among participants depends on the deployment.

The distinction concerns the semantic interface, not whether executions are
deterministic.  CALM and CRDTs already account for operational nondeterminism in message delivery, batching, retries, and
merge order.  Their usual declared outputs are fixed for a given input.  Complete CALM
adds specifications whose semantic interface itself offers several outputs and
models the commitments that resolve them.

\subsection{A semantic criterion, not an implementation discipline}

The input/output relation and its two refinement orders omit internal
computation.  This is useful for stating a common boundary and insufficient for
building a system.  Relational transducers provide a language and operational
network semantics; semilattices provide a local algebra and deterministic
merge; transaction protocols provide scheduling, validation, and recovery.
Neither Functional CALM nor Complete CALM says how to represent $S$, choose the
refinement orders, compute a common refinement, or do so efficiently.  Those
questions belong to the programming language, protocol, and verification
method used to realize the specification.

\subsection{Specification design determines the boundary}

The Complete CALM criterion is a property of the declared specification, not a
permanent label on ``register,'' ``transaction,'' or ``shopping cart.''
The deployment determines whether a commitment requires agreement between
participants.
A linearizable register and a causal register admit different explanations.  A
CRDT state and an exact read expose different promises.  A database permitted
to abort one transaction implements a different relation from one required to
commit both.

Narrowing $S$ may eliminate an incompatibility.  Broadening $O$ with a new
output that refines two alternatives may also eliminate it.  Changing the
deployment may keep an incompatibility under one participant's authority.  Each option
changes the interface or system design; none is a loophole in the criterion.

\subsection{Complete CALM is not convergence}

Complete CALM requires admitted common refinements, not unique or least ones.  The
sharded log has several minimal interleavings.  Strong eventual consistency adds
a stronger promise: replicas that receive the same input reach equivalent
state.  A semilattice join provides that promise.  Conversely,
last-writer-wins may converge by discarding one concurrent value without
preserving an interface that promised both values.  Convergence and semantic
preservation are distinct requirements.

\subsection{Safety is not liveness}

The theorems identify when exposed outputs remain refinement-consistent; they
do not guarantee message delivery, termination, or computability of a common
refinement.  The availability premise of the Complete CAP Theorem excludes an
implementation that never responds, but a full protocol result must still
state appropriate fairness, failure, and cost assumptions.

\section{Coordination-Free Frontiers}
\label{sec:frontiers}

So far we have taken each interface as given and tested the CALM criteria.  A
designer often faces the inverse question: how much can the interface promise
before coordination becomes unavoidable?  The answer depends on what must stay
fixed.  Here we fix the inputs, visible claims, refinement orders, and---when
partitions matter---the deployment.

We compare interface strength by the behaviors a specification admits.  An
interface $T$ is \emph{stronger} than $S$ when every behavior admitted by $T$ is
also admitted by $S$, but not conversely.  The stronger interface rules out more
implementations and gives clients a tighter guarantee.  For example,
linearizability is stronger than causal consistency because it admits fewer
histories.  Thus ``stronger'' corresponds to a smaller set of allowed behaviors.
This is a separate order from output refinement: $o\OutputOrd u$ says that one
particular output $u$ preserves the information already exposed by $o$.

We obtain two frontier results.  The first concerns information arriving over
time: how precisely can an interface answer while preserving every answer it
has already exposed?  The second concerns information arriving independently:
how much cross-node agreement can an interface require without withdrawing any
choice that it still offers locally?

\subsection{Remembering exactly what may remain true}

A small lookup table illustrates both the construction and its possible
compaction.
Suppose a data item initially resides at node $a$ and later moves to $b$.  Let
$o_a$ claim that a lookup starting at $a$ succeeds, and let $o_b$ make the same
claim for a lookup starting at $b$.  The richer output $o_{a\leadsto b}$ records
both that $b$ is the current location and that a lookup from $a$ succeeds by
following its forwarder to $b$.  It therefore preserves both simpler lookup
claims:
        \begin{equation}
  o_a\OutputOrd o_{a\leadsto b},
  \qquad o_b\OutputOrd o_{a\leadsto b}.
  \label{eq:lookup-output-dominance}
        \end{equation}
For $i_0\InputOrd i_1$, the transformation is explicit in
Table~\ref{tab:lookup-lift}.

\begin{table}[t]
  \centering
  \small
  \caption{Remembering every output a lookup relation has admitted so far.
  Dominated outputs can then be dropped without loss of content.}
  \label{tab:lookup-lift}
  \begin{tabular}{@{}c l l l@{}}
    \toprule
    Input & Original fiber $X(i)$ & Remembered $X^\uparrow(i)$
          & Pruned $M(i)$ \\
    \midrule
    $i_0$ & $\{o_a\}$
          & $\{o_a\}$
          & $\{o_a\}$ \\
    $i_1$ & $\{o_b,o_{a\leadsto b}\}$
          & $\{o_a,o_b,o_{a\leadsto b}\}$
          & $\{o_{a\leadsto b}\}$ \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
\end{table}

The middle column is the original specification table, grouped into one fiber
per input.  The next column remembers: at each input it collects every
output admitted by the original table at that input or any preceding input.
The last column removes $o_a$ and $o_b$ because the retained
$o_{a\leadsto b}$ refines both.  Nothing they said has been forgotten.  If two
remembered outputs were incomparable, both would have to remain unless another
retained output refined them.

We now state this construction for an arbitrary ordered relational specification
$S\subseteq I\times O$.  Write its original fiber as
        \begin{equation}
  X(i)=S(i)=\{o\in O:(i,o)\in S\}.
  \label{eq:hull-original-fiber}
        \end{equation}
The transformation replaces the output carrier $O$ by $\mathcal P(O)$,
compared under the lifted preorder of Equation~\ref{eq:lifted-output-order}.
Its unpruned output is
        \begin{equation}
  X^\uparrow(i)=\bigcup_{j\InputOrd i}X(j).
  \label{eq:monotone-hull}
        \end{equation}
Equivalently, each original row $(j,o)$ contributes $o$ to the transformed row
at every $i\succeq_I j$.  Literal union remembers all rows; the lifted preorder
captures their semantic coverage.  In particular, one retained output may cover
many remembered rows by refining them.

Call $A:I\to\mathcal P(O)$ a \emph{powerset relaxation} of $X$ when
$X(i)\LiftedOutputOrd A(i)$ at every input: every currently admitted output has
a retained refinement.  Such a relaxation is monotone when
$i\InputOrd j$ implies $A(i)\LiftedOutputOrd A(j)$.

\begin{theorem}[Remembered-output frontier]
\label{thm:monotone-hull}
The map $X^\uparrow$ is a monotone powerset relaxation.  Moreover, every monotone
powerset relaxation $A$ semantically covers every output admitted so far:
        \begin{equation}
  X^\uparrow(i)\LiftedOutputOrd A(i)
  \qquad\text{for every }i.
  \label{eq:hull-coverage}
        \end{equation}
If $X^\uparrow(i)$ is finite, let $M(i)$ contain its undominated elements.  Then
$M(i)$ and $X^\uparrow(i)$ are equivalent under $\LiftedOutputOrd$, the map $M$
is monotone, and $M(i)$ is the unique smallest subset of $X^\uparrow(i)$ that
covers all of $X^\uparrow(i)$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
The union includes $X(i)$, so $X^\uparrow$ is a relaxation.  If
$i\InputOrd k$, every predecessor of $i$ is also a predecessor of $k$; hence
$X^\uparrow(i)\subseteq X^\uparrow(k)$, which implies monotonicity under
$\LiftedOutputOrd$.

Now let $A$ be any monotone powerset relaxation and take
$o\in X^\uparrow(i)$.  Some $j\InputOrd i$ has $o\in X(j)$.  Relaxation gives
an $a\in A(j)$ with $o\OutputOrd a$, and monotonicity gives a
$b\in A(i)$ with $a\OutputOrd b$.  Thus $b$ refines $o$, proving
Equation~\ref{eq:hull-coverage}.

For a finite set, every element is below an undominated element.  Therefore
$X^\uparrow(i)\LiftedOutputOrd M(i)$; the reverse direction follows from
$M(i)\subseteq X^\uparrow(i)$.  The same observation proves monotonicity of $M$.
Finally, any subset of $X^\uparrow(i)$ that covers an undominated element must
contain that element itself.  Every covering subset therefore contains $M(i)$,
which proves minimality and uniqueness.
\end{proof}

The theorem's perhaps surprising content is minimality.  Remembering all prior
outputs is an obvious safe construction, but every monotone relaxation must
preserve their semantic content.  Dominance is the only free pruning available
when representatives must come from the remembered outputs; further pruning
requires introducing some other admitted output that refines what was removed.

\subsection{Persistent forwarding in search structures}

B-link trees provide a concrete systems instance.  During a node split, a parent
may still direct a search to the old node.  The tree remains searchable because
the old node carries a high key and a right link to the new range; the parent
can be repaired later~\cite{lehman1981efficient}.  In other words, the
implementation preserves an old search starting point even though it is no
longer exact.

We can state the required invariant without committing to one tree algorithm.
Inputs are finite prefixes $h$ of legal restructuring histories.  For a key $k$
and stable search position $p$, put $(k,p)$ in $R(h)$ when the key-directed
search procedure can start at $p$ and follow a finite path to a current
occurrence of $k$.  A restructuring step must satisfy three obligations:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item exposed search positions retain stable identities;
  \item every old key-directed edge is replaced by a key-directed path; and
  \item every old occurrence of $k$ can reach a current occurrence of $k$.
\end{enumerate}
The last two obligations allow paths to change while preserving their meaning.
Deletion or reclamation needs a separate seal or lifetime mechanism because it
can violate them.

\begin{proposition}[Persistent-forwarding monotonicity]
\label{prop:persistent-forwarding}
If the three obligations hold, then $h\InputOrd h'$ implies
$R(h)\subseteq R(h')$.  Positive forward-reachability claims are therefore
monotone, and Functional CALM permits their incremental exposure without
sealing.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Take a search path valid at $h$.  At $h'$, replace each old edge by the path that
simulates it.  From the old terminal occurrence, follow the preserved path to a
current occurrence.  The concatenated path validates the original search
claim.
\end{proof}

This positive result meets the remembered-output frontier.  For a fixed key in
a split-only migration chain, let $X(g)$ be the exact search certificates at
prefix $g$.  Every historical certificate in $X^\uparrow(h)$ has a refinement
in $R(h)$, namely its current forwarding path:
        \begin{equation}
  X^\uparrow(h)\LiftedOutputOrd R(h).
  \label{eq:forwarding-hull}
        \end{equation}
Theorem~\ref{thm:monotone-hull} says that any monotone relaxation must provide
this semantic coverage.  Current forwarding paths may compact many obsolete
certificates by dominating them, just as in Table~\ref{tab:lookup-lift}.  This is deliberately a
split-only statement.  Full implementations with deletion, merging, or memory
reclamation must prove stronger lifetime invariants or coordinate before old
search positions are reclaimed.

\subsection{Preserving independent local choices}

A second small example captures the cross-node question.  Suppose replicas $p$
and $q$ may each expose configuration 0 or 1.  With no cross-replica constraint,
the four complete outputs are
        \begin{equation}
  (0,0),\quad(0,1),\quad(1,0),\quad(1,1).
  \label{eq:product-config-example}
        \end{equation}
Now strengthen the interface by requiring both replicas to expose the same
configuration.  Only $(0,0)$ and $(1,1)$ remain.  Each replica still appears to
have both local choices, so the strengthening has changed no one-replica
interface.  During a partition, however, $p$ may choose 0 while $q$ chooses 1.
The mixed pair $(0,1)$ is locally legal but globally forbidden, and no output
can preserve both choices.  The new global constraint therefore requires
agreement or sacrifices one of the advertised local choices.

The theorem generalizes this example.  Fix a finite participant set $P$.  At
input $i$, let $V_p(i)$ be the complete views locally available to participant
$p$.  The baseline \emph{product interface} admits every combination of local
views.  A stronger interface may remove combinations.  We call it locally
conservative when it leaves each one-participant interface unchanged and adds
only cross-participant constraints.

For the formal result, assume three local properties:
views can continue when input grows; complete views are maximal under local
refinement; and locally co-occurring claims of one owner have a common local
refinement.  The product interface consists of every tuple in
$\prod_{p\in P}V_p(i)$ and every partial tuple obtained by omitting coordinates.
A \emph{normalized strengthening} $T$ selects some complete product tuples and
admits exactly their partial restrictions.  Local conservativity means that, at
every input, the projection of $T$ onto coordinate $p$ remains all of $V_p(i)$.

\begin{theorem}[Independent-product frontier]
\label{thm:product-frontier}
Suppose each coordinate has one owner, every relevant group of owners can be
separated by a legal partition, and availability permits every locally admitted
choice.  The product interface is monotone across partitions, so by
Theorem~\ref{thm:complete-cap-deployment} it remains available and consistent
under every partition.  Every proper normalized, locally conservative
strengthening is not monotone across partitions.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
For the product interface, take any family of choices that a partition can
separate.  Claims made by one owner have a common local refinement by assumption;
claims of different owners occupy different coordinates and can simply be put
in the same tuple.  Continue each coordinate at the common refined input.  The
resulting tuple preserves the whole family, as monotonicity across partitions
requires.

Now let $T$ be a proper strengthening.  Some complete product tuple $x$ is
missing from $T$.  Local conservativity says that every coordinate $x_p$ is
still locally available to its owner, perhaps as part of a different admitted
tuple.  A partition can separate those owners, and availability lets each choose
its coordinate.  If their selections had a common $T$-refinement, normalize and
complete it.  Maximality of the coordinates forces the result to be exactly
$x$, the tuple that $T$ forbids.  Hence the separated family has no admitted
common refinement.
\end{proof}

\paragraph{A register frontier.}
The abstract product has a direct consistency interpretation.  For each process
$p$, let $V_p(i)$ contain the register views that are individually explainable
by some legal real-time-respecting execution.  A view records the source write
for $p$'s reads, but it need not order concurrent writes in the same way as
another process.  The product interface allows all combinations of these
individually legal views.  Downward-closed history extension preserves old
views, so this causal-view interface is monotone across partitions when each
read has a unique owner.

Linearizability keeps every one-process behavior but adds one cross-process
requirement: all views must be projections of a single global total order.  In
the execution from \S\ref{sec:cap-corollary}, one read forces
$\dog<\catv$ and the other forces $\catv<\dog$.  Each local view is legal alone,
but their pair has no global explanation.  Linearizability therefore removes a
product tuple and crosses the frontier.

The qualification is important.  A system can avoid this obstruction by
removing a local choice, assigning the choice to one authority, restricting
the deployment, or forbidding only combinations that a partition cannot
separate.
The theorem does not rank every consistency model.  It identifies the exact
cost of adding cross-participant constraints while promising to preserve all
local behavior.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

The CALM conjecture connected logical monotonicity with coordination freedom
~\cite{hellerstein2010declarative,hellerstein2020keeping}.  Ameloot et al.
established the theorem using relational transducers and studied how data
placement and system knowledge change coordination requirements
~\cite{ameloot2013relational,ameloot2015weaker}.  Functional CALM generalizes the
semantic order-preservation result; Complete CALM adds semantic output choice and the
effect of coordination on that choice.

Brewer's conjecture and the Gilbert--Lynch theorem focus attention on
availability during partitions~\cite{brewer2000towards,gilbert2002cap}.
The Complete CAP Theorem applies Complete CALM to choices that deployments
and partitions can separate.  It extends the classical impossibility to both
sides of the specification-level dichotomy: the total-order register recovers
the negative result, while the common-refinement condition supplies the
positive result.  Local choices controlled by one participant do not require
cross-partition agreement.

CRDTs derive strong eventual consistency from carefully designed state or
operation algebras~\cite{shapiro2011crdt}.  Keep CALM and CRDT On argued that
these algebraic guarantees stop at the query boundary and proposed monotonicity
as the criterion for safe coordination-free observations~\cite{laddad2022keep}.
Functional CALM formalizes that semantic criterion for arbitrary ordered result
domains, while Complete CALM additionally handles nondeterministic observations.
The distinction from CRDT merge is between a uniform least merge and the weaker
existence of some admitted common refinement.  Work on consistency semantics likewise distinguishes visibility,
ordering, availability, and convergence
~\cite{burckhardt2014principles,mahajan2011consistency}.

HATs classify transactional guarantees realizable without cross-partition
coordination under stated client and failure assumptions~\cite{bailis2013hat}.
Invariant confluence characterizes when independently valid transactional
states preserve an application invariant on merge
~\cite{bailis2014coordination}.  Complete CALM exposes their common-refinement structure
while preserving their different output domains.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

A semantic specification abstracts distribution away.  Functional CALM asks when
a functional specification can be implemented over distributed input arrival
without coordination: monotonicity means no seal is needed.  This generalizes the
classic result from logical fact sets to arbitrary input and output refinement
orders.

Complete CALM extends the model to specifications that offer several outputs for one
input.  Modeling exposure and commitment reveals Directed Outputs as the
additional requirement for a coordination-free specification.
When directedness fails, some admissible alternative must be removed by a
commitment.  If different participants can make incompatible commitments,
coordination determines which alternative survives.

The Complete CAP Theorem is the partition form of the same equivalence.  It recovers the
classical impossibility when a cut separates incompatible choices and supplies
the positive side when all separated choices have common refinements.  A cut
may prevent agreement on input sealing or separate participants empowered to
make incompatible output choices.  Query monotonicity, CRDT merge,
transactional availability, and invariant confluence then become directly comparable without being identified
with one another.

\appendix
\section{Additional Applications}
\label{app:additional-applications}

The examples below show how the same two questions recur in classical
distributed tasks: can each choice survive more input, and can independent
choices survive together?  We isolate those semantic obstructions here.  The
results do not replace model-specific theorems about failures, computability, or
termination.

\subsection{One commitment, then monotone authority}

Agreement protocols illustrate what coordination can accomplish.  Before an
authority is established, several incompatible authority lineages may remain
possible.  Publishing one lineage rules the others out: that is a commitment.
Suppose the commitment fixes both the initial authority and a rule that uniquely
determines every certified successor.  The system can then expose three kinds
of growing evidence: a certified epoch chain $E$, votes or certificates $V$,
and decisions $D$ indexed by epoch and slot.  Define refinement by
        \begin{equation}
  (E,V,D)\OutputOrd(E',V',D')
  \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
  E\preceq_{\rm pref}E',\quad V\subseteq V',\quad D\subseteq D'.
  \label{eq:authority-refinement}
        \end{equation}
Protocol safety ensures that a later certificate extends the unique lineage and
preserves prior decisions.  Public authority changes are therefore refinements,
not revisions: votes, certificates, epochs, and decisions all accumulate after
the initial semantic commitment.

This statement does not claim that later certificates are free.  Producing one
may require communication and agreement.  It says something more specific:
later protocol work does not choose among remaining semantic branches or
retract a public result.  If the first commitment did not fix a unique
succession rule, competing next epochs would present another semantic choice
and require another commitment.

\subsection{Why $k$-set agreement needs finite families}

Consensus forbids two different decision values.  The more general $k$-set
agreement task allows at most $k$ values, which makes pairwise examples
misleading when $k>1$.  Fix $k\geq1$, at least $k+1$ participants, and $k+1$
distinct valid proposals $v_1,\ldots,v_{k+1}$.  Outputs are irrevocable partial
decision maps $m:P\rightharpoonup V$, ordered by extension, with at most $k$
values in their range.

At participant $p_r$'s local proposal input, the decision
$\{p_r\mapsto v_r\}$ is admissible.  The union of all proposal inputs is a common
input refinement.  Each local decision can continue there by itself, and any
family of at most $k$ distinct decisions can coexist.  The family of all $k+1$
cannot: every common refinement would preserve $k+1$ values and violate the
specification.  This is exactly the obstruction detected by Complete CALM's
finite-family form.  If a partition lets the owners decide independently, the
same family witnesses the negative side of Complete CAP.  Consensus is the
special case $k=1$.

This argument identifies the semantic incompatibility.  Classical wait-free and
topological impossibility theorems additionally depend on a computation model,
failure assumptions, and progress requirements~\cite{saks2000set}.

\subsection{Membership-sensitive naming}

Tight strong renaming exposes a different source of incompatibility.  The input
is the finite participant set $P$, ordered by inclusion.  The common output
universe contains finite partial naming maps; at input $P$, legal outputs are
injective maps
        \begin{equation}
  m:P\longrightarrow\{1,\ldots,|P|\},
  \label{eq:tight-renaming}
        \end{equation}
ordered by map extension.

Now start two processes independently.  At input $\{p\}$, tightness forces the
only name $p\mapsto1$; at input $\{q\}$ it likewise forces $q\mapsto1$.  The
combined input $\{p,q\}$ refines both singleton inputs.  Either assignment can
continue alone by giving the other process name 2, so Lifted Monotonicity is
not the problem.  They cannot continue together: an injective map cannot
preserve both assignments to name 1.  Complete CALM fails on this two-row family,
and a deployment that places $p$ and $q$ in different components gives the
corresponding Complete CAP obstruction.

Notice why simply extending one completed naming is not a witness.  If $n$
processes already use names $1$ through $n$, a new process can receive $n+1$
without changing an old assignment.  The obstruction comes from independent
partial memberships making incompatible early choices.  The classical renaming
hierarchy adds model-specific topological and wait-free conclusions
~\cite{herlihy1991waitfree,herlihy1999topological}.

\subsection{What does a snapshot promise?}

The word ``snapshot'' covers interfaces with very different semantic strength.
Consider inputs that are finite downward-closed causal histories, ordered by
induced extension.  A modest \emph{cut} interface exposes a finite
downward-closed subset of the available history as a lower bound on what has
happened.  Old cuts remain legal as history grows, and the union of finitely
many cuts remains downward closed.  This interface satisfies Lifted
Monotonicity and Directed Outputs.

That easy positive result should not be mistaken for a conventional snapshot
implementation.  The cut interface promises neither freshness nor completeness;
it could legally return an old cut.  A stronger interface may promise that a
vector or cut is the complete state at a globally finished boundary.  Under
equality of final answers, another relevant event changes the required result.
Functional CALM then says that the boundary must be sealed before the
completeness claim is exposed.

These are safety statements about two declared interfaces.  A distributed
snapshot protocol must also collect a consistent cut and terminate under its
channel and failure assumptions~\cite{chandy1985distributed}.  An atomic
snapshot records values at a chosen linearization point instead.  Classic
wait-free shared-memory implementations demonstrate that total ordering alone
does not prove a need for coordination; the process, memory, placement, and
failure model all matter~\cite{afek1993atomic}.
