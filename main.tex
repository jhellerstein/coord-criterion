\begin{abstract}
When does a distributed specification intrinsically require
coordination?  Existing answers---CALM, CAP, CRDTs, Highly Available
Transactions, and invariant confluence---use incompatible formalisms.
We model a specification as an input--outcome relation, a refinement
order, and semantic states recording active inputs and residual legal
choices.  \emph{Complete CALM} says that local exposure without prior
agreement is possible exactly when every finite set of independently
legal outcomes has an admissible common refinement in every compatible
continuation.  Restricting the test to partition-separated states yields
\emph{Complete CAP}: for a full interface, consistency, availability,
and partition tolerance coexist exactly for distributed-monotone
specifications.  The criterion also answers questions that fragmentation
made hard to pose: the CRDT merge algebra (``ACID~2.0'') is sufficient
but not necessary for coordination-freeness; convergent storage alone
does not provide highly available transactions; and invariant
confluence is monotonicity of invariant-valid states under merge.  Finally,
\emph{proper coordination} characterizes a commitment that turns a
non-monotone specification into a monotone residual specification.
\end{abstract}

\section{The Missing Generality}
\label{sec:intro}

Coordination requires a participant to communicate or share
serialization before committing to an answer.  That prerequisite is
expensive in the common case and fatal to availability when it crosses
a partition.  Avoiding unnecessary coordination is therefore a central
design goal for distributed systems, parallel computing and databases.
The hard question is not how to make a particular protocol faster.  It
is a more intrinsic question: which system specifications can avoid coordination
altogether, and which require it?

The CALM Conjecture offered a crisp answer: consistency can
be achieved without coordination if and only if the computation is
monotone~\cite{hellerstein2010declarative}.  The claim was intentionally
bidirectional.  Monotonicity should constructively identify safe
coordination-free designs, and non-monotonicity should prove that every
correct design must coordinate.  The subsequent CALM theorem~\cite{ameloot2013relational} was both
a blessing and a curse.  It proved a precise version of the conjecture,
but in the specialized language of Datalog queries running in networks
of relational transducers.  The proof is
subtle, and the formal result is narrower than the systems principle it
came to represent~\cite{hellerstein2020keeping}.

Meanwhile, closely related results grew up in separate mathematical
languages.  Brewer's CAP conjecture was formalized as an impossibility
theorem for linearizable registers~\cite{brewer2000towards,gilbert2002cap}.
State-based CRDTs made replicated state converge by arranging it as a
join-semilattice and making updates inflationary~\cite{shapiro2011crdt}.
Highly Available Transactions (HATs) classified isolation guarantees
that survive partitions~\cite{bailis2013hat}.  Invariant confluence
characterized when independently executed transactions can merge
without violating an invariant~\cite{bailis2014coordination}.  These
results plainly rhyme, but their separate objects and formalisms left
cross-cutting questions difficult even to state.

Is CAP exactly a monotonicity theorem under partitions, rather than
merely an impossibility for one register?  Are the CRDT merge
laws---associativity, commutativity, and idempotence, celebrated as
``ACID~2.0''~\cite{helland2009building}---necessary for
coordination-free consistency, or merely sufficient?  Does convergent
CRDT storage by itself provide a HAT interface?  Do strong eventual
consistency and HAT availability together preserve application
invariants, or is invariant confluence an additional monotonicity
condition?  Each is the same question at a different layer:
\emph{is the exposed specification monotone?}
Section~\ref{sec:cap} answers the CAP question.  Section~\ref{sec:instances}
uses the same test to answer the CRDT, HAT, and invariant-confluence
questions.

This paper returns to CALM's original aspiration.  The missing step is
not a richer machine model.  It is a minimalist semantic model.  We need
only two ingredients:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item a relation from system inputs to allowed outcomes;
        and
  \item a partial order saying when one outcome extends another without
        contradicting it.
\end{enumerate}
The relation makes ambiguity explicit.  System specifications often
allow a set of answers for a given scenario: leader election permits
any agreed-upon leader, transaction systems permit any serializable
schedule, and deadlock resolution may choose any transaction on a
cycle to abort.  A running implementation progressively commits within
this set.  A semantic state records that progression: input events
activate inputs, while commitment events shrink the residual relation.
The partial order distinguishes a commitment that can be extended from
one that must rule out alternatives.

This simple declarative definition yields three contributions.  First, \emph{Complete
CALM} gives one semantic test for coordination across arbitrary
interface specifications: monotonicity w.r.t.\ the partial order.
Its partition-aware companion, \emph{Complete
CAP}, says that, for a full interface, consistency, availability, and
partition tolerance can coexist exactly when the interface is
distributed-monotone.  Unlike the
Gilbert--Lynch theorem for one register specification, this result is
bidirectional and applies to any ordered relational specification, not just
linearizable registers.
Second, translating CRDTs, HATs, and invariant confluence into this
vocabulary answers the remaining questions.  ACI join is a sufficient
discipline for monotonicity, not a necessary condition; convergent
storage does not by itself supply a HAT interface; and availability
plus convergence does not by itself guarantee invariant safety---under
the standard transaction-and-merge model, invariant confluence is
precisely the missing monotonicity condition.  Third, the
criterion can be re-applied after a system coordinates.  This resolves a useful puzzle: non-monotone tasks need
coordination, but coordination itself makes a non-monotone choice.  How
do two wrongs make a right?  \emph{Proper coordination} makes exactly
the choice needed to leave a monotone residual interface.

Our claim is deliberately semantic.  It classifies the full interface a
specification declares, not the syntax or existence of one
implementation.  An implementation may expose a strict subrelation of
a permissive specification; its choices are commitment events that
produce a residual specification, to which the test applies again.  The benefit
of this level is that the same question can be asked before choosing
Datalog, transactions, a CRDT library, or a consensus protocol.

\section{Two Registers, One Lesson}
\label{sec:registers}

Consider a register replicated at processes $p$ and $q$.  Initially it
contains $\bot$.  Process $p$ writes \dog, process $q$ concurrently
writes \catv, and the replicas exchange their writes later.  The events
carry the familiar Lamport partial order: local program order plus
send-to-receive order and its transitive closure~\cite{lamport1978time}.
The two writes are concurrent because neither happens before the other.

Under linearizability, the system must behave as if all operations
occurred in one total order~\cite{herlihy1990linearizability}.  Suppose
$p$ has received $q$'s write and returns \catv\ from a read.  This
commits to an order in which the \dog\ write precedes the \catv\ write.
At the same time, $q$ can receive $p$'s write and, from its local
semantic state, return \dog.  That answer commits to the opposite write
order.  Either local behavior has a legal linearization by itself.
There is no total order that preserves both commitments.  Once the
partition heals, the replicas cannot merge their answers or take either
one back.  They must therefore agree on the write order before exposing
the reads.

Now change the register specification from linearizability to causal
consistency~\cite{ahamad1995causal}.
An outcome is a family of per-process causal views rather than one
global total order.  Each view grows by adding operations and causal
edges, while concurrent writes may remain unordered or appear in
different orders at different processes.  The two reads above now
coexist: $p$'s view can explain \catv, $q$'s can explain \dog, and a
larger outcome contains both views without revising either.  The same
execution that forced agreement under linearizability is harmless under
the causal-consistency specification.

The distinction is not merely ``different'' versus ``equal.''  The
sets $\{\dog\}$ and $\{\catv\}$ are incomparable under inclusion, but
they merge at $\{\dog,\catv\}$.  Incomparability is harmless when an
admissible common refinement exists.  Coordination is forced when
locally legal commitments are \emph{incompatible}: no admissible outcome
can extend them together.  The rest of the paper packages that
observation as one test.

\section{Relational Specifications and Commitments}
\label{sec:model}

Start with the simplest possible declarative specification.  Let $I$ be
the input domain and $O$ the domain of externally visible outcomes.  A
deterministic specification is a function $f:I\to O$: each input has
one correct output.  Concurrent and distributed systems commonly have
a more permissive specification: for one input, several outcomes may
all be within spec.  We therefore start from a relation rather than a
function, and build the model in small steps---specification, semantic
state, semantic events, refinement---ending in the monotonicity test.

\begin{definition}[Relational specification]
A \emph{relational specification} is a relation $S\subseteq I\times O$.
For each input $i$, we call the set $S(i)=\{o:(i,o)\in S\}$ the
\emph{fiber} over $i$: the set of outcomes the specification permits
for input $i$.  We require every fiber to be nonempty.
\end{definition}

When a fiber has more than one member, we call that multiplicity an
\emph{ambiguity}.  Ambiguity is usually intentional: a specification
may permit many serializations, causal views, transaction results, or
replicated states.  An input may include whatever application state or
context is relevant to its result.  The question that drives this
section is how the permitted outcomes change as the system receives
input and makes externally visible choices.

Answering that question requires a notion of the ``state'' a running
system occupies.  We want a \emph{semantic} state, not an
implementation state: memory contents and message queues are
irrelevant here.  Only two bookkeeping facts matter.  First, which
inputs have arrived?  The specification is a complete, typically
infinite object: it describes every input the system could ever
receive, carrying potentially unbounded ambiguity.  At any moment a
running system has received only a finite set of \emph{active} inputs,
and only their ambiguity is at stake so far.  Second, which of the
permitted input--output pairs are still on the table?  As the system
makes visible choices, some pairs are ruled out; those that remain
form a \emph{residual specification}, an object of the same shape as
$S$.  A semantic state packages these two facts.

\begin{definition}[Semantic state]
A semantic state $\sigma=(I_\sigma,S_\sigma)$ consists of a finite set
$I_\sigma\subseteq I$ of active inputs and a relation
$S_\sigma\subseteq S$ with nonempty fibers.  We call $S_\sigma$ the
\emph{residual specification}: it contains exactly the input--output
pairs that remain admissible at $\sigma$.
\end{definition}

The initial state is $\sigma_0=(\varnothing,S)$: nothing has arrived
and nothing has been ruled out.  An active input $i$ remains ambiguous
while $|S_\sigma(i)|>1$.  The semantic state is self-contained: it
carries every semantic consequence relevant to what may happen next,
so no separate account of how it was reached enters the model.

How does a semantic state change?  An implementation takes many kinds
of steps---messages, timeouts, retries---but only two kinds matter
semantically: new input can arrive, growing the active set; or the
system can rule out previously admissible outcomes, shrinking the
residual specification.  Fibers may lose members, but no fiber may
empty out, and nothing excluded ever becomes legal again.  We model
each such step as a \emph{semantic event}, a function from the current
semantic state to the next.  An event need not apply in every
state---closing an auction makes sense only while the auction is
open---so formally an event is a \emph{partial} function.

\begin{definition}[Semantic events]
A \emph{semantic event} is a partial function on semantic states.  An
\emph{input event} presents a finite batch $B\subseteq I$, changing
$I_\sigma$ to $I_\sigma\cup B$ while preserving $S_\sigma$.  A
\emph{restriction event} preserves $I_\sigma$ and replaces
$S_\sigma$ by a subrelation with nonempty fibers.  It is a
\emph{commitment event} when the inclusion is strict.
\end{definition}

The register of Section~\ref{sec:registers} makes this concrete.
Delivering a write is an input event.  When replica $p$ returns \catv\
from a read under linearizability, that is a commitment event: it
permanently strikes every ordering in which the \catv\ write precedes
the \dog\ write.  A strict commitment is the semantically irreversible
act in this model, precisely because an excluded pair can never become
legal again.  The application declares which events are legal, and
whether an event applies depends only on the current semantic state.
Participants in a distributed system hold local semantic states, and
communication affects which events they can apply.

This dynamics exposes the split-brain problem.  Suppose partitioned
replicas $p$ and $q$ are in compatible local states.  Replica $p$
exposes a locally legal $o_1$ while $q$ exposes a locally legal $o_2$.
Each exposure is a sound commitment in isolation.  But clients may act
on both, so the system must eventually reach a semantic state that
preserves both commitments.  If no such state exists, the replicas have
split brain: neither answer can be retracted, yet they cannot coexist.

Refinement identifies exactly when different commitments can coexist.

\begin{definition}[Ordered relational specification]
An \emph{ordered relational specification} is a pair $(S,\Ord)$, where
$S\subseteq I\times O$ is a relational specification and $\Ord$ is a
partial order on outcomes.  We read $o\Ord o'$ as ``$o'$ refines $o$
without contradicting it.''
\end{definition}

The refinement order is part of the specification: declaring which
outcomes count as compatible extensions of which is a design decision,
just as the relation $S$ is.  Section~\ref{sec:registers} already used
one: register read-sets under inclusion, where
$\{\dog\}\Ord\{\dog,\catv\}$.

The final ingredient names the scenario in which coordination
questions arise: several participants, each in its own local semantic
state, each poised to expose an answer for one of its active inputs;
and, later, a joint semantic state in which their information has been
pooled.  We package the scenario in one \emph{continuation} relation,
written
\[
  \mathsf C((\sigma_1,i_1),\ldots,(\sigma_k,i_k);(\tau,i))
\]
and read ``the local states $\sigma_j$, answering inputs $i_j$, can
continue jointly to state $\tau$ with input $i$.''  The relation holds
when:
\begin{itemize}
  \item each $i_j$ is active at $\sigma_j$, and the $k$ local states
        can occur together in one run;
  \item $\tau$ is a joint state reached by exchanging the sources'
        existing inputs and commitments and applying any further input
        events, and $i$ is active at $\tau$.  For a query, $i$ may be
        the union of the facts in the $i_j$; for the register, it
        contains both writes;
  \item no \emph{new} restriction event on the way to $\tau$ chooses
        among the candidate outcomes: $\tau$ reflects pooling and new
        input, not further decisions.
\end{itemize}
A family of pairs is \emph{compatible} when it has at least one
continuation.  The relation $\mathsf C$ is supplied by the
application's semantic events, and it depends only on the displayed
states, not on how they were reached.

One more observation sets up the main definition.  Exposing an
outcome is not a passive read; it is a restriction event.  When a
participant at state $\sigma$ returns an outcome $o\in S_\sigma(i)$,
the system becomes obligated to stand by $o$: every outcome that does
not refine $o$ is struck from the residual specification.  (The
residual specification is defined on all of $I$, so the obligation is
recorded even for inputs that have not yet arrived.)  If participants
independently expose $o_1,\ldots,o_k$, then at any continuation
$(\tau,i)$ the outcomes still admissible for $i$ are those
$u\in S_\tau(i)$ that refine every exposed $o_j$.  Everything now
rests on whether such a $u$ always exists.

\begin{definition}[Monotone specification]
\label{def:monotone}
An ordered specification $(S,\Ord)$ is \emph{monotone} when, for every
finite family satisfying
$\mathsf C((\sigma_1,i_1),\ldots,(\sigma_k,i_k);(\tau,i))$ and every
choice $o_j\in S_{\sigma_j}(i_j)$, there is an outcome
$u\in S_\tau(i)$ such that
\[
  o_j\Ord u\qquad\text{for every }j.
  \tag{1}
\]
\end{definition}

Two special cases give the intuition.  With $k=1$, the definition says
an exposed answer must remain refinable as more input arrives.  With
$k>1$, it says independently exposed answers must have one admissible
common refinement.  Requiring one refinement for every finite family
is deliberately stronger than pairwise compatibility, which need not
imply a joint extension.  Correlated interface calls can be
represented as one product input and outcome, so the definition
imposes no independence assumption across calls.  Finally, we will
later re-run the test on systems that have already coordinated
(Section~\ref{sec:proper}); we write $\mathsf{Mono}(\rho)$ for the
same condition restricted to source states and continuations extending
a semantic state $\rho$.

Commitment and coordination live at different levels.  A commitment is
the semantic effect of ruling out alternatives.  \emph{Coordination} is
the distributed mechanism that makes a participant communicate or
share serialization before applying such a restriction, so that other
participants cannot make an incompatible commitment.  Gossip that
merely carries data or an already safe result is communication, but not
coordination in this sense.

A full interface permits every $o\in S_\sigma(i)$ for an active input.
It is \emph{locally exposable without coordination} when a participant
may expose any such $o$ from its local state alone and, at every
$\mathsf C$ continuation, some admissible outcome refines everything
independently exposed.

\begin{theorem}[Complete CALM]
\label{thm:calm}
A full ordered relational interface is locally exposable without
coordination if and only if it is monotone.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
If the specification is monotone, Equation~(1) supplies an admissible
common refinement for every finite collection of locally exposed
outcomes at every $\mathsf C$ continuation.  That refinement survives
all the induced restrictions, so no participant must first rule out
another participant's locally legal commitment.  They may communicate
and refine to the common outcome later.

If the specification is not monotone, its witness supplies local
semantic states, locally admissible outcomes, and a $\mathsf C$
continuation with no outcome refining all of them.  Independent
exposure commits the interface to preserve every witness outcome, and
no admissible outcome refines them all.  A correct realization
of the full interface must prevent at least one exposure until it has
learned enough to rule out a conflicting commitment or agree on a
choice.  That is coordination.
\end{proof}

The proof is short on purpose.  The original CALM work had to define a
network language, data placement, and operational coordination before
proving its query result.  Here those concerns are parameters of the
semantic events and the compatibility of participant states.  The
theorem is a semantic criterion, not an algorithm, liveness result, or
claim that communication and durability are free.  Its value is that
the same criterion can now be instantiated across formalisms.

\section{Complete CAP: CAP Is Distributed Monotonicity}
\label{sec:cap}

The first cross-formalism question is whether CAP is exactly a
monotonicity theorem.  The classical theorem fixes a linearizable
register; the original CALM theorem fixes relational queries and
transducers.  Neither could compare their boundaries.  For the full
interface defined below, the unified model answers yes, with
monotonicity restricted to participants separated by a partition.

Complete CALM forbids prior agreement among any participants that may
expose outcomes.  Availability under partition forbids only agreement
that must cross the partition: processes within one connected component
may still communicate and coordinate.  This distinction gives CAP its
own monotonicity condition.

Fix the assignment of semantic events to participants.  A partition
$\Pi$ divides them into two or more nonempty connected components and
delays every message sent between components.  Each component may
continue to receive client input, apply local commitments, and exchange
messages internally.

Let $\mathsf C_\Pi$ be the subset of $\mathsf C$ in which each source
state belongs to one connected component, the components reach those
states while cross-component messages remain undelivered, and the joint
state follows their independent progress, including possible healing.
Thus $\mathsf C_\Pi\subseteq\mathsf C$ by definition.  A source family
appearing in $\mathsf C_\Pi$ is \emph{$\Pi$-separated}.  Internal
coordination is already reflected in each $S_{\sigma_j}$; what a
component cannot know is how the others have progressed.

\begin{definition}[Distributed monotonicity]
\label{def:distributed-monotone}
An ordered specification is \emph{distributed-monotone} when, for every
partition $\Pi$, every
\[
  \mathsf C_\Pi((\sigma_1,i_1),\ldots,(\sigma_k,i_k);(\tau,i)),
\]
and every choice $o_j\in S_{\sigma_j}(i_j)$, there is an outcome
$u\in S_\tau(i)$ such that
\[
  o_j\Ord u\qquad\text{for every }j.
  \tag{2}
\]
\end{definition}

Thus distributed monotonicity asks exactly whether answers produced on
opposite sides can coexist.  Define consistency at the interface to
mean that all exposed answers have an admissible common refinement.
Define \emph{full-interface availability} to mean that every nonfailed
component may expose any outcome its residual specification permits,
without waiting for a message across the cut.  Partition tolerance
requires both properties for every $\Pi$.  An implementation that
filters these choices is implementing a residual subrelation, to which
the test can be applied again.

\begin{theorem}[Complete CAP]
\label{thm:cap}
A full ordered relational interface permits consistent exposure and
full-interface availability under every network partition if and only
if it is distributed-monotone.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
If the interface is distributed-monotone, each connected component may
expose an outcome from its current semantic state without a
cross-partition wait.  Definition~\ref{def:distributed-monotone}
guarantees a common admissible refinement after independent progress
and after healing, so consistency is preserved.

If it is not distributed-monotone, a witness supplies a partition,
locally legal component outcomes, and a $\mathsf C_\Pi$ continuation
with no joint refinement.  Full-interface availability permits the
components to expose those outcomes because none can observe the
others' progress.  Doing so leaves no sound residual specification.
Preventing at least one exposure requires either waiting across the cut
or narrowing the full interface, sacrificing availability or changing
the specification.
\end{proof}

Complete CAP answers the first question: the full-interface CAP
boundary is monotonicity over partition-separated semantic states.  To
see that it subsumes the classical theorem, encode the Gilbert--Lynch
register~\cite{gilbert2002cap}: inputs are read and write requests,
outcomes are operation orders that explain every returned read, and
refinement extends an order without reordering it.  The two opposite
orders of Section~\ref{sec:registers} are a $\mathsf C_\Pi$ witness
against monotonicity, recovering the impossibility.  Now recast the
same inputs under causal consistency: outcomes become families of
per-process causal views, refined by adding operations and edges, and
partition-separated views always combine---distributed-monotone, hence
available.  What was an impossibility proof for one data type and a
folklore contrast is a short calculation in a shared vocabulary,
applicable to any outcome domain, not only registers.

Because every $\mathsf C_\Pi$ continuation is a $\mathsf C$
continuation, Complete CALM implies Complete CAP's premise:
\[
  \text{monotone}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
  \text{distributed-monotone}.
\]
The converse can fail when every incompatible choice lies inside one
connected component, which can settle it without waiting across the
cut.  The two conditions coincide when every relevant $\mathsf C$
witness can also be realized as a $\mathsf C_\Pi$ witness.  The important
point is not to rename CAP's ``C.''  It is to identify its obstruction: consistency needs cross-partition
coordination exactly when partition-separated outcomes lack a common
refinement.

\section{Monotonicity Across the Literature}
\label{sec:instances}

Section~\ref{sec:cap} united CAP and CALM by restricting one
monotonicity test to partitions.  This section brings state-based
CRDTs, HATs, and invariant confluence under the same test, in two
steps per formalism.  First, \emph{encode}: choose inputs, outcomes,
and a refinement order, and show that the formalism's defining
guarantee is monotonicity of that specification.  Second,
\emph{answer}: with the formalisms in one vocabulary, each remaining
question from Section~\ref{sec:intro} becomes an instance of the same
test---is the exposed specification monotone?

Table~\ref{tab:instances} summarizes the encodings.  Each row chooses
outcomes and a refinement order.  A positive result proves monotonicity
by supplying common refinements; a negative result exhibits
independently legal commitments with no admissible refinement.

\subsection{Warm-Up: CALM Is Query Monotonicity}

The simplest encoding calibrates the method.
For a deterministic query $Q$, take an input $I$ to be a database
instance, let $S(I)=\{Q(I)\}$, and order query results by set inclusion.
Input events activate successively larger instances, with
$\mathsf C((\sigma,I);(\tau,J))$ only when $I\subseteq J$.
Definition~\ref{def:monotone} becomes
\[
  I\subseteq J\quad\Longrightarrow\quad Q(I)\subseteq Q(J),
\]
the usual definition of a monotone query.  The relational-transducer
CALM theorem then supplies the operational result for that language:
exactly these queries have coordination-free transducer programs.  Our
criterion does not replace that machine theorem.  It shows which part
of it is semantic and carries that part to other interfaces.

\subsection{CRDTs: Is the Merge Algebra Necessary?}

State-based CRDTs achieve convergence by algebra: replica states form
a join-semilattice, updates are inflationary, and replicas merge with
join~\cite{shapiro2011crdt}.

\paragraph{Encoding.}  An input is the finite set $U$ of update
operations a replica has received; an outcome is a replica state;
refinement is the lattice order.  Because join is associative,
commutative, and idempotent (ACI) and updates only inflate, every
delivery order of $U$ reaches the same state $s(U)$.  This is Strong
Eventual Consistency (SEC), and it is what makes the fiber
$S(U)=\{s(U)\}$ well defined.  Exchanging updates unions the delivered
sets, so continuations satisfy
$\mathsf C((\sigma_1,U_1),(\sigma_2,U_2);(\tau,U_1\cup U_2))$, and
exposed states have the common refinement
$s(U_1)\sqcup s(U_2)=s(U_1\cup U_2)$.  The CRDT construction is a
monotonicity proof for the state specification, with join as a uniform
witness for Definition~\ref{def:monotone}.

\paragraph{Payoff.}  Is that algebra necessary, or merely sufficient?
The ACI laws, celebrated as ``ACID~2.0''~\cite{helland2009building},
have been advocated as the design discipline for coordination-free
systems.  Complete CALM shows the semantic requirement is strictly
weaker: Definition~\ref{def:monotone} asks only that every finite
family of exposed outcomes have \emph{some} admissible common
refinement in each continuation---not a least one, not one computed by
a uniform binary operator, not one drawn from a global lattice.  A
sharded log separates the two.  Each item is delivered to exactly one
shard, each shard exposes its local sequence, and the specification
admits any total order that preserves every shard's sequence---the
guarantee familiar from partitioned log systems such as
Kafka~\cite{kreps2011kafka}, whose consumers may each observe a
different admissible interleaving.  Every family of
exposed shard sequences has admissible common refinements, namely the
interleavings, so the specification is monotone and shards expose
without coordination.  But sequences under order-preserving extension
form no semilattice: minimal interleavings are mutually incomparable,
there is no least common refinement, and a uniform merge operator would
have to commit to an order the specification never forced.  ACI join is
a powerful sufficient route to monotonicity, not a necessary condition
for it.

One scope note: SEC governs the state interface.  A system that also
exposes exact reads whose values causally influence later
updates~\cite{lamport1978time} has enlarged its outcome domain, and
those reads must pass the same test~\cite{laddad2022keep}.  The
transactional setting below makes this concrete.

\subsection{HATs: Does Convergent Storage Provide Available
Transactions?}

HATs classify transactional guarantees by whether every transaction
can complete at an available replica~\cite{bailis2013hat}.

\paragraph{Encoding.}  Inputs are transactional requests---begins,
reads, writes, commits---with each guarantee's replica-contact,
session, and failure assumptions encoded in $\mathsf C_\Pi$.  Outcomes
are growing sets of facts such as $\mathsf{commit}(T)$ and
$\mathsf{read}(T,x,v)$; refinement is set inclusion.  Relative to those
assumptions, Theorem~\ref{thm:cap} reads the classification
bidirectionally: a guarantee is a HAT exactly when its full interface
is distributed-monotone.  Read committed and the session guarantees
preserve facts exposed from local semantic states, and any family of
such fact sets remains jointly extensible.  Unavailable levels fail
with the shape of Equation~(2): partition-separated states admit
locally legal fact sets $o_1,\ldots,o_k$ and a continuation $(\tau,i)$
for which no $u\in S_\tau(i)$ contains them all---serializability's
incompatible commit orders are the classic witness---so some exposure
must wait across the cut.

\paragraph{Payoff.}  The encoding answers the next cross-formalism
question: does convergent CRDT storage by itself provide a HAT
interface?  No.  SEC constrains replica states after updates arrive; a
transactional guarantee constrains which read, commit, and session
facts may be exposed before then.  An arbitrary stale read is legal
under one guarantee and a violation under another, and when a read
causally produces a later write, subsequent convergence cannot retract
the illegal observation.  State convergence therefore selects no
isolation or session guarantee.  None of this rules out carefully
engineered HAT interfaces over CRDT storage; it says their read and
commit paths must pass the monotonicity test in the transactional
outcome domain, not the storage domain.

\subsection{Invariant Confluence: Does Availability Preserve
Invariants?}

Invariant confluence asks when replicas that transact independently
can merge without violating an application
invariant~\cite{bailis2014coordination}.

\paragraph{Encoding.}  Inputs are the transactions applied since a
common invariant-valid ancestor state; outcomes are invariant-valid
database states; refinement is the order induced by the application's
prescribed merge.  A transaction set is $I$-confluent exactly when
every pair of $I$-valid states reachable from a common valid ancestor
has an $I$-valid merge.  In the standard associative-merge model, with
merged states remaining reachable, the pairwise condition extends
inductively to finite families: under $\mathsf C_\Pi$ the replica
states supply $o_1,\ldots,o_k$, and their prescribed merge supplies the
common refinement $u$.  Thus $I$-confluence is distributed monotonicity
of the invariant-valid specification.  A uniqueness constraint gives
the standard negative witness: two separated states may each allocate
the same supposedly unique value, but their joint continuation has no
invariant-valid outcome refining both allocations.

\paragraph{Payoff.}  The encoding answers a question that spans three
literatures: do SEC and HAT availability together preserve application
invariants?  No.  SEC does not prevent replicas from converging to an
invalid merged state, and HAT availability says only that operations
complete without cross-partition coordination; neither implies that
independently valid choices remain valid together.  Invariant
confluence is the missing monotonicity condition, one layer up.  State
convergence, interface availability, and invariant safety are
monotonicity of three different exposed specifications; each must be
checked in its own outcome domain and refinement order.

\section{Proper Coordination}
\label{sec:proper}

Non-monotone programs require coordination.  Yet coordination is itself
non-monotone: it selects a cutoff, winner, or order and excludes the
alternatives.  How can composing two non-monotone pieces produce a safe
system?  Complete CALM answers by testing what the composition exposes.

A coordinating boundary is now a semantic event rather than an
external transformation of the model.  Applied at state $\sigma$, a
coordinating commitment $c$ produces
\[
  c(\sigma)=\sigma_C,
  \qquad I_{\sigma_C}=I_\sigma,
  \qquad S_{\sigma_C}\subsetneq S_\sigma.
\]
The inclusion is soundness: coordination may remove choices, but it may
not invent a forbidden answer.  We keep the input and outcome domains
fixed across the boundary; application phases, such as cart
generations, are represented explicitly in those values.

\begin{definition}[Proper coordination]
A coordinating commitment $c$ is \emph{proper} at $\sigma$ when it is
sound, $\neg\mathsf{Mono}(\sigma)$, and
$\mathsf{Mono}(\sigma_C)$.
\end{definition}

The definition gives a coordinating event a proof obligation.  If
$\neg\mathsf{Mono}(\sigma_C)$, the event did not settle enough; an
incompatible choice remains downstream.  If
$\mathsf{Mono}(\sigma)$, it settled nothing that required agreement.
Proper coordination is the middle case: one commitment removes the
necessary ambiguity and leaves no coordination debt behind.

Shopping-cart checkout, the canonical example from Dynamo and
Bloom~\cite{decandia2007dynamo,alvaro2011bloom}, makes this concrete.
While a cart is open, replicas record add and remove intent in growing
sets.  The replicated cart payload---for example, a pair $(A,R)$---
merges by componentwise union even though the displayed contents $A\setminus R$
may change.  Shopping can therefore remain available and
coordination-free.

Checkout is different.  Model every intent, rejection, and order fact
with a cart generation $g$, all within the original input and outcome
domains.  Checkout must close one generation and commit to the finite
actions included in its order.  Without a common cut, one replica
could issue a receipt while a late action at another replica changed the purchased contents.  Checkout therefore applies a
non-monotone commitment event: \emph{this generation is closed, with
these line items}.  The event removes every outcome inconsistent with
that cut from the residual specification; later actions are routed to a
new generation or rejected.  The resulting order exposes an append-only
record---order placed, payment evidence, shipment evidence, receipt---
whose facts only accumulate.  A non-monotone application boundary and
a non-monotone coordination mechanism have composed into a monotone
residual specification.

This suggests a direct architecture review.  At each lock, barrier, or
coordination service, ask two questions: which incompatible outcomes
does it rule out, and is its downstream interface monotone?  No witness
means the coordination may be wasted.  A residual witness means it is
insufficient.  Passing the test is evidence that the coordination is
proper.

\section{From Criterion to Design Tool}
\label{sec:practice}

Complete CALM suggests a four-step design loop:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Write the binary input/output relation, its refinement order,
        and the semantic events that change its residual state.
  \item Search for a small witness: compatible participant states and
        legal exposures with no common refinement.
  \item If one exists, either weaken the interface or coordinate at
        that boundary.
  \item Re-run the test on the residual specification produced by the
        coordinating commitment.
\end{enumerate}
A useful weakening may change only the refinement order: preserve a
causal partial order instead of selecting a total order, or return a
set of possibilities instead of one winner.  More generally, the
\emph{coordination-free frontier} consists of the smallest specification
weakenings that eliminate every common-refinement witness.  Finding
that frontier algorithmically is a useful direction for future work.
This view is consistent with the known role of causal consistency as a
strong available specification for replicated
storage~\cite{mahajan2011consistency}.

The general test is semantic and therefore not decidable from arbitrary
program text.  Practical tools need checkable interface languages.
Existing work already supplies pieces: Bloom checks relational
monotonicity~\cite{alvaro2011bloom}; CRDT and lattice libraries certify
merge structure; HAT and invariant-confluence analyses check restricted
transactional specifications; languages such as Flo track monotonic
stream progress in their types~\cite{laddad2025flo}.  The new opportunity is to
apply these checks at service boundaries and, especially, after a
coordinating component.  A tool need not prove all of
Definition~\ref{def:monotone}; finding one witness already pins down a
real coordination obligation.

The criterion also marks its own limits.  Monotone exposure may still
require communication to obtain data, storage to retain it, and
replication to survive faults.  It does not guarantee convergence,
termination, or durability.  Those properties require additional
structure.  Nor does the criterion decide which refinement order users
want; declaring two answers compatible is a product decision, not a
proof trick.

Complete CALM is exact relative to those modeling choices.  Once the
binary relation $S$, refinement order $\Ord$, and state-based
continuation relation $\mathsf C$ are fixed, monotonicity completely
characterizes safe local exposure of the full interface.  No finer
semantic coordination boundary remains without changing one of those
components.  Disagreements about whether a system needs coordination
therefore reduce to disagreements about its declared outcomes,
refinements, or possible semantic continuations.

Recent results fit within this modeling envelope rather than sitting
outside it.  Weaker CALM changes $\mathsf C$ by varying what nodes know
about placement and membership~\cite{ameloot2015weaker}.  Baccaert and
Ketsman enlarge the allowed asynchronous and nondeterministic
computations~\cite{baccaert2026spectrum}.  Arbitration-free consistency
supplies a related bidirectional criterion for storage
specifications~\cite{attiya2025arbitration}; we do not claim a
separation from that framework here.  Complete CALM contributes the
small vocabulary in which such choices can be stated uniformly across
queries, consistency models, transactions, invariants, and replicated
data types---and re-applied to residual specifications after
coordination.

\section{Conclusion}

CALM proposed the right bright line; it was waiting for a formalism as
simple as its claim.  Complete CALM characterizes when a full
interface is safely locally exposable without prior agreement, and
Complete CAP gives its partition-aware companion: for a full interface,
C+A+P is possible exactly when the specification is
distributed-monotone.  The common question across the literature is
monotonicity, at distinct layers.  ACI join proves CRDT state monotone
but is not necessary for monotonicity; convergent storage does not by
itself provide a HAT interface; and SEC plus HAT availability does not
by itself preserve invariants---invariant confluence supplies the
missing condition.  The practical next step is to make this test---and
the audit of proper coordination---part of how systems interfaces are
designed.
