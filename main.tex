\begin{abstract}
When does a system inherently require coordination?  CAP, CALM, CRDTs, Highly
Available Transactions, and invariant confluence draw related coordination
boundaries in different mathematical languages.  Existing accounts characterize
when coordination can be avoided, but do not generally model what coordination
accomplishes when it is needed.

We complete this semantic picture in two steps.  \emph{Complete CALM} lifts
the classical result from Datalog and fact inclusion to functions between
arbitrary partially ordered input and output domains, giving the broader
folklore interpretation a formal semantic basis.  Independent of implementation
language, monotonicity characterizes exactly when a function's output can be
exposed as distributed input arrives without first sealing that input.

\emph{CALMADO}---\emph{Consistency As Lifted Monotonicity And Directed
Outputs}---extends the analysis from functions to relational specifications
that allow a choice among outputs.  Such a specification permits \emph{safe
exposure at will}
exactly when it is monotone and the outputs allowed for each input are
\emph{finitely directed}.
A \emph{safe exposure} retains a refinement of every admissible alternative;
a \emph{commitment} rules at least one out; and \emph{coordination} is
agreement before participants make incompatible commitments.
This covers settings such as leader election and transactional commit ordering.
As a corollary, CALMADO yields a Generalized CAP Theorem that extends classical
CAP from a one-way impossibility about linearizability to a bidirectional
dichotomy parameterized by the declared consistency semantics.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Coordination occurs when one participant must wait for information or agreement
from another before acting.  The wait adds latency and can destroy availability
when the needed communication crosses a network partition.  Avoiding
unnecessary coordination is therefore a central goal in distributed systems
and databases.  This paper asks which specifications intrinsically require it.

CALM proposed a crisp, bidirectional answer: consistency can be achieved
without coordination exactly when the computation is monotone
~\cite{hellerstein2010declarative}.  Ameloot et al. proved this connection for
Datalog queries evaluated by networks of relational transducers
~\cite{ameloot2013relational}.  The theorem characterizes both the
coordination-free cases and the cases in which coordination is unavoidable, but
inside a particular language and machine model
~\cite{hellerstein2020keeping}.

Several other traditions draw closely related boundaries, each in its own
formalism.  CAP proves an impossibility for available linearizable registers
under partition~\cite{brewer2000towards,gilbert2002cap}.  State-based CRDTs give
a strong sufficient discipline for coordination-free convergence using
join-semilattices and inflationary updates~\cite{shapiro2011crdt}.  Highly
Available Transactions (HATs) classify transaction guarantees that survive
partitions~\cite{bailis2013hat}.  Invariant confluence gives a necessary and
sufficient condition for independently executed transactions to merge without
breaking an application invariant~\cite{bailis2014coordination}.

The results plainly share an idea, but their mathematical frameworks do not
compose: logical queries and transducers, register executions, semilattices,
transaction histories, and invariant-preserving database states expose
different objects and assumptions.  This makes simple cross-cutting questions
awkward.  Does CALM encompass CAP?  Are CRDT join laws necessary for
coordination freedom, or one strong sufficient discipline?  Does convergent
storage provide highly available transactions?  Do convergence and transaction
availability preserve application invariants?  Section~\ref{sec:cap-corollary}
answers the first question; the CRDT, HAT, and invariant-confluence parts of
Section~\ref{sec:instances} answer the others.

Our account begins by abstracting distribution away.  A functional semantic
specification is an arbitrary function
\[
  f:(I,\InputOrd)\longrightarrow(O,\OutputOrd)
\]
between a partially ordered input domain and a separately partially ordered
output domain.  It is monotone in the standard mathematical sense when
\[
  i\InputOrd j \quad\Longrightarrow\quad f(i)\OutputOrd f(j).
\]
Distribution makes input arrival uncertain: participants may hold different
input refinements, received in different orders and batches.  Monotonicity says
that output exposed from any one of them remains valid as more input arrives.
If the function is not monotone, exposing an output may first require agreement
that the relevant input is complete.  We call that agreement an input
\emph{seal}.

Our first step, \emph{Complete CALM}, lifts the CALM result from Datalog and fact
inclusion to these arbitrary functions and refinement partial orders.  This
suggests a second reading of CALM: historically, \emph{Consistency As Logical
Monotonicity}; here, also \emph{Consistency As Lifted Monotonicity}.  The lift
provides a common language for coordination-freedom results whose semantic
output is fixed for each input, including query evaluation and the usual CRDT
state interface.

Our second step addresses a case those interfaces do not expose: the semantic
specification itself may allow several distinguishable outputs for one input.
Leader election may permit either eligible leader; a database may permit several
serial schedules.  We call this extension \emph{CALMADO}: \emph{Consistency As
Lifted Monotonicity And Directed Outputs}.  In order theory, a nonempty
partially ordered set is directed if every pair of elements has one or more
common upper bounds.  Directedness requires neither a unique upper bound nor a
least one; a join-semilattice provides that stronger structure.  Here,
``directed outputs'' is intended \emph{per input}: every nonempty finite
collection of outputs allowed for one input has one or more admitted common
refinements.  It does not require the entire output domain to be directed.

Why do directed outputs matter?  If the outputs allowed for one input are not
finitely directed, some finite subset has no common refinement.  The system
cannot preserve every alternative: proceeding must irrevocably rule at least
one out.  We call that step a \emph{commitment}.  If different participants can make
incompatible commitments, they must \emph{coordinate} first.  A \emph{safe exposure}
is the opposite case: it makes an output public while retaining a refinement of
every allowed alternative.  CALMADO covers both cases---safe exposure when the
alternatives can coexist, and coordinated commitment when they cannot.

This does not mean that earlier work assumed deterministic executions.
Relational-transducer CALM allows message timing and local state to vary, and
CRDTs allow updates and merges to arrive in different orders.  At their usual
semantic interfaces, however, a fixed input is associated with a single result:
one query answer or one replicated state.  In our terms, the outputs allowed for
each input form a singleton set and are therefore directed automatically.

CALMADO also yields a Generalized CAP Theorem.  Classical CAP gives an
impossibility for linearizable registers under partition.  The Generalized CAP Theorem extends this impossibility to both sides of the
boundary for a declared consistency specification, with linearizability as a
special case on the negative side. If every set of choices that a legal placement and
partition can separate has an allowed common refinement, consistency and
availability can coexist through the partition.  Otherwise the system must wait
across the cut and lose availability, or make incompatible commitments and lose
consistency.

This paper makes five contributions:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Complete CALM lifts monotonicity to semantic input/output specifications
        with separate input- and output-refinement partial orders.
  \item CALMADO extends the model to semantic output choice and identifies
        monotonicity plus finite directedness among the outputs allowed for each
        input as the criterion for safe exposure at will.
  \item A residual semantics for safe exposure and commitment explains what
        coordination accomplishes when either input must be sealed or output
        alternatives must be resolved.
  \item The Generalized CAP Theorem extends the classical impossibility to a
        bidirectional dichotomy parameterized by the declared consistency
        specification.
  \item Mappings to query monotonicity, CRDTs, HATs, and invariant confluence
        make results from these traditions directly comparable and clarify the
        guarantees that do---and do not---carry between them.
\end{enumerate}

These are semantic safety results.  Richer formalisms remain essential for
programming, implementation, and liveness arguments.
Section~\ref{sec:complete-calm} develops Complete CALM.
Section~\ref{sec:calmado} introduces semantic choice, commitment, and CALMADO,
including the Generalized CAP Theorem.  Section~\ref{sec:instances} maps the
surrounding literature into the common model, followed by discussion and
related work.

\section{Complete CALM: Lifting Monotonicity}
\label{sec:complete-calm}

A semantic specification describes allowed input/output behavior without
committing to a placement, protocol, or message schedule.  We begin with the
case used by classical query semantics and the usual CRDT state interface: each
input has one specified output.

\subsection{Functional specifications with refinement orders}

\begin{definition}[Functional ordered specification]
\label{def:functional-specification}
A \emph{functional ordered specification} is a function
\begin{equation}
  f:(I,\InputOrd)\longrightarrow(O,\OutputOrd),
  \label{eq:functional-specification}
\end{equation}
where $(I,\InputOrd)$ and $(O,\OutputOrd)$ are partially ordered sets.  We call
$\InputOrd$ the \emph{input-refinement order}: $i\InputOrd j$ means that $j$
contains at least the input information in $i$.  We call $\OutputOrd$ the
\emph{output-refinement order}: $o\OutputOrd u$ means that $u$ preserves every
externally visible claim in $o$ and may add more.
\end{definition}

These are two separate partial orders.  The input order compares inputs; the
output order compares outputs.  Neither orders executions or rows in a table.
Both are part of the semantic specification.

\begin{definition}[Monotonicity]
\label{def:functional-monotonicity}
A functional ordered specification $f$ is \emph{monotone} if
\begin{equation}
  i\InputOrd j
  \quad\Longrightarrow\quad
  f(i)\OutputOrd f(j).
  \label{eq:functional-monotonicity}
\end{equation}
\end{definition}

This is the standard mathematical definition of a monotone function.  In
systems terms, an output exposed from partial input remains valid as more input
arrives.

\subsection{Voting and query evaluation}

Consider three named voters, each of whom casts at most one immutable ballot for
$a$ or $b$.  The input is the partial ballot map, ordered by extension.  The
output is $\bot$, $a$, or $b$, with $\bot$ below either certified winner.

The specification is monotone: additional ballots may refine $\bot$ to a
winner, but cannot change one certified winner into the other.

A database query gives the canonical case.  Inputs are finite fact sets $J$
ordered by inclusion; outputs are answer sets $Q(J)$, also ordered by inclusion.
Equation~\ref{eq:functional-monotonicity} becomes
\begin{equation}
  J\subseteq J'
  \quad\Longrightarrow\quad
  Q(J)\subseteq Q(J'),
  \label{eq:query-monotonicity}
\end{equation}
the standard definition of a monotone query
~\cite{abiteboul1995foundations}.  Positive Datalog queries satisfy this
condition.  Negation may not: an answer derived from the absence of a fact can
be invalidated when that fact arrives.

\subsection{The Complete CALM lift}

A distributed implementation assigns partial input and computation to
participants.  We say that a functional specification permits
\emph{incremental exposure without sealing} if a participant may expose $f(i)$
while any input refinement $j\succeq_I i$ remains possible, with every later
output preserving that exposure.  A \emph{seal} is agreement that rules out
further relevant input refinement.

\begin{theorem}[Complete CALM]
\label{thm:complete-calm}
A functional ordered specification permits incremental exposure without
sealing if and only if it is monotone.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
If $f$ is monotone, then for every $i\InputOrd j$,
$f(i)\OutputOrd f(j)$.  Output exposed at $i$ is therefore preserved at every
possible later input $j$, so no seal is required for safety.

If $f$ is not monotone, some $i\InputOrd j$ satisfies
$f(i)\not\OutputOrd f(j)$.  Exposing $f(i)$ while $j$ remains possible risks a
later required output that contradicts the exposure.  A correct implementation
must therefore wait for agreement that rules out $j$ or actively prevent $j$.
Waiting for agreement is coordination; preventing $j$ is an input-closing
commitment.
\end{proof}

This is the familiar CALM dichotomy stated at the semantic interface rather than
inside a particular programming language.  Relational-transducer CALM connects
query monotonicity to coordination-free programs in its operational model
~\cite{ameloot2013relational,ameloot2015weaker}.  Complete CALM lifts the
semantic statement from fact sets under inclusion to arbitrary input and output
refinement partial orders.

The usual state-based CRDT interface has the same functional shape.  Let $U$ be
the immutable joinable delta or state payloads received so far, ordered by
inclusion, and define $s(U)=s_0\sqcup\bigsqcup U$.  The semilattice order is
the output-refinement order.  This function is monotone, while lattice-join provides a
canonical refinement when replicas have seen different partial inputs
~\cite{shapiro2011crdt}.  Section~\ref{sec:crdt-instance} returns to what this
stronger algebra provides.

Historically, CALM stands for \emph{Consistency As Logical Monotonicity}.  At
this semantic level, Complete CALM may also be read as \emph{Consistency As
Lifted Monotonicity}: the coordination-free condition is lifted out of a
particular language or algebra and stated over declared refinement orders.

Complete CALM handles uncertainty about input arrival.  We next allow the
specification itself to offer several outputs for one input.

\section{CALMADO: Semantic Choice and Coordination}
\label{sec:calmado}

A semantic specification may admit more than one output for the same input.
Leader election may allow either eligible leader, and serializability may allow
several transaction orders.  Such specifications are relational rather than
functional.  CALMADO extends Complete CALM to this semantic output choice.

\subsection{Relational specifications and output fibers}

\begin{definition}[Ordered relational specification]
\label{def:relational-specification}
An \emph{ordered relational specification} is
\begin{equation}
  \Spec=(I,\InputOrd,O,\OutputOrd,S),
  \qquad S\subseteq I\times O,
  \label{eq:relational-specification}
\end{equation}
where $(I,\InputOrd)$ and $(O,\OutputOrd)$ are partially ordered sets.  A row
$(i,o)\in S$ says that output $o$ is admissible at input $i$.  The \emph{output
fiber} at $i$ is
\begin{equation}
  \fiber{i}=\{o\in O:(i,o)\in S\}.
  \label{eq:fiber}
\end{equation}
We assume every input under discussion has a nonempty output fiber.  A
functional specification is the special case $\fiber{i}=\{f(i)\}$.
\end{definition}

The two partial orders retain their earlier meanings: $\InputOrd$ compares
inputs, $\OutputOrd$ compares outputs, and neither orders rows of $S$.

The output-refinement order lifts from individual outputs to nonempty sets of
outputs.  For nonempty $A,B\subseteq O$, define
\begin{equation}
  A\LiftedOutputOrd B
  \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
  \forall a\in A\;\exists b\in B:\;a\OutputOrd b.
  \label{eq:lifted-output-order}
\end{equation}
This lifted relation is a preorder: every output in the first set can continue
to some output in the second.  It is often called the Hoare preorder.

\begin{definition}[Relational monotonicity]
\label{def:relational-monotonicity}
An ordered relational specification is \emph{monotone} when the mapping
$i\mapsto\fiber{i}$ is monotone under the lifted output preorder:
\begin{equation}
  i\InputOrd j
  \quad\Longrightarrow\quad
  \fiber{i}\LiftedOutputOrd\fiber{j}.
  \label{eq:relational-monotonicity}
\end{equation}
Equivalently, whenever $i\InputOrd j$ and $o\in\fiber{i}$, some
$o'\in\fiber{j}$ satisfies $o\OutputOrd o'$.
\end{definition}

For singleton fibers this reduces to functional monotonicity,
Equation~\ref{eq:functional-monotonicity}.  Relational monotonicity governs how
outputs continue \emph{between} fibers as input grows.  It says nothing about
how different outputs relate \emph{within} one fiber.  We now turn to that
second question.

\begin{definition}[Compatibility]
\label{def:compatibility}
Outputs $o_1,\ldots,o_k$ are \emph{compatible at input $i$} if there is an
admissible common refinement $u\in\fiber{i}$ such that
$o_r\OutputOrd u$ for every $r$.  Otherwise they are \emph{incompatible at
$i$}.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Finite fiber-directedness]
\label{def:fiber-directedness}
An ordered relational specification has \emph{Directed Outputs} when every
output fiber is finitely directed: every nonempty finite collection in
$\fiber{i}$ is compatible at $i$.
\end{definition}

Thus compatibility is the property of one finite collection of outputs;
Directed Outputs requires compatibility for every finite collection in every
fiber.  Compatibility is not comparability.  Figure~\ref{fig:orders} shows an
output chain, two incomparable but compatible outputs, and two incompatible
outputs.  Only the last pair lacks a common refinement.

Rows 3 and 6 are ordered because row 6 adds a continuation of an existing

\subsection{Nondirected ambiguous specifications}

The traceroute specification admits every finite combination of its compatible
observations.  Other specifications admit alternatives that cannot be jointly
refined.  Leader election is a simple example.  If \texttt{server1} and
\texttt{server2} are eligible and the interface permits either as the sole
leader, no allowed output refines both choices.  The output fiber is not
directed.

A serial commit log gives a database example.  Suppose concurrent transactions
$T_1$ and $T_2$ both commit.  For the same completed input history, the log may
admit either $[T_1,T_2]$ or $[T_2,T_1]$.  Prefix refinement cannot preserve both
orders, so this fiber is also not directed.  Section~\ref{sec:hat-instance}
gives a stronger witness in which exposed reads force opposite serialization
orders.

\subsection{The CALMADO criterion}

The name CALMADO---\emph{Consistency As Lifted Monotonicity And Directed
Outputs}---states its two requirements directly.

\begin{definition}[CALMADO criterion]
\label{def:calmado-criterion}
An ordered relational specification satisfies the \emph{CALMADO criterion}
when it has both of the following properties:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
  \item \emph{Lifted Monotonicity:} the mapping $i\mapsto\fiber{i}$ is
        monotone under $\LiftedOutputOrd$, as in
        Equation~\ref{eq:relational-monotonicity}; and
  \item \emph{Directed Outputs:} every output fiber is finitely directed under
        $\OutputOrd$, as in Definition~\ref{def:fiber-directedness}.
\end{enumerate}
\end{definition}

The first clause concerns refinement across inputs; the second concerns
outputs admitted at one fixed input.  Neither implies the other.  This
two-clause form isolates the distinct obligations, but an execution may already
have exposed several outputs at different earlier inputs.  To continue without
invalidating any of them, one future output must refine the whole finite family.
The following equivalent form packages exactly that combined obligation and is
the form used in the exposure analysis below.

\begin{proposition}[Finite-family form]
\label{prop:calmado-decomposition}
The CALMADO criterion is equivalent to the following condition: for every
nonempty finite family of rows $(i_1,o_1),\ldots,(i_k,o_k)\in S$ and every
common input refinement $j$ with $i_r\InputOrd j$ for all $r$, there is a
$u\in\fiber{j}$ such that
\begin{equation}
  o_r\OutputOrd u
  \qquad\text{for every }r\in\{1,\ldots,k\}.
  \label{eq:calmado-criterion}
\end{equation}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Assume the CALMADO criterion.  Lifted Monotonicity gives, for each row,
$o'_r\in\fiber{j}$ with $o_r\OutputOrd o'_r$.  Directed Outputs gives
$u\in\fiber{j}$ refining every $o'_r$, and hence every $o_r$.

Conversely, assume the finite-family condition.  To prove Lifted Monotonicity,
fix arbitrary $i\InputOrd j$ and $o\in\fiber{i}$.  Apply the condition to the
singleton family $(i,o)$ and its common input refinement $j$.  It yields
$u\in\fiber{j}$ with $o\OutputOrd u$, exactly
$\fiber{i}\LiftedOutputOrd\fiber{j}$.

To prove Directed Outputs, fix arbitrary $i$ and a nonempty finite collection
$o_1,\ldots,o_k\in\fiber{i}$.  Apply the condition to
$(i,o_1),\ldots,(i,o_k)$ with common input refinement $j=i$.  It yields
$u\in\fiber{i}$ refining every $o_r$.
\end{proof}

The condition ranges over every common input refinement because the environment
may determine which refinement occurs.  Finite families are required because
pairwise compatibility need not imply compatibility of a larger family.

\subsection{Residuals, retirement, and commitment}

The specification $S$ lists every row allowed initially.  During an execution,
some rows no longer need to remain explicit.  We write $R\subseteq S$ for the
\emph{residual relation}: the rows still admissible at the current point.

\begin{definition}[Retirement]
\label{def:retirement}
A \emph{retirement} is the deletion of a superseded input or output justified
by refinement, so that the larger value semantically preserves all information
in the deleted value:
\begin{itemize}
  \item when the current input advances from $i$ to $j$ with $i\InputOrd j$,
        rows for the superseded input $i$ may be retired; and
  \item when, for some input $i$, an output advances from $x\in R(i)$ to a
        retained $u\in R(i)$ with $x\OutputOrd u$, the row $(i,x)$ may be
        retired.
\end{itemize}
In either case, deletion loses no information and rules out no semantic
possibility.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Commitment]
\label{def:commitment}
Let $R'\subseteq R$ be a new residual.  The deletion of an output row
$(i,x)\in R\setminus R'$ is a \emph{commitment} if it is not an input retirement
and no retained output at that input refines it:
\begin{equation}
  \nexists u\in R'(i): x\OutputOrd u.
  \label{eq:commitment}
\end{equation}
Thus a commitment rules out the alternative represented by $x$ instead of
preserving it in a larger retained value.
\end{definition}

\subsection{Safe exposure}

Consider making $o\in R(i)$ public.  At every input $j\succeq_I i$, later
output must refine $o$.  Remove outputs that cannot meet this obligation and
write the candidate residual as $R\mathbin{\upharpoonright}(i,o)$.  At inputs
$j\succeq_I i$,
\begin{equation}
  \bigl(R\mathbin{\upharpoonright}(i,o)\bigr)(j)
  =\{u\in R(j):o\OutputOrd u\}
  \qquad (j\succeq_I i),
  \label{eq:residual-refining-fibers}
\end{equation}
All other inputs remain unchanged:
\begin{equation}
  \bigl(R\mathbin{\upharpoonright}(i,o)\bigr)(j)=R(j)
  \qquad (i\not\InputOrd j).
  \label{eq:residual-other-fibers}
\end{equation}

\begin{definition}[Safe exposure]
\label{def:safe-exposure}
An output $o\in R(i)$ is \emph{safe to expose} when every row removed by the
restriction to $R\mathbin{\upharpoonright}(i,o)$ is a retirement, not a
commitment.  Equivalently,
\begin{equation}
  \forall j\succeq_I i\;\forall x\in R(j)\;\exists u\in R(j):
  \quad x\OutputOrd u\ \land\ o\OutputOrd u.
  \label{eq:safe-exposure}
\end{equation}
Selecting such an output is a \emph{safe exposure}.  If the condition fails,
exposing $o$ is disallowed at the current residual: doing so would silently
remove an alternative with no retained refinement.  The execution must instead
make the required commitment explicitly, thereby reducing $R$ to a new
residual in which exposing $o$ may become safe.
\end{definition}

The witness $u$ in Equation~\ref{eq:safe-exposure} is retained because it
refines $o$, and it justifies retiring $x$ because it also refines $x$.  The
nontrivial requirement is not that $o$ survives its own exposure---it does by
reflexivity---but that every other removed row has a retained refinement and can
therefore be retired.

It is worth following one exposure carefully.  Suppose traceroute first exposes
$o=\{s{\to}\texttt{server1}\}$.  The output
$x=\{s{\to}a\}$ is incomparable with $o$: neither refines the other.  The
restriction therefore removes $(i,x)$.  At first this may look like a choice
against $x$, but the residual retains
$u=\{s{\to}\texttt{server1},s{\to}a,
      s{\to}a{\to}\texttt{server2}\}$.  Because $u$ refines both $o$ and
$x$, all information in $x$ remains represented; deleting $(i,x)$ is retirement,
not commitment.  Leader election behaves differently.  Selecting
\texttt{server1} removes \texttt{server2} with no retained common refinement,
so that deletion is a commitment.

A residual is \emph{reachable by safe exposure} if it is obtained from $S$ by
a finite sequence of safe exposures.

\begin{definition}[Safe exposure at will]
\label{def:exposure-at-will}
A specification permits \emph{safe exposure at will} if every row $(i,o)$ in
every residual reachable by safe exposure is safe to expose.
\end{definition}

\begin{theorem}[CALMADO]
\label{thm:calmado}
An ordered relational specification permits safe exposure at will if and only
if it satisfies the CALMADO criterion.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Assume the CALMADO criterion.  Let $R$ be the residual after safe exposures
$(i_1,o_1),\ldots,(i_m,o_m)$, and choose any remaining $(i,o)\in R$.  To show
that $o$ is safe to expose, fix $(j,x)\in R$ with $i\InputOrd j$.  Apply the
finite-family form to $(i,o)$, $(j,x)$, and every earlier exposure
$(i_h,o_h)$ whose input satisfies $i_h\InputOrd j$.  It gives
$u\in\fiber{j}$ refining $o$, $x$, and every such $o_h$.  The latter
conditions show that $(j,u)$ survived every earlier exposure; $o\OutputOrd u$
shows that it survives exposure of $o$.  The retained row $(j,u)$ refines $x$,
so $x$ can be retired and $o$ is safe to expose.

Conversely, assume safe exposure at will.  For Lifted Monotonicity, fix
$i\InputOrd j$ and $o\in\fiber{i}$.  Since $\fiber{j}$ is nonempty, choose
$x\in\fiber{j}$.  Safe exposure of $o$ in the initial residual gives
$u\in\fiber{j}$ with $o\OutputOrd u$, proving
$\fiber{i}\LiftedOutputOrd\fiber{j}$.

For Directed Outputs, suppose some fiber contains a finite incompatible family
$X=\{x_1,\ldots,x_k\}$ of least cardinality.  If $k=2$, exposing $x_1$ is not
safe because $x_2$ has no common refinement with it.  If $k>2$, minimality gives
$v$ refining $X\setminus\{x_k\}$ and $w$ refining
$X\setminus\{x_{k-1}\}$.  Both $v$ and $w$ refine $x_1$, so both remain after
exposing $x_1$.  They have no common refinement, since one would refine all of
$X$.  Hence $v$ is not safe to expose in the resulting residual, again a
contradiction.  Every fiber is finitely directed, so the CALMADO criterion
holds.
\end{proof}

The theorem identifies when safe exposure at will is possible.  Computing a
common refinement and guaranteeing termination remain protocol questions.

Why did earlier CALM results not state Directed Outputs separately?  Their
semantic query specification is functional: each input $J$ has one result
$Q(J)$, so each output fiber is a singleton and directed automatically.
State-based CRDTs also present a functional state for a fixed set of incorporated
updates; semilattice join additionally provides least common refinements across
partial inputs.  CALMADO makes multiple semantic outputs at one input explicit,
so directedness can no longer be assumed.

\subsection{Coordination, placement, and the Generalized CAP Theorem}
\label{sec:cap-corollary}

\begin{definition}[Coordination]
\label{def:coordination}
\emph{Coordination} is agreement required before a participant makes a
commitment that could exclude an admissible output alternative available to
another participant.
\end{definition}

Safe exposure at will depends only on $S$.  Whether a commitment is necessary
is therefore a semantic question; whether it requires agreement between
participants also depends on \emph{placement}: which participant may select each
row.  A participant that controls an entire choice
may commit locally; participants that may make incompatible commitments must
coordinate.  If a system forbids some rows of $S$, its declared interface is a
proper subrelation $S'\subsetneq S$, and the criterion applies to $S'$.

A seal is an input-side commitment: it rules out further input refinements.
Selecting among incompatible outputs is an output-side commitment: it rules out
an alternative at a fixed input.  The functional setting of Complete CALM has
only the first kind; CALMADO accounts for both.

The classical CAP theorem is a one-way impossibility: a linearizable register
cannot remain available through arbitrary partitions~\cite{gilbert2002cap}.  CALMADO
yields a Generalized CAP Theorem that turns this negative result into a
dichotomy for declared consistency specifications.

A placement $\Delta$ assigns participant $p$ a set $\Delta_p\subseteq S$ of
rows it may select.  A finite family is \emph{separable} by a partition if some
legal execution permits participants in at least two components to select its
rows jointly while cross-partition messages are delayed.  Joint realizability
matters: two mutually exclusive choices owned by one participant do not become
separable merely because another component acts.

At this interface, \emph{consistency} means refinement consistency: outputs
exposed in different components have an admitted common refinement when their
inputs combine.  \emph{Availability} means that each nonfailed component may
make every locally permitted selection without waiting across the cut.
\emph{Partition tolerance} requires both properties for every partition and
legal execution.

\begin{corollary}[Generalized CAP Theorem]
\label{cor:generalized-cap}
Across all legal placements and network partitions, consistency, availability,
and partition tolerance coexist exactly when every family separable by a
partition has an admitted common output refinement at every common input
refinement.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
Fix a legal placement and partition.  If each separable family has a common
refinement, components may make locally permitted selections and their outputs
remain compatible when communication resumes.  If a separable family has no
common refinement, availability permits its selections on different sides of
the cut, but consistency cannot preserve them all.  The system must wait across
the cut, retain incompatible commitments, or suppress an allowed row.  These
lose availability, consistency, or the declared specification, respectively.
\end{proof}

The theorem quantifies over all legal placements and partitions.  Placement
determines which inputs and choices can occur independently, just as data
placement does in the relational-transducer model.

\paragraph{Registers.}
Consider two replicas of a register initially holding no value.  Process $p$
completes a write of \dog{} while $q$ concurrently completes a write of
\catv.  The replicas exchange both writes, after which a partition separates
them.  Each then serves a local read; both writes complete before either read
begins.

An input is a finite causally closed operation history, ordered by
induced-subhistory extension.  An output is an explanation $(L,\rho)$, where
$L$ is an operation order admitted by the consistency model and $\rho$ maps each
completed read to the write supplying its response.  Refinement extends $L$
without reversing an existing order and extends $\rho$ without changing an
answered read.

For linearizability, $L$ is a total order extending real time
~\cite{herlihy1990linearizability}.  If $p$ returns \catv, its explanation
requires $w_p(x,\dog)<_L w_q(x,\catv)$.  If $q$ returns \dog, its explanation
requires the opposite order.  No total order refines both explanations.  The
partition separates the read owners, recovering the classical CAP impossibility
as the negative side of the Generalized CAP theorem
~\cite{gilbert2002cap}.

Under causal consistency, the writes remain concurrent and one explanation can
assign $p$'s read to \catv{} and $q$'s read to \dog{}
~\cite{lamport1978time,ahamad1995causal}.  The responses have a common
refinement.  Two possible answers for the same read remain incompatible, but
only that read's owner selects one; no legal placement and partition separate
one local decision into two independent choices.  With the central results in
place, we now compare their consequences across the surrounding literature.

\subsection{Query monotonicity}
\label{sec:query-instance}

Section~\ref{sec:complete-calm} gave the functional encoding
$J\mapsto Q(J)$.  Relational-transducer CALM proves the operational result for
that language: monotone queries admit coordination-free evaluation under its
data-placement and execution assumptions
~\cite{ameloot2013relational,ameloot2015weaker}.  Complete CALM does not replace
that theorem.  It isolates the semantic order-preservation property so that it
can be compared with interfaces outside Datalog.

\subsection{CRDTs: stronger structure than the criterion}
\label{sec:crdt-instance}

For a state-based CRDT, let $U$ be the immutable joinable delta or state
payloads received so far, ordered by inclusion, and define
$s(U)=s_0\sqcup\bigsqcup U$.  Under the usual state-based CRDT conditions,
local states move upward and merge is join~\cite{shapiro2011crdt}.  Hence
\begin{equation}
  s(U_1)\sqcup s(U_2)=s(U_1\cup U_2)
  \label{eq:crdt-join}
\end{equation}
is a least common refinement.  Treating $U$ as immutable joinable payloads is
important: arbitrary state-dependent update operations need not commute or be
idempotent.

The semilattice supplies more than CALMADO requires.  It gives a uniform least
common refinement throughout the output domain and a fixed operator for
computing it.  CALMADO asks only for some admitted common refinement for each
relevant finite family.  The refinement need not be least or produced by one
global binary operator.

A sharded log demonstrates the gap.  Let shard $A$ expose $[a_1,a_2]$ and shard
$B$ expose $[b_1,b_2]$.  Sequence refinement is order-preserving subsequence.
At the combined input, both
\begin{equation}
  [a_1,a_2,b_1,b_2]
  \qquad\text{and}\qquad
  [b_1,b_2,a_1,a_2]
  \label{eq:log-interleavings}
\end{equation}
are minimal common refinements, and neither refines the other.  There is no
least interleaving, yet either is an admitted reconciliation of the shard-local
sequences.  A global join is unnecessary for this interface.

The interface boundary matters.  A deterministic global log would need an
additional rule to select one interleaving.  Exact reads and transactional
observations likewise require their own CALMADO tests; state convergence alone
does not choose their semantics
~\cite{burckhardt2014principles,laddad2022keep}.

\subsection{Highly available transactions}
\label{sec:hat-instance}

For a transactional interface, an input is the set of begin, read, write, and
commit requests received so far, ordered by inclusion.  An output is a growing
set of public facts such as $\mathsf{read}(T,x,v)$,
$\mathsf{commit}(T)$, and session-order facts, also ordered by inclusion.  The
isolation or session guarantee determines which fact sets are admissible.
Placement assigns transactions and requests to replicas.  The Generalized
CAP Theorem tests the combinations those replicas can select across a partition,
under the contact, failure, and session assumptions of the interface
~\cite{bailis2013hat,terry1994session}.

\paragraph{Accumulating facts.}
Suppose two replicas execute transactions on disjoint keys.  One exposes
$\mathsf{read}(T_1,x,0)$ and $\mathsf{commit}(T_1)$; the other exposes
$\mathsf{read}(T_2,y,0)$ and $\mathsf{commit}(T_2)$.  If each read observes a
committed version at its replica, the union contains no dirty read or write
conflict.  Under the stated read-committed interface, later delivery preserves
all four facts.  This is the positive accumulation pattern behind HAT results;
it is not a claim that every combination of session guarantees is available.

\paragraph{Serializability and transaction ordering.}
The ordering issue becomes a distributed obstruction when exposed reads force
opposite serialization orders.  Initially $x=y=0$.  On one side, $T_1$ reads
$x=0$, writes $y=1$, and commits.
On the other, $T_2$ reads $y=0$, writes $x=1$, and commits.  Preserving $T_1$'s
read requires $T_1$ before $T_2$ in any serial explanation; preserving $T_2$'s
read requires $T_2$ before $T_1$.  The serialization graph contains a cycle, so
no serial history refines both sets of facts~\cite{adya2000generalized}.

\paragraph{Snapshot isolation.}
Let overlapping transactions $T_1$ and $T_2$ read the same snapshot and both
write $x$.  Separate replicas could each expose its local commit.  Snapshot
isolation's first-committer-wins rule admits no output containing both commit
facts~\cite{berenson1995critique,bailis2013hat}.  Eventual storage convergence
may choose a final state; it cannot retract a commit already exposed to a
client.

These examples answer the question posed in the introduction.  Convergent
storage does not automatically provide a highly available transactional
interface.  Storage convergence constrains states after update delivery;
isolation constrains reads and commits exposed before then.

\subsection{Invariant confluence}
\label{sec:iconfluence-instance}

Let the input be a set of applied transactions, ordered by inclusion.  We use
the standard invariant-confluence assumptions: each transaction remains
applicable where the specification admits it and maps an invariant-valid state
to another invariant-valid state; independently reached states share the
required ancestor; the prescribed merge is associative and preserves all
included effects; and merged states remain reachable.  Outputs are reachable,
invariant-valid states.  We order outputs by merge refinement: $s\OutputOrd s'$
when $s'$ is an admitted merge that preserves every effect in $s$ (quotienting
states with identical effects if necessary to obtain a partial order).

Invariant confluence asks whether independently reachable valid states merge to
a valid state~\cite{bailis2014coordination}.  Under the assumptions above,
individual transaction preservation supplies relational monotonicity, while
invariant confluence supplies common refinements for independently reached
states at the combined transaction input.  Associativity and reachability let
the pairwise merge argument extend inductively to finite families.  Thus the
prescribed merge witnesses the CALMADO criterion for the deployed transaction
families.

A uniqueness constraint supplies the standard counterexample.  Two replicas
each observe that username \texttt{sam} is free and allocate it to different
accounts.  Each local state satisfies uniqueness, but no invariant-valid common
refinement preserves both allocations.  The system must reserve names, reject
one allocation, or weaken the invariant.

State convergence, transaction availability, and invariant preservation test
three different specifications.  A convergent merge may produce an invalid
state; an available transaction may expose facts that later violate an
invariant; an invariant-confluent application need not provide a particular
isolation level.  The shared model makes these distinctions explicit rather
than collapsing the properties.  We close by drawing out the resulting design
lessons and scope limits.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Input arrival versus output choice}

Complete CALM and CALMADO separate two reasons a commitment may be necessary.
A failure of monotonicity means that more input can invalidate an exposed output.  The system
must close the input---a seal---before that output is safe.  A failure of finite
fiber-directedness means that outputs admitted at one input cannot all be
preserved; proceeding requires a commitment.  Whether either commitment
requires agreement among participants depends on placement.

The distinction concerns the semantic interface, not whether executions are
deterministic.  CALM and CRDTs already account for operational nondeterminism in message delivery, batching, retries, and
merge order.  Their usual declared outputs are fixed for a given input.  CALMADO
adds specifications whose semantic interface itself offers several outputs and
models the commitments that resolve them.

\subsection{A semantic criterion, not an implementation discipline}

The input/output relation and its two refinement orders omit internal
computation.  This is useful for stating a common boundary and insufficient for
building a system.  Relational transducers provide a language and operational
network semantics; semilattices provide a local algebra and deterministic
merge; transaction protocols provide scheduling, validation, and recovery.
Neither Complete CALM nor CALMADO says how to represent $S$, choose the
refinement orders, compute a common refinement, or do so efficiently.  Those
questions belong to the programming language, protocol, and verification
method used to realize the specification.

\subsection{Specification design determines the boundary}

The CALMADO criterion is a property of the declared specification, not a
permanent label on ``register,'' ``transaction,'' or ``shopping cart.''
Placement determines whether a commitment requires agreement between
participants.
A linearizable register and a causal register admit different explanations.  A
CRDT state and an exact read expose different promises.  A database permitted
to abort one transaction implements a different relation from one required to
commit both.

Narrowing $S$ may eliminate an incompatibility.  Broadening $O$ with a new
output that refines two alternatives may also eliminate it.  Changing placement
may keep an incompatibility under one participant's authority.  Each option
changes the interface or system design; none is a loophole in the criterion.

\subsection{CALMADO is not convergence}

CALMADO requires admitted common refinements, not unique or least ones.  The
sharded log has several minimal interleavings.  Strong eventual consistency adds
a stronger promise: replicas that receive the same input reach equivalent
state.  A semilattice join provides that promise.  Conversely,
last-writer-wins may converge by discarding one concurrent value without
preserving an interface that promised both values.  Convergence and semantic
preservation are distinct requirements.

\subsection{Safety is not liveness}

The theorems identify when exposed outputs remain refinement-consistent; they
do not guarantee message delivery, termination, or computability of a common
refinement.  The availability premise of the Generalized CAP Theorem excludes an
implementation that never responds, but a full protocol result must still
state appropriate fairness, failure, and cost assumptions.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

The CALM conjecture connected logical monotonicity with coordination freedom
~\cite{hellerstein2010declarative,hellerstein2020keeping}.  Ameloot et al.
established the theorem using relational transducers and studied how data
placement and system knowledge change coordination requirements
~\cite{ameloot2013relational,ameloot2015weaker}.  Complete CALM lifts the
semantic order-preservation result; CALMADO adds semantic output choice and the
effect of coordination on that choice.

Brewer's conjecture and the Gilbert--Lynch theorem focus attention on
availability during partitions~\cite{brewer2000towards,gilbert2002cap}.
The Generalized CAP Theorem applies CALMADO to choices that legal placements
and partitions can separate.  It extends the classical impossibility to both
sides of the specification-level dichotomy: the total-order register recovers
the negative result, while the common-refinement condition supplies the
positive result.  Local choices controlled by one participant do not require
cross-partition agreement.

CRDTs derive strong eventual consistency from carefully designed state or
operation algebras~\cite{shapiro2011crdt}.  The distinction here is between a
uniform least merge and the weaker existence of some admitted common
refinement.  Work on consistency semantics likewise distinguishes visibility,
ordering, availability, and convergence
~\cite{burckhardt2014principles,mahajan2011consistency}.

HATs classify transactional guarantees realizable without cross-partition
coordination under stated client and failure assumptions~\cite{bailis2013hat}.
Invariant confluence characterizes when independently valid transactional
states preserve an application invariant on merge
~\cite{bailis2014coordination}.  CALMADO exposes their common-refinement structure
while preserving their different output domains.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

A semantic specification abstracts distribution away.  Complete CALM asks when
a functional specification can be implemented over distributed input arrival
without coordination: monotonicity means no seal is needed.  This lifts the
classic result from logical fact sets to arbitrary input and output refinement
orders.

CALMADO extends the model to specifications that offer several outputs for one
input.  Modeling safe exposure and commitment reveals finite
fiber-directedness as the additional requirement for safe exposure at will.
When directedness fails, some admissible alternative must be removed by a
commitment.  If different participants can make incompatible commitments,
coordination determines which alternative survives.

The Generalized CAP Theorem is the partition corollary.  It recovers the
classical impossibility when a cut separates incompatible choices and supplies
the positive side when all separated choices have common refinements.  A cut
may prevent agreement on input closure or separate participants empowered to
make incompatible output choices.  Query monotonicity, CRDT merge,
transactional availability, and invariant confluence then become directly comparable without being identified
with one another.
